# Galois Theory

## What this area is about

Galois theory attaches a finite group to a field extension, the group of symmetries of the roots of a polynomial that respect every algebraic relation among them, and shows that the group knows everything about the fields in between. Subgroups correspond to intermediate fields, normal subgroups to normal extensions, and indices to degrees. A question about infinitely many numbers becomes a question about a finite group, which can be settled by counting.

Galois created the theory around 1830, before the word "group" had its modern meaning, to answer a question three centuries old: which polynomial equations can be solved by a formula built from roots? The answer, that the group must be solvable, explains why the quintic has no general formula. The same machinery, restricted to square roots, settles the Greek construction problems that had stood for two millennia. Beyond these classical results, the theory is the first and clearest example of a correspondence between two kinds of mathematical structure, a pattern that recurs in topology, number theory and logic.

## How the children fit together

| Child | Its role in the whole | Depends on |
|---|---|---|
| [The Galois Correspondence](the-galois-correspondence.md) | The fundamental theorem: subgroups and intermediate fields match, reversing inclusion | Field extensions, group actions |
| [Solvability by Radicals](solvability-by-radicals.md) | Radical formulas exist exactly when the Galois group is solvable; x⁵ − 6x + 3 has group S₅ | The correspondence, simple groups |
| [Ruler-and-Compass Constructions](ruler-and-compass-constructions.md) | Constructible numbers live in quadratic towers; the cube, the angle, the circle and the polygons | The correspondence, the tower law |

The correspondence is the engine; the other two leaves are its two classical applications, and they run in parallel. Each translates a question about allowed operations into a property of a group. Radicals of any order correspond to cyclic layers, so the group must be solvable. Square roots alone correspond to layers of order 2, so the group must have order a power of 2. Comparing the two shows how a change in the permitted operations becomes a change in the permitted groups.

```
               the Galois correspondence
                 /                    \
                v                      v
   solvability by radicals    ruler-and-compass constructions
   (solvable groups)          (2-groups)
```

The leaves can be read with only the first two areas of this layer behind them; the solvability leaf also needs composition series from groups.
