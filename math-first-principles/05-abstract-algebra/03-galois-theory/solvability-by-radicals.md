## Essence

A polynomial is solvable by radicals when its roots can be written from its coefficients using only addition, subtraction, multiplication, division and n-th roots. Quadratics, cubics and quartics always are; the formulas for degrees 3 and 4 were published by Cardano in 1545. For the quintic, Ruffini (1799) gave a flawed proof and Abel (1824) a complete one that no general formula exists. Galois explained why: a polynomial over ℚ is solvable by radicals exactly when its Galois group is a solvable group, one built from abelian pieces. The symmetric group S₅ is not solvable, and the explicit quintic x⁵ − 6x + 3 has S₅ as its Galois group, so its roots cannot be expressed by radicals at all.

## Core tenets

1. **A radical expression is a tower of fields, each step adjoining a root.** The roots of f are expressible by radicals over ℚ when they lie in a field ℚ = K₀ ⊆ K₁ ⊆ ⋯ ⊆ Kₘ with each Kᵢ₊₁ = Kᵢ(αᵢ) and αᵢⁿ ∈ Kᵢ for some n. Cardano's formula for x³ + px + q is such a tower: a square root, then a cube root.
   ```
   x = ∛(−q/2 + √(q²/4 + p³/27)) + ∛(−q/2 − √(q²/4 + p³/27))
   ```

2. **Adjoining a root, with roots of unity present, gives a cyclic Galois group.** Suppose K contains the n-th roots of unity and αⁿ = a ∈ K. Every automorphism σ of K(α) over K sends α to another root of xⁿ − a, so σ(α)/α is an n-th root of unity, and σ ↦ σ(α)/α is an injective homomorphism into the cyclic group of n-th roots of unity. Adjoining the roots of unity themselves gives an abelian group, a subgroup of (ℤ/n)ˣ.

3. **Galois's theorem: in characteristic 0, f is solvable by radicals if and only if Gal(f) is solvable.** A group is solvable when it has a chain of subgroups, each normal in the next, with abelian quotients. Forward: enlarge the radical tower to a Galois one; by tenet 2 and the Galois correspondence its group has abelian layers, and Gal(f) is a quotient of it, hence solvable. Backward: adjoin roots of unity, then each cyclic layer of order n is generated by a single n-th root, found by a Lagrange resolvent α + ζσ(α) + ζ²σ²(α) + ⋯, whose n-th power is fixed by σ.

4. **Every polynomial of degree at most 4 is solvable by radicals.** Its Galois group is a subgroup of S₄, and S₄ is solvable through the chain S₄ ⊃ A₄ ⊃ V₄ ⊃ ℤ/2 ⊃ 1, where V₄ is the Klein four-group. The quotient S₄/V₄ ≅ S₃ is why Ferrari's method for the quartic passes through a resolvent cubic, whose roots are expressions in the four roots fixed by V₄.

5. **S₅ is not solvable, so there is no general quintic formula.** Its only normal subgroups are 1, A₅ and S₅, and A₅ is simple and non-abelian, so no chain with abelian quotients reaches 1. Any formula in the coefficients of the general quintic would make S₅ solvable. The same holds for Sₙ with n ≥ 5.

6. **x⁵ − 6x + 3 has Galois group S₅.** It is irreducible by Eisenstein's criterion at p = 3. Its derivative 5x⁴ − 6 has two real zeros, so f has at most three real roots, and the sign changes below give exactly three.
   ```
   x     −2    −1    0    1    2
   f(x)  −17    8    3   −2   23
   ```
   So two roots are non-real conjugates. Since 5 divides the degree of the splitting field, Cauchy's theorem gives an element of order 5 in the Galois group, a 5-cycle on the roots. Complex conjugation fixes the three real roots and swaps the other two, a transposition. In S₅ a 5-cycle and a transposition generate the whole group, so Gal(f) = S₅, and the real root near 0.6 is not expressible by radicals.

7. **Particular quintics can be solvable.** The roots of x⁵ − 2 are ζ·⁵√2 for the fifth roots of unity ζ; its Galois group has order 20 and is solvable. The theorem classifies polynomials one by one; it does not forbid every quintic a formula.

8. **Unsolvable does not mean unknowable.** Newton's method computes the roots of x⁵ − 6x + 3 to any precision, and Hermite (1858) solved the general quintic exactly using elliptic modular functions. The theorem concerns one specific vocabulary of operations, not whether roots exist or can be found.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Solvable by radicals | Roots expressible with field operations and n-th roots |
| Radical extension | A tower in which each step adjoins an n-th root of an element below |
| Solvable group | One with a subnormal chain whose quotients are abelian |
| Lagrange resolvent | α + ζσ(α) + ζ²σ²(α) + ⋯, an n-th root that generates a cyclic layer |
| Resolvent cubic | The cubic used to reduce a quartic, reflecting S₄/V₄ ≅ S₃ |
| Abel–Ruffini theorem | The general polynomial of degree 5 or more has no radical formula |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Abel proved that quintics cannot be solved."** He proved there is no formula in radicals for the general quintic. Particular quintics may be solvable, and all have roots that can be computed.
- **"Real roots have real radical expressions."** An irreducible cubic with three real roots, such as x³ − 3x − 1, needs square roots of negative numbers in any radical formula: the casus irreducibilis that pushed Renaissance algebraists towards complex numbers.
- **"Galois's theorem is about degree."** It is about the group: degree 5 allows groups of order 5, 10 and 20, which are solvable, as well as A₅ and S₅, which are not.
