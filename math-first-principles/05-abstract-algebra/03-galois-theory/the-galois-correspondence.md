## Essence

The Galois group of a field extension L/K is the group of automorphisms of L that fix every element of K; for a splitting field it permutes the roots of the polynomial while preserving every algebraic relation among them. The fundamental theorem says that for a finite Galois extension, one that is normal and separable, intermediate fields and subgroups of the Galois group correspond exactly, with inclusions reversed and degrees equal to indices. Questions about fields, which are infinite, become questions about a finite group, which can be computed. Galois found this in about 1830 to decide which equations are solvable by radicals; it is now the prototype of a "dictionary" between two kinds of structure.

## Core tenets

1. **Gal(L/K) is the group of field automorphisms of L fixing K pointwise, and it permutes roots.** If σ fixes K and f has coefficients in K, then f(σ(α)) = σ(f(α)) = 0, so σ sends roots of f to roots of f. A splitting field is generated by the roots, so σ is determined by how it permutes them, and Gal(L/K) is a subgroup of Sₙ for a polynomial of degree n. Complex conjugation is the non-trivial element of Gal(ℂ/ℝ).

2. **For finite L/K, |Gal(L/K)| ≤ [L : K], with equality if and only if L/K is Galois.** Each automorphism must send a generator α to a root of its minimal polynomial, and that root determines it, which bounds the count. Equality needs enough distinct roots inside L: normality puts all roots of each minimal polynomial in L, and separability makes them distinct. ℚ(∛2)/ℚ is not normal, and its only automorphism is the identity, since the other cube roots of 2 are not real.

3. **The fundamental theorem.** Let L/K be finite Galois with group G.
   ```
   subgroup H ≤ G        ⟷   intermediate field K ⊆ M ⊆ L
   H ↦ L^H = {x ∈ L : σ(x) = x for all σ ∈ H}
   M ↦ Gal(L/M)

   the two maps are inverse bijections that reverse inclusion
   [L : M] = |Gal(L/M)|     [M : K] = [G : Gal(L/M)]
   M/K is Galois  if and only if  Gal(L/M) is normal in G,
   and then Gal(M/K) ≅ G/Gal(L/M)
   ```
   The key step is Artin's lemma: if a finite group H acts on L, then [L : L^H] = |H|. Its proof is linear algebra: any |H| + 1 elements of L satisfy a non-trivial linear relation over L^H, found by solving a system of equations. Normal subgroups match normal extensions because conjugating H corresponds to applying σ to the fixed field L^H.

4. **Example: x³ − 2 over ℚ has Galois group S₃.** The splitting field ℚ(∛2, ω) has degree 6, and S₃ permutes the three roots. Its three subgroups of order 2 fix the three fields ℚ(∛2), ℚ(ω∛2) and ℚ(ω²∛2), none of which is normal, matching the three non-normal subgroups. The normal subgroup A₃ of order 3 fixes ℚ(ω), the splitting field of x² + x + 1, which is Galois over ℚ with group S₃/A₃ ≅ ℤ/2.

5. **Example: ℚ(√2, √3) has group ℤ/2 × ℤ/2 and three quadratic subfields.**
   ```
                 ℚ(√2, √3)
               /     |     \
          ℚ(√2)   ℚ(√6)   ℚ(√3)
               \     |     /
                     ℚ
   ```
   Each automorphism independently fixes or negates √2 and √3. The three subgroups of order 2 fix exactly the three middle fields, and the picture is the subgroup lattice turned upside down. No other intermediate fields exist, which would be hard to prove without the theorem.

6. **Galois groups of familiar extensions are computable.** Gal(𝔽_(p^n)/𝔽ₚ) is cyclic of order n, generated by Frobenius. Adjoining a primitive n-th root of unity ζₙ to ℚ gives Gal ≅ (ℤ/n)ˣ, abelian, with a ↦ (ζₙ ↦ ζₙᵃ). The Kronecker–Weber theorem says that every finite abelian extension of ℚ lies inside some ℚ(ζₙ).

7. **Galois theory proves the fundamental theorem of algebra with little analysis.** Use only that real polynomials of odd degree have a real root (the intermediate value theorem) and that complex numbers have square roots. Take a Galois extension L of ℝ containing ℂ, and a Sylow 2-subgroup P of its group. The fixed field of P has odd degree over ℝ, so it is ℝ, and the group is a 2-group. If L ≠ ℂ, then Gal(L/ℂ) would have a subgroup of index 2, giving a quadratic extension of ℂ, which cannot exist. So L = ℂ.

8. **Beyond finite extensions, and open problems.** Infinite Galois extensions need a topology on the group (the Krull topology), and the absolute Galois group of ℚ, the group of all automorphisms of ℚ̄, is a central object of number theory. The inverse Galois problem, whether every finite group is a Galois group over ℚ, is open as of 2026. It is known for many families, including all symmetric and alternating groups and every solvable group, the latter by Shafarevich.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Gal(L/K) | Automorphisms of L fixing every element of K |
| Galois extension | A finite extension that is normal and separable |
| Fixed field L^H | The elements of L fixed by every σ in H |
| Galois correspondence | The inclusion-reversing bijection between subgroups and intermediate fields |
| Artin's lemma | [L : L^H] = ∣H∣ for a finite group H of automorphisms |
| Cyclotomic field ℚ(ζₙ) | ℚ with a primitive n-th root of unity adjoined |
| Absolute Galois group | Gal(ℚ̄/ℚ), the symmetries of all algebraic numbers |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Every finite extension has a Galois group of size equal to its degree."** Only Galois extensions do: ℚ(∛2) has degree 3 and one automorphism.
- **"The Galois group is the full symmetric group on the roots."** It is often smaller: x⁴ − 1 has group ℤ/2 over ℚ, since the roots ±1 are rational.
- **"Galois theory is about solving equations."** That was its origin; its lasting content is the correspondence, which reappears in topology and number theory.
