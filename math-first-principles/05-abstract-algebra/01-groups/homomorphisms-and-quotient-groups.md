## Essence

A homomorphism is a map between groups that respects the operation; it is how groups are compared. Its kernel, the set of elements sent to the identity, records exactly what the map forgets. The subgroups that can be kernels are the normal ones, those unchanged by conjugation, and for them the cosets themselves multiply to form a quotient group. The first isomorphism theorem says that every homomorphism is a quotient followed by an inclusion, so images and quotients are one idea seen from two sides. This is the template for every "structure modulo a substructure" construction in algebra.

## Core tenets

1. **A homomorphism φ: G → H satisfies φ(ab) = φ(a)φ(b), and then preserves the identity and inverses.** From φ(e) = φ(e)φ(e), cancellation gives φ(e) = e; from φ(a)φ(a⁻¹) = φ(e) we get φ(a⁻¹) = φ(a)⁻¹. An isomorphism is a bijective homomorphism: the same group with its elements renamed. Examples: det: GLₙ(ℝ) → ℝˣ, the non-zero reals under multiplication, since det(AB) = det A · det B; the sign of a permutation, sgn: Sₙ → {±1}; reduction ℤ → ℤ/n.

2. **The kernel ker φ = {g : φ(g) = e} is a normal subgroup, and φ is injective if and only if ker φ = {e}.** A subgroup N is normal, written N ◁ G, if gNg⁻¹ = N for every g ∈ G. If φ(n) = e, then φ(gng⁻¹) = φ(g)φ(g)⁻¹ = e, so kernels are normal. Since φ(a) = φ(b) if and only if a⁻¹b ∈ ker φ, the fibres of φ are exactly the cosets of its kernel.

3. **Normal subgroups are exactly the subgroups whose cosets can be multiplied.** Try (aN)(bN) = abN. If a′ = an and b′ = bm with n, m ∈ N, then a′b′ = ab(b⁻¹nb)m, which lies in abN for all choices precisely when b⁻¹nb ∈ N. So for normal N the quotient G/N is a group with identity N, and the projection g ↦ gN is a homomorphism with kernel N: every normal subgroup is a kernel. Without normality it fails: H = {e, (12)} in S₃ is not normal, since (13)(12)(13) = (23) ∉ H, and the product of two cosets of H depends on the representatives chosen.

4. **First isomorphism theorem: for a homomorphism φ: G → H, the rule g·ker φ ↦ φ(g) is an isomorphism G/ker φ ≅ im φ.** It is well defined and injective because φ is constant exactly on the cosets of its kernel, and it is a homomorphism because φ is. Hence GLₙ(ℝ)/SLₙ(ℝ) ≅ ℝˣ; Sₙ/Aₙ ≅ {±1} for n ≥ 2, so the alternating group Aₙ has index 2; ℤ/n is the quotient of ℤ by nℤ; and t ↦ e^(2πit) shows that ℝ/ℤ is isomorphic to the circle group of complex numbers of modulus 1.

5. **The other isomorphism theorems organise quotients.** For H ≤ G and N ◁ G, HN is a subgroup and HN/N ≅ H/(H ∩ N). For normal subgroups N ≤ K of G, (G/N)/(K/N) ≅ G/K. By the correspondence theorem, subgroups of G/N match subgroups of G that contain N, with normal matching normal. Each is the first theorem applied to a natural map.

6. **Normality is common but not transitive.** Every subgroup of an abelian group is normal, and so is every subgroup of index 2, since both its left and its right cosets are "H and the rest". But N ◁ K ◁ G does not give N ◁ G. In A₄ the Klein four-group V₄ = {e, (12)(34), (13)(24), (14)(23)} is normal, and ⟨(12)(34)⟩ is normal in the abelian V₄, yet (123)(12)(34)(123)⁻¹ = (14)(23), so ⟨(12)(34)⟩ is not normal in A₄.

7. **A normal subgroup and its quotient do not determine the group.** ℤ/4 and ℤ/2 × ℤ/2 each contain a copy of ℤ/2 with quotient ℤ/2, yet only the first is cyclic. Rebuilding G from N and G/N is the extension problem. The simplest solutions are the direct product N × Q and the semidirect product N ⋊ Q, in which Q acts on N by automorphisms; the dihedral group Dₙ is ℤ/n ⋊ ℤ/2, with the reflection inverting each rotation.

8. **The abelianisation G/[G, G] is the largest abelian quotient.** The commutator subgroup [G, G] is generated by all aba⁻¹b⁻¹. Any homomorphism to an abelian group sends these to e, so it factors through G/[G, G]. For Sₙ with n ≥ 2 the commutator subgroup is Aₙ, so every homomorphism from Sₙ to an abelian group factors through the sign.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Homomorphism | A map with φ(ab) = φ(a)φ(b) |
| Isomorphism, G ≅ H | A bijective homomorphism |
| Kernel, ker φ | The elements sent to e; always a normal subgroup |
| Image, im φ | The subgroup φ(G) of the target |
| Normal subgroup, N ◁ G | A subgroup with gNg⁻¹ = N for all g |
| Quotient group G/N | The cosets of N, multiplied by (aN)(bN) = abN |
| Semidirect product N ⋊ Q | Pairs from N and Q, with Q acting on N by automorphisms |
| Commutator subgroup [G, G] | Generated by all aba⁻¹b⁻¹; the quotient is abelian |

## Common misconceptions

- **"G/N is a subgroup of G."** It is a group of cosets. ℤ/2 is a quotient of ℤ, but ℤ has no element of order 2 and so no subgroup isomorphic to ℤ/2.
- **"A normal subgroup commutes with every element."** Normality says gN = Ng as sets, not gn = ng. The rotations form a normal subgroup of S₃, yet (12) does not commute with (123).
- **"Knowing N and G/N, you know G."** ℤ/4 and ℤ/2 × ℤ/2 have the same N and the same quotient.
