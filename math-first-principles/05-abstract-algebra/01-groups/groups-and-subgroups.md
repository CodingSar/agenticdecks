## Essence

A group is a set with one associative operation, an identity element and inverses: the least structure in which every action can be undone. The symmetries of any object form a group, because symmetries compose, "do nothing" is a symmetry, and each one can be reversed. The first real theorem is Lagrange's: in a finite group the size of a subgroup divides the size of the group, because the subgroup's cosets cut the group into equal pieces. Fermat's little theorem and Euler's theorem are Lagrange's theorem applied to the multiplicative group of ℤ/n. The converse fails, and its smallest failure, the alternating group A₄, shows that divisibility alone does not dictate structure.

## Core tenets

1. **A group is a set G with an associative operation, an identity e and inverses.** Associativity lets brackets be dropped; inverses let ax = b be solved uniquely as x = a⁻¹b, so each row of the multiplication table is a permutation of G. Examples: (ℤ, +); (ℤ/n, +); the units (ℤ/n)ˣ, the residues coprime to n, under multiplication; the symmetric group Sₙ of all n! permutations of n objects; the dihedral group Dₙ of the 2n symmetries of a regular n-gon; the invertible real n × n matrices GLₙ(ℝ). Drop inverses and you get a monoid such as (ℕ, +), where 3 + x = 2 has no solution. A group with ab = ba for all a, b is abelian; S₃, of order 6, is the smallest group that is not.

2. **A subgroup H ≤ G is a non-empty subset with ab⁻¹ ∈ H whenever a, b ∈ H.** That one condition gives e, inverses and closure. For a finite subset, closure under the operation is enough: the powers of a must repeat, so aᵏ = e for some k ≥ 1 and a⁻¹ = aᵏ⁻¹. For an infinite subset it is not: ℕ is closed under + inside ℤ but is not a subgroup. Examples: the even integers in ℤ; the rotations in Dₙ.

3. **Cyclic groups are completely understood.** The subgroup generated by g is ⟨g⟩ = {gᵏ : k ∈ ℤ}, and its size is the order of g, the least n ≥ 1 with gⁿ = e (or infinity). Every cyclic group is isomorphic to ℤ or to ℤ/n. Every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic: take the least positive exponent m in it and divide any other exponent by m; the remainder must be 0. So ℤ/n has exactly one subgroup of each order d dividing n, namely the one generated by n/d.

4. **Lagrange's theorem: if H is a subgroup of a finite group G, then |H| divides |G|.** The left cosets aH = {ah : h ∈ H} are the classes of the equivalence relation a ∼ b if and only if a⁻¹b ∈ H, so they partition G. The map h ↦ ah is a bijection from H to aH, so every coset has exactly |H| elements. Hence |G| = [G : H]·|H|, where the index [G : H] is the number of cosets.

5. **Lagrange's corollaries are elementary number theory in disguise.** The order of any element g divides |G|, since it is the size of ⟨g⟩, and so g^|G| = e. A group of prime order p is cyclic, generated by any element other than e. Applied to (ℤ/p)ˣ, of order p − 1, this gives Fermat's little theorem: a^(p−1) ≡ 1 (mod p) when p does not divide a. Applied to (ℤ/n)ˣ, whose order is Euler's totient φ(n), it gives a^φ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n) when the greatest common divisor (gcd) of a and n is 1. That identity is why Rivest–Shamir–Adleman (RSA) decryption undoes encryption.

6. **The converse of Lagrange is false: A₄ has order 12 and no subgroup of order 6.** The alternating group A₄ consists of the even permutations of four objects: the identity, three double transpositions such as (12)(34), and eight 3-cycles. Suppose H ≤ A₄ had order 6, hence index 2. If g ∉ H, the only other coset is gH, so g² ∉ H would force g² = gh with h ∈ H, and then g = h ∈ H, a contradiction. So g² ∈ H for every g. Every 3-cycle σ has σ³ = e, so σ = σ⁴ = (σ²)² ∈ H. That puts all eight 3-cycles in H, which has only six elements.

7. **Partial converses hold, and they are deep.** Cauchy's theorem: if a prime p divides |G|, then G has an element of order p. Sylow's first theorem: if pᵏ divides |G|, then G has a subgroup of order pᵏ. Finite abelian groups satisfy the full converse. Beyond that, whether a subgroup of order d exists depends on how the group multiplies, not on its size.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Group | Set with an associative operation, an identity and inverses |
| Order of a group, ∣G∣ | Its number of elements |
| Order of an element | Least n ≥ 1 with gⁿ = e, or infinity |
| Subgroup H ≤ G | Subset that is a group under the same operation |
| Coset aH | The set {ah : h ∈ H}; the cosets of H partition G |
| Index [G : H] | Number of cosets of H in G |
| Cyclic group | Generated by one element; isomorphic to ℤ or ℤ/n |
| Sₙ and Aₙ | All permutations of n objects, of order n!; the even ones, of order n!/2 |

## Common misconceptions

- **"(ab)⁻¹ = a⁻¹b⁻¹."** In general (ab)⁻¹ = b⁻¹a⁻¹: undo the last thing first, as with socks and shoes. The two agree exactly when a and b commute.
- **"The cosets of a subgroup are subgroups too."** Only H itself contains e. The other cosets are translated copies of H, equal in size but not closed under the operation.
- **"Groups are about numbers."** The elements can be permutations, matrices or rotations. The content lies entirely in how they combine.
