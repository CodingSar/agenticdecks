## Essence

Finite fields are completely classified, and the classification is short. A field with q elements exists if and only if q is a prime power pⁿ, and it is unique up to isomorphism, so one can speak of the field 𝔽_q. It is the set of roots of x^q − x, built as 𝔽ₚ[x]/(f) for any irreducible f of degree n, and its non-zero elements form a cyclic group. The Frobenius map x ↦ xᵖ is a field automorphism that generates all the symmetries. Because they are finite, exact and fully understood, these fields are the arithmetic of error-correcting codes, much of cryptography and the reduction of equations mod p.

## Core tenets

1. **A finite field has prime-power order.** Adding 1 to itself must eventually give 0, and the least such number p is prime, since a factorisation p = ab would make a·1 and b·1 zero divisors. So the field contains the prime field 𝔽ₚ = ℤ/p and is a vector space over it, of some finite dimension n, hence has pⁿ elements. There is no field with 6 or 10 elements.

2. **The Frobenius map x ↦ xᵖ is a ring homomorphism in characteristic p.** In (a + b)ᵖ every middle binomial coefficient C(p, k) with 0 < k < p is divisible by p, so (a + b)ᵖ = aᵖ + bᵖ, the "freshman's dream" that is true here. On a finite field Frobenius is injective, hence bijective: an automorphism.

3. **For each prime power q = pⁿ a field of order q exists: the splitting field of x^q − x over 𝔽ₚ.** Its derivative is q·x^(q−1) − 1 = −1, which has no roots, so x^q − x has q distinct roots. These roots are closed under addition, multiplication and inverses, because they are the fixed points of the n-th power of Frobenius. So they form a field of exactly q elements.

4. **It is unique up to isomorphism.** In any field F of order q, the non-zero elements form a group of order q − 1, so Lagrange gives a^(q−1) = 1 and hence a^q = a for all a, including 0. So F consists of the q roots of x^q − x and is a splitting field of that polynomial, and splitting fields are unique up to isomorphism.

5. **The multiplicative group 𝔽_q^× is cyclic.** Let ψ(d) be the number of elements of order d. Elements of order d are roots of x^d − 1, which has at most d roots in a field, so if any exist they make up a cyclic group of order d, and ψ(d) is 0 or φ(d), Euler's totient. Since ∑ ψ(d) = q − 1 = ∑ φ(d) over divisors d of q − 1, every ψ(d) equals φ(d), and ψ(q − 1) > 0 gives a generator. The argument needs a field: in the ring ℤ/8 the units {1, 3, 5, 7} all square to 1 and form no cyclic group.

6. **Subfields and symmetries are as simple as possible.** 𝔽_(p^m) sits inside 𝔽_(p^n) if and only if m divides n. The automorphism group of 𝔽_(p^n) is cyclic of order n, generated by Frobenius, and its subgroups correspond exactly to these subfields: the cleanest instance of the Galois correspondence.

7. **Concrete arithmetic uses an irreducible polynomial.** 𝔽₄ = 𝔽₂[x]/(x² + x + 1) = {0, 1, α, α + 1} with α² = α + 1; it is not ℤ/4, in which 2 has no inverse. Irreducibles of every degree exist over 𝔽ₚ: over 𝔽₂ there is 1 of degree 2, 2 of degree 3 and 3 of degree 4, and in general (1/n)·∑ μ(d)·q^(n/d) of degree n over 𝔽_q, summed over the divisors d of n, where μ is the Möbius function. The Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) does its byte arithmetic in 𝔽₂₅₆ = 𝔽₂[x]/(x⁸ + x⁴ + x³ + x + 1); see [symmetric encryption](../../../cs-first-principles/11-security-and-cryptography/02-cryptography/symmetric-encryption.md).

8. **Finite fields power codes and cryptography.** Reed–Solomon codes evaluate polynomials over 𝔽_q at many points; a polynomial of degree below k is determined by any k values, so lost symbols can be recovered. Diffie–Hellman key exchange relies on exponentiation in the cyclic group 𝔽ₚ^× being easy while the discrete logarithm appears hard. Elliptic curves over 𝔽_q give smaller keys for the same security. Counting the points of equations over 𝔽_q links them to number theory and to the Weil conjectures.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 𝔽_q | The field with q = pⁿ elements, unique up to isomorphism |
| Characteristic | The prime p with p·1 = 0 |
| Prime field 𝔽ₚ | ℤ/p, contained in every field of characteristic p |
| Frobenius | The automorphism x ↦ xᵖ |
| Primitive element | A generator of the cyclic group 𝔽_q^× |
| Irreducible polynomial | Used to represent 𝔽_(p^n) as 𝔽ₚ[x]/(f) |
| Discrete logarithm | Finding k from gᵏ in a cyclic group |

## Common misconceptions

- **"𝔽₄ is ℤ/4."** ℤ/4 has the zero divisor 2. The field with four elements has characteristic 2 and is built from an irreducible quadratic.
- **"Finite fields are just integers mod p."** Only the prime fields are. The others need irreducible polynomials.
- **"Some finite division ring might be non-commutative."** Wedderburn's theorem (1905) says every finite division ring is a field.
