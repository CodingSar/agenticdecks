## Essence

A ring is a set where you can add, subtract and multiply, as in ℤ, polynomials or matrices. Its ideals play the role normal subgroups play for groups: they are exactly the kernels of ring homomorphisms, and dividing by an ideal gives a quotient ring, the general form of "arithmetic mod n". The kind of ideal controls the kind of quotient: dividing by a prime ideal leaves an integral domain, where products of non-zero elements are non-zero, and dividing by a maximal ideal leaves a field. This dictionary lets one construct new number systems, such as ℂ = ℝ[x]/(x² + 1), and turns questions about equations into questions about ideals.

## Core tenets

1. **A ring is an abelian group (R, +) with an associative, distributive multiplication and an identity 1.** Here rings are commutative unless stated otherwise. Examples: ℤ, ℤ/n, ℚ, polynomials ℝ[x], continuous functions on [0, 1], and the non-commutative n × n matrices Mₙ(ℝ). A unit has a multiplicative inverse; a zero divisor is a non-zero a with ab = 0 for some non-zero b, as 2 · 3 = 0 in ℤ/6. An integral domain has no zero divisors, which is what makes cancellation (ab = ac with a ≠ 0 gives b = c) valid. A field is a ring in which every non-zero element is a unit.

2. **Ideals are exactly the kernels of ring homomorphisms.** An ideal I is an additive subgroup that absorbs multiplication: r ∈ R and a ∈ I give ra ∈ I. Kernels absorb, since φ(ra) = φ(r)·0 = 0. Conversely, absorption is precisely what makes (a + I)(b + I) = ab + I well defined, because (a + i)(b + j) = ab + (aj + ib + ij). So R/I is a ring and R → R/I has kernel I. A subring such as ℤ ⊂ ℚ is not an ideal, and ℚ/ℤ has no well-defined product.

3. **First isomorphism theorem: R/ker φ ≅ im φ.** Evaluation at i gives ℝ[x] → ℂ with kernel the multiples of x² + 1, so ℝ[x]/(x² + 1) ≅ ℂ: the complex numbers are polynomials with x² + 1 declared zero. Reduction gives ℤ/nℤ. Every ideal of ℤ is nℤ, by division with remainder, and the ideal (a, b) generated by a and b is (d), where d is the greatest common divisor (gcd): Bézout's identity in ideal language.

4. **I is prime if and only if R/I is an integral domain; I is maximal if and only if R/I is a field.** A proper ideal P is prime when ab ∈ P forces a ∈ P or b ∈ P, which says exactly that R/P has no zero divisors. A proper ideal M is maximal when no ideal lies strictly between M and R. By the correspondence theorem, ideals of R/M match ideals between M and R, and a ring whose only ideals are 0 and itself is a field, since any non-zero a generates the ideal (a) = R, so a is a unit. Fields are integral domains, so maximal ideals are prime.

5. **Prime need not be maximal.** In ℤ, (0) is prime, since ℤ is a domain, but not maximal. In ℤ[x], the ideal (x) is prime with quotient ℤ, but it lies inside (2, x), whose quotient is the field 𝔽₂ with two elements. In ℤ the non-zero primes and maximal ideals coincide: they are the pℤ, with quotient the field 𝔽ₚ = ℤ/p.

6. **Every proper ideal lies in a maximal ideal, by Zorn's lemma.** A chain of proper ideals has a proper union, because none contains 1, so Zorn's lemma gives a maximal element. In ℤ or any ring where ideals are finitely generated no choice is needed, but in general the statement is equivalent to the axiom of choice. It guarantees that every non-zero ring has a field as a quotient.

7. **The Chinese remainder theorem splits rings.** If I + J = R, then R/(I ∩ J) ≅ R/I × R/J, by sending r to its two residues; surjectivity comes from writing 1 = i + j. For coprime m and n, ℤ/mn ≅ ℤ/m × ℤ/n, which explains why φ(mn) = φ(m)φ(n) for Euler's totient. Without coprimality it fails: ℤ/4 has an element of order 4 and ℤ/2 × ℤ/2 does not.

8. **Ideals of polynomial rings are geometry.** The maximal ideals of ℂ[x, y] are exactly the (x − a, y − b), one for each point (a, b) of the plane: this is Hilbert's Nullstellensatz. More generally, ideals cut out solution sets of polynomial equations, which is the starting point of algebraic geometry. Dedekind introduced ideals to repair unique factorisation in rings of algebraic integers.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ring | Addition and multiplication with the usual laws, not necessarily inverses |
| Unit | An element with a multiplicative inverse |
| Zero divisor | A non-zero a with ab = 0 for some non-zero b |
| Integral domain | A non-zero commutative ring with no zero divisors |
| Field | A ring where every non-zero element is a unit |
| Ideal | An additive subgroup closed under multiplication by any ring element |
| Quotient ring R/I | Cosets a + I with the induced operations |
| Prime ideal | ab ∈ P forces a ∈ P or b ∈ P; quotient is a domain |
| Maximal ideal | No ideal strictly between it and R; quotient is a field |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A subring is the ring analogue of a normal subgroup."** Ideals are; most subrings are not ideals, and a ring cannot be divided by them.
- **"Prime ideals are generated by prime numbers."** In ℤ[x], (x) and (0) are prime. Primality is a property of the quotient.
- **"Every ring is commutative."** Matrix rings are not, and Mₙ(F) over a field F has no two-sided ideals except 0 and itself, although it is far from a field.
