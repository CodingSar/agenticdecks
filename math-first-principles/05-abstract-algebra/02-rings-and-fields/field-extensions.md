## Essence

A field extension L/K is a field L containing a smaller field K, such as ℚ ⊂ ℚ(√2) ⊂ ℝ ⊂ ℂ. The decisive observation is that L is a vector space over K, so linear algebra applies: the degree [L : K] is its dimension, and degrees multiply along towers. An element is algebraic if it satisfies a polynomial over K, and adjoining it produces a field K(α) ≅ K[x]/(m) whose degree is the degree of its minimal polynomial m. Splitting fields contain all roots of a polynomial, and the algebraic closure contains all roots of all polynomials. These ideas turn questions about numbers, such as "is ∛2 expressible with square roots?", into questions about dimensions.

## Core tenets

1. **The degree [L : K] is the dimension of L as a vector space over K.** Addition in L and multiplication by elements of K satisfy the vector space axioms. [ℂ : ℝ] = 2 with basis 1, i; [ℚ(√2) : ℚ] = 2 with basis 1, √2; [ℝ : ℚ] is infinite, indeed uncountable. An extension is finite when its degree is.

2. **The tower law: for fields K ⊆ M ⊆ L, [L : K] = [L : M]·[M : K].** If αᵢ is a basis of M over K and βⱼ a basis of L over M, the products αᵢβⱼ form a basis of L over K: every element is ∑ cⱼβⱼ with cⱼ ∈ M, and each cⱼ expands in the αᵢ. Consequence: ∛2 ∉ ℚ(√2), since ℚ(∛2) has degree 3 over ℚ, and 3 does not divide 2. Most impossibility proofs in Galois theory are this divisibility argument.

3. **An algebraic α has a minimal polynomial m, and K(α) ≅ K[x]/(m) has degree deg m.** Evaluation K[x] → L, f ↦ f(α), has a kernel that is a non-zero prime ideal, hence generated by a unique monic irreducible m. Its image is a field, so K[α] = K(α), with basis 1, α, …, α^(deg m − 1). If α is transcendental, as π is over ℚ, evaluation is injective and K(α) is the field of rational functions K(x), of infinite degree.

4. **Finite extensions are algebraic, and algebraic elements form a field.** If [L : K] = n, the n + 1 elements 1, α, …, αⁿ are linearly dependent, which is a polynomial relation. If α and β are algebraic, K(α, β) is finite over K by the tower law, so α + β, αβ and α/β are algebraic. The algebraic numbers ℚ̄ form a field, which is countable, since there are countably many polynomials with rational coefficients; hence most real numbers are transcendental.

5. **Every polynomial has a splitting field, unique up to isomorphism.** Adjoin a root of an irreducible factor by Kronecker's construction F[x]/(f), and repeat; after at most deg f steps the polynomial splits into linear factors, in a field of degree at most (deg f)!. The splitting field of x³ − 2 over ℚ is ℚ(∛2, ω), where ω = e^(2πi/3) is a primitive cube root of unity. It has degree 6, because ℚ(∛2) is real and of degree 3 while ω is not real and has degree 2 over it.

6. **Every field has an algebraic closure, unique up to isomorphism.** An algebraically closed field contains a root of every non-constant polynomial over itself. ℂ is algebraically closed by the fundamental theorem of algebra, and the algebraic closure of ℚ is ℚ̄ ⊂ ℂ. For a general field, Steinitz (1910) built the closure using Zorn's lemma, which the existence proof genuinely needs in general.

7. **Separability: an irreducible polynomial can have repeated roots only in characteristic p.** A repeated root is a common root of f and its formal derivative f′. For irreducible f this forces f′ = 0, impossible in characteristic 0 for non-constant f. Over 𝔽ₚ(t), rational functions in t, the polynomial xᵖ − t is irreducible, yet in its splitting field it equals (x − t^(1/p))ᵖ: one root repeated p times. Fields of characteristic 0 and finite fields are perfect, meaning all their extensions are separable.

8. **Primitive elements and normality.** A finite separable extension is generated by a single element: ℚ(√2, √3) = ℚ(√2 + √3). An extension is normal if every irreducible polynomial over K with one root in L splits completely in L; splitting fields are exactly the finite normal extensions. ℚ(∛2) is not normal over ℚ: it contains one root of x³ − 2 and not the other two, which are not real. Normal and separable together are Galois, the setting of the Galois correspondence.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Extension L/K | A field L containing K as a subfield |
| Degree [L : K] | The dimension of L as a K-vector space |
| Algebraic element | A root of a non-zero polynomial over K |
| Minimal polynomial | The monic irreducible polynomial of least degree with α as a root |
| K(α) | The smallest subfield containing K and α |
| Splitting field | The field generated over K by all roots of a polynomial |
| Algebraic closure | An algebraic extension in which every polynomial splits |
| Separable | Irreducible factors have distinct roots |
| Normal | Each irreducible polynomial with a root in L splits in L |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Adjoining one root adjoins them all."** ℚ(∛2) contains only the real cube root of 2.
- **"K(α) needs fractions as well as polynomials in α."** For algebraic α, K[α] is already a field, since 1/α is a polynomial in α.
- **"Degree measures size."** ℚ(√2) and ℚ(i) both have degree 2 over ℚ but are not isomorphic: only one of them embeds in ℝ.
