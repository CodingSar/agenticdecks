## Essence

Unique factorisation into primes feels like a law of nature, but it is a property of particular rings. In an integral domain one can still define irreducible elements, which cannot be split further, and prime elements, which divide one factor whenever they divide a product; uniqueness of factorisation holds exactly when the two notions agree. Division with remainder implies that every ideal is generated by one element, and that in turn implies unique factorisation: Euclidean domain ⇒ principal ideal domain (PID) ⇒ unique factorisation domain (UFD). The ring ℤ[√−5], where 6 = 2 · 3 = (1 + √−5)(1 − √−5), shows the chain can break. Kummer and Dedekind's response, factorising ideals instead of numbers, founded algebraic number theory.

## Core tenets

1. **In a domain, divisibility ignores units, and "prime" splits into two notions.** Associates differ by a unit factor, as 3 and −3 do in ℤ. A non-zero non-unit p is irreducible if p = ab forces a or b to be a unit, and prime if p dividing ab forces p to divide a or b. Prime implies irreducible in any domain. The converse is the heart of the matter: Euclid's lemma says it holds in ℤ.

2. **A UFD is a domain where every non-zero non-unit is a product of irreducibles, uniquely up to order and units.** Given that factorisations exist, uniqueness is equivalent to "every irreducible is prime": a prime dividing one factorisation must divide some factor of the other, so they can be cancelled one at a time. Examples: ℤ, F[x] for a field F, ℤ[x] and F[x, y].

3. **Euclidean domain ⇒ PID.** A Euclidean domain has a size function N with division with remainder: a = qb + r with r = 0 or N(r) < N(b). In a non-zero ideal I, pick b of least size; any a ∈ I leaves remainder r = a − qb ∈ I of smaller size, so r = 0 and I = (b). Examples: ℤ with absolute value, F[x] with degree, and the Gaussian integers ℤ[i] with N(a + bi) = a² + b².

4. **PID ⇒ UFD.** Two facts suffice. First, an increasing chain of ideals (a₁) ⊆ (a₂) ⊆ ⋯ has a union that is again an ideal, hence principal, generated by some element lying in one (aₙ), so the chain stops; this blocks infinite factorisation and makes factorisations exist. Second, if p is irreducible, (p) is maximal among principal ideals, hence among all ideals, so R/(p) is a field and (p) is prime. The converse fails: ℤ[x] is a UFD, but the ideal (2, x) is not principal. Nor is every PID Euclidean: ℤ[(1 + √−19)/2] is a PID with no Euclidean function.

5. **ℤ[√−5] is not a UFD.** Use the multiplicative norm N(a + b√−5) = a² + 5b². The factors in 6 = 2 · 3 = (1 + √−5)(1 − √−5) have norms 4, 9, 6 and 6. No element has norm 2 or 3, since a² + 5b² never takes those values, so all four factors are irreducible. The only units are ±1, so 2 is not associate to 1 ± √−5. Thus 2 is irreducible but not prime: it divides the product (1 + √−5)(1 − √−5) without dividing either factor.

6. **Ideals restore uniqueness.** In ℤ[√−5] let P = (2, 1 + √−5), Q = (3, 1 + √−5) and Q′ = (3, 1 − √−5). Then
   ```
   (2) = P²        (3) = QQ′
   (1 + √−5) = PQ  (1 − √−5) = PQ′
   (6) = P²QQ′     in both factorisations
   ```
   The prime ideals P, Q and Q′ are not principal, and the two factorisations of 6 are regroupings of one ideal factorisation. In the ring of integers of any number field, every non-zero ideal factors uniquely into prime ideals. The class group, ideals modulo principal ones, measures the failure of unique factorisation; for ℤ[√−5] it has order 2.

7. **Unique factorisation has arithmetic consequences.** Because ℤ[i] is Euclidean, a prime p ≡ 1 (mod 4) is not prime in ℤ[i]: it splits as p = (a + bi)(a − bi) = a² + b², giving Fermat's two-squares theorem. Lamé's 1847 attempt at Fermat's last theorem assumed unique factorisation in the rings ℤ[ζₚ] generated by a p-th root of unity ζₚ. Kummer had already shown that it fails, the smallest case being p = 23, and developed "ideal numbers" to recover a proof for many primes.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Unit | An invertible element |
| Associates | Elements that differ by a unit factor |
| Irreducible | A non-unit with no factorisation into two non-units |
| Prime element | p dividing ab forces p to divide a or b |
| Euclidean domain | A domain with division with remainder by a size function |
| PID | A domain in which every ideal has a single generator |
| UFD | A domain with unique factorisation into irreducibles |
| Norm N | A multiplicative size, such as a² + 5b² on ℤ[√−5] |
| Class group | Ideals modulo principal ideals; trivial exactly for PIDs among rings of integers |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Irreducible and prime mean the same thing."** Only in a UFD. In ℤ[√−5], 2 is irreducible but not prime.
- **"Every UFD is a PID."** ℤ[x] and F[x, y] are UFDs with non-principal ideals such as (2, x) and (x, y).
- **"Bigger rings factor better."** In the ring of all algebraic integers there are no irreducibles at all, since α = √α · √α forever.
