## Essence
The exterior algebra of a vector space is the algebra of oriented areas, volumes and their higher analogues. Its product, the wedge product, is anticommutative: v ∧ w = −w ∧ v, so v ∧ v = 0. The element v ∧ w represents the oriented parallelogram spanned by v and w, and v₁ ∧ ⋯ ∧ vₖ the oriented k-dimensional parallelepiped. Hermann Grassmann introduced this calculus in 1844, decades before it was understood. Its top degree is one-dimensional, and the action of a linear map there is multiplication by the determinant, which gives the cleanest definition of the determinant. Its dual version, applied at every point of a manifold, is the language of differential forms and of Stokes's theorem.

## Core tenets
1. **The exterior algebra is a quotient with a universal property.** Λ(V) is the tensor algebra of V modulo the ideal generated by all v ⊗ v. Its degree-k part Λᵏ(V) has the universal property for alternating k-linear maps: each one out of V × ⋯ × V factors uniquely through v₁ ∧ ⋯ ∧ vₖ. Anticommutativity follows from expanding (v + w) ∧ (v + w) = 0.

2. **Bases of exterior powers count subsets.** If e₁, …, eₙ is a basis of V, the products e_{i₁} ∧ ⋯ ∧ e_{iₖ} with i₁ < ⋯ < iₖ form a basis of Λᵏ(V). So dim Λᵏ(V) = C(n, k), Λᵏ(V) = 0 for k > n, and the whole algebra has dimension 2ⁿ. For ℝ³ the dimensions in degrees 0 to 3 are 1, 3, 3, 1: scalars, vectors, oriented planes and oriented volumes.

3. **A wedge product vanishes if and only if the vectors are dependent.** If one vᵢ is a combination of the others, expanding gives terms with a repeated vector, all zero. If they are independent, extend them to a basis, and the wedge is a basis element. So a non-zero v₁ ∧ ⋯ ∧ vₖ determines the subspace they span, up to scale: the Plücker embedding of the Grassmannian of k-dimensional subspaces into the [projective space](../../08-geometry/01-classical-geometry/projective-geometry.md) of Λᵏ(V).

4. **The determinant is the top exterior power.** Λⁿ(V) is one-dimensional, so any linear T: V → V acts on it as multiplication by a scalar: Tv₁ ∧ ⋯ ∧ Tvₙ = c · v₁ ∧ ⋯ ∧ vₙ. That scalar is det T, defined with no basis or matrix at all. The product rule is then immediate, since applying S after T multiplies by det T and then by det S. Expanding Te₁ ∧ ⋯ ∧ Teₙ in a basis recovers the permutation formula of [determinants](../02-determinants-and-eigenvalues/determinants.md).

5. **The other exterior powers carry the rest of the characteristic polynomial.** T induces Λᵏ(T) on each Λᵏ(V), and

   ```
   det(tI − T) = Σ over k from 0 to n of (−1)ᵏ · tr Λᵏ(T) · tⁿ⁻ᵏ
   ```

   so the trace is tr Λ¹(T) and the determinant is Λⁿ(T). The matrix entries of Λᵏ(T) are the k × k minors of T, and functoriality, Λᵏ(ST) = Λᵏ(S)Λᵏ(T), is the Cauchy–Binet formula for minors of a product.

6. **The cross product is a three-dimensional accident.** With an inner product and an orientation, the Hodge star identifies Λᵏ(V) with Λⁿ⁻ᵏ(V). In ℝ³ this identifies Λ²(ℝ³) with ℝ³, and v × w is the image of v ∧ w. Among positive n, C(n, 2) = n holds only for n = 3, so this construction works only in ℝ³. Elsewhere one keeps v ∧ w as an oriented plane, which is how rotations and angular momentum are best described in other dimensions.

7. **Exterior algebra on the dual gives differential forms.** Elements of Λᵏ(V∗) are alternating k-linear functionals, which measure k-dimensional parallelepipeds. A differential k-form assigns one to each point of a manifold, and the exterior derivative d unifies gradient, curl and divergence. Stokes's theorem says that the integral of dω over a region M equals the integral of ω over its boundary ∂M, containing the fundamental theorem of calculus and the theorems of Green, Gauss and Stokes ([vector calculus](../../06-real-analysis/03-calculus-of-several-variables/vector-calculus-and-the-integral-theorems.md), [differential forms](../../08-geometry/02-differential-geometry/differential-forms-and-de-rham-cohomology.md)).

8. **Non-degenerate 2-forms need even dimension.** A symplectic form is an element ω of Λ²(V∗) whose associated map V → V∗ is invertible. Since an antisymmetric n × n matrix A has det A = det Aᵀ = (−1)ⁿ det A, an odd-dimensional space has none. In dimension 2n the n-fold power ω ∧ ⋯ ∧ ω is a volume form, which underlies Liouville's theorem that [Hamiltonian flows](../../10-differential-equations-and-dynamics/04-dynamical-systems/hamiltonian-systems-and-integrability.md) preserve phase-space volume.

## Key concepts
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Wedge product | Anticommutative product with v ∧ v = 0 |
| Λᵏ(V) | k-th exterior power; dimension C(n, k) |
| k-vector | Element of Λᵏ(V); oriented k-dimensional piece |
| Decomposable | Of the form v₁ ∧ ⋯ ∧ vₖ |
| Top exterior power | Λⁿ(V), one-dimensional; T acts on it by det T |
| Hodge star | Identifies Λᵏ with Λⁿ⁻ᵏ, using an inner product and orientation |
| Differential form | Element of Λᵏ of the cotangent space at each point |
| Symplectic form | Non-degenerate alternating 2-form |

## Common misconceptions
- **"Every element of Λ²(V) is v ∧ w."**: in ℝ⁴, e₁ ∧ e₂ + e₃ ∧ e₄ is not, since its wedge with itself is 2 e₁ ∧ e₂ ∧ e₃ ∧ e₄ ≠ 0, while (v ∧ w) ∧ (v ∧ w) = 0.
- **"The cross product exists in every dimension."**: the construction through the Hodge star works only in ℝ³. (A different bilinear cross product, built from the octonions, exists in ℝ⁷.)
- **"Exterior algebra is just determinants in disguise."**: determinants are its top degree only; the lower degrees carry subspaces, minors, the characteristic polynomial and differential forms.
