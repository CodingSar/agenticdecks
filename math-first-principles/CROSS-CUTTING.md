# Cross-Cutting Principles

The rest of this knowledge base is a tree: sixteen layers, each split into areas and leaves. A tree gives every topic one parent, which is what makes bottom-up reading possible. But mathematics is a graph. A small set of ideas does the work in layer after layer, under different names and at different levels of abstraction. The parity count that shows a chessboard with two opposite corners removed cannot be tiled by dominoes, the determinant that shows a matrix has no inverse, and the homology group that shows a disc cannot be retracted onto its boundary circle are one idea: find a quantity that the allowed moves cannot change. Newton's method for √2, the stability of an equilibrium read off its Jacobian matrix, and the Lie algebra of the rotation group are another: replace the curved by its best straight approximation, then control the error. Seeing the idea once saves learning it a dozen times, and lets a warning learned in one layer ("this invariant is not complete") protect you in another.

Each section below states one principle as a claim, explains why it keeps recurring, and follows it up the stack, lowest layer first, with a link to a leaf for each appearance. Each ends with the failure mode: concrete cases where the principle, applied without its hypotheses, gives a wrong answer, and which neighbouring principle pulls against it.

**How to use it.** Read across, not down. Pick a principle, open two leaves from distant layers in its table and compare them: the determinant in layer 04 and the Euler characteristic in layer 07 are worth reading side by side. Parent READMEs throughout the tree end with "Cross-cutting threads" that link back here, each with a phrase on how the principle shows up in that area. The last section, [Principles that pull against each other](#principles-that-pull-against-each-other), lists the tensions behind many hard choices of method: when to generalise, when to compute, when to trust a linear model, when an existence proof is enough.

## Abstraction and generalisation

**Claim: a proof uses only some of the properties of its object; restate the theorem with only those properties as hypotheses and it becomes true of every structure that has them, and is often easier to prove because the irrelevant detail has gone.** The force behind it is the axiomatic method itself. A proof is a finite object that cites finitely many facts, so "what did this proof actually use?" always has an answer, and a theorem derived from axioms holds in every model of those axioms. The payoff is economy (one proof of Lagrange's theorem covers permutations, residues and matrices at once) and clarity, since stripping away the specific features of ℝ or ℝⁿ often removes the very features that suggested a wrong approach. The twentieth century turned this habit into a programme: groups, rings, metric and topological spaces, measure spaces and categories are all the hypotheses of some family of proofs, given a name.

|---|---|---|

| 04 | [Fields and vector spaces](04-linear-algebra/01-vector-spaces-and-linear-maps/fields-and-vector-spaces.md) | The vector space axioms make sense over any field, so Gaussian elimination and dimension work over the two-element field 𝔽₂, where codes live, exactly as over ℝ. |
| 05 | [Groups and subgroups](05-abstract-algebra/01-groups/groups-and-subgroups.md) | Lagrange's theorem, proved once for an abstract finite group, says the order of every symmetry, every residue class and every permutation divides the order of its group. |
| 06 | [Metric spaces and continuity](06-real-analysis/03-metric-spaces/metric-spaces-and-continuity.md) | A metric keeps exactly what the proofs about ℝ used (positivity, symmetry, the triangle inequality), so the same proofs run in spaces of functions. |
| 07 | [Topological spaces and continuous maps](07-topology/01-point-set-topology/topological-spaces-and-continuous-maps.md) | Open sets replace distances; continuity, compactness and connectedness survive with no metric at all. |
| 11 | [Probability spaces and Kolmogorov's axioms](11-probability-and-statistics/01-measure-theoretic-probability/probability-spaces-and-kolmogorovs-axioms.md) | Probability becomes a measure of total mass 1, so dice, coin sequences and continuous distributions become one theory. |
| 12 | [Schemes](12-number-theory-and-algebraic-geometry/03-algebraic-geometry/schemes.md) | Replacing varieties over ℂ by spectra of arbitrary commutative rings lets geometric methods act on ℤ itself. |
| 15 | [Learning and teaching mathematics](15-mathematics-in-context/learning-and-teaching-mathematics.md) | The abstract definition is the end of an understanding, not its start; learners who meet it first often cannot use it. |

**When it misleads.** Dropping a hypothesis can drop the theorem with it, and the loss is easy to miss when the proof in the special case never mentioned the hypothesis by name. The intermediate value theorem fails in the ordered field ℚ (x² − 2 changes sign on [1, 2] with no rational root), so "ordered field" is too general for it. Finite-dimensional linear algebra says an injective linear map V → V is surjective; on the infinite-dimensional space of sequences the right shift (x₁, x₂, …) ↦ (0, x₁, x₂, …) is injective and not surjective. In a general topological space, sequences no longer detect closure or compactness, so a proof that quietly uses sequential compactness has assumed first countability. "Every vector space has a basis" is true only with the axiom of choice, and the basis of ℝ over ℚ that it supplies cannot be written down. Generality also has a cost in content: the more objects a theory covers, the less it can say about each, which is the tension with [decomposition and classification](#decomposition-and-classification). And the abstract statement still needs the [structure and morphism](#structure-and-morphism) principle to say which examples are "the same".

## Structure and morphism

**Claim: within a theory, an object is known by the maps into and out of it that preserve the theory's structure, so choosing the morphisms chooses the theory and defines what "the same" means.** The force is that maps compose, and composition carries information that elements do not. A homomorphism's kernel and image already contain the isomorphism theorems; a continuous map transports compactness and connectedness; an isometry transports curvature. Category theory makes this exact: the Yoneda lemma says an object X of a category is determined up to unique isomorphism by the functor Hom(−, X) that records every map into it. Isomorphism, the invertible morphism, is precisely "indistinguishable by this theory".

| 04 | [Linear maps and matrices](04-linear-algebra/01-vector-spaces-and-linear-maps/linear-maps-and-matrices.md) | A matrix is a linear map written in a chosen basis; a change of basis conjugates it, A ↦ P⁻¹AP, so the map's own properties are those unchanged by conjugation. |
| 05 | [Homomorphisms and quotient groups](05-abstract-algebra/01-groups/homomorphisms-and-quotient-groups.md) | The kernel of a homomorphism φ: G → H is a normal subgroup, and G/ker φ ≅ im φ: the first isomorphism theorem. |
| 05 | [The Galois correspondence](05-abstract-algebra/03-galois-theory/the-galois-correspondence.md) | The field automorphisms fixing the base field carry all the information about a Galois extension. |
| 07 | [Topological spaces and continuous maps](07-topology/01-point-set-topology/topological-spaces-and-continuous-maps.md) | Continuous maps are the morphisms and homeomorphisms the isomorphisms; a coffee cup and a doughnut are the same object. |
| 12 | [Elliptic curves](12-number-theory-and-algebraic-geometry/04-elliptic-curves-and-modular-forms/elliptic-curves.md) | The chord-and-tangent law makes a cubic curve a group, and isogenies are the maps that respect both the geometry and the group. |
| 13 | [Categories and functors](13-structure-and-metamathematics/01-category-theory/categories-and-functors.md) | Arrows become the primary data, and functors are the structure-preserving maps between whole theories. |
| 13 | [Natural transformations and the Yoneda lemma](13-structure-and-metamathematics/01-category-theory/natural-transformations-and-the-yoneda-lemma.md) | An object is determined up to unique isomorphism by the maps into it. |

**When it misleads.** The morphisms are a choice, and the wrong choice gives the wrong notion of sameness. Bare functions are too coarse to see dimension: there is a bijection ℝ → ℝ², so set-theoretic isomorphism cannot tell a line from a plane, and continuity is needed (invariance of domain then separates them). Homeomorphisms are too coarse for smooth geometry: Milnor's exotic 7-spheres are homeomorphic to the standard sphere and not diffeomorphic to it, and ℝ⁴ carries uncountably many inequivalent smooth structures. Knowing that two objects are isomorphic is also less than knowing an isomorphism. A finite-dimensional vector space V is isomorphic to its dual V∗, but every isomorphism depends on a choice of basis, while V ≅ V∗∗ needs no choice; arguments that identify V with V∗ silently fix a basis and break when it changes. The algebraic closure of a field is unique only up to an isomorphism that is itself not unique, and ℂ has a non-trivial automorphism (conjugation) fixing ℝ, so "the" square root of −1 is not determined by algebra alone. See [equivalence and quotients](#equivalence-and-quotients) for the price of identifying isomorphic objects.

## Equivalence and quotients

**Claim: when a construction should ignore certain differences, declare objects that differ only in those ways equivalent and work with the equivalence classes; a function or operation on the classes is well defined exactly when it gives the same answer on every representative.** The force is that every equivalence relation partitions its set, every surjection is the quotient by "has the same image", and quotients are how new objects are built from old ones. The number systems are constructed this way: ℤ from pairs of natural numbers, ℚ from pairs of integers, ℝ from Cauchy sequences of rationals up to null difference, and ℤ/n from ℤ. Algebra builds quotient groups and rings, topology glues spaces, and homotopy theory identifies maps that deform into one another.

| 02 | [Integers and rational numbers](02-number-systems/01-constructing-the-numbers/integers-and-rational-numbers.md) | ℤ is pairs (a, b) of natural numbers with (a, b) ~ (c, d) when a + d = b + c; ℚ is pairs of integers up to cross-multiplication. |
| 02 | [Modular arithmetic](02-number-systems/02-elementary-number-theory/modular-arithmetic.md) | ℤ/n identifies integers with the same remainder; sums and products are well defined because nℤ absorbs multiplication by any integer. |
| 03 | [Counting principles and binomial coefficients](03-combinatorics-and-graph-theory/01-enumeration/counting-principles-and-binomial-coefficients.md) | There are n!/(n − k)! ordered selections of k items, and each k-subset arises from k! of them, so C(n, k) = n!/(k!(n − k)!) counts classes. |
| 05 | [Homomorphisms and quotient groups](05-abstract-algebra/01-groups/homomorphisms-and-quotient-groups.md) | The cosets of N multiply consistently exactly when N is normal; then G/N is a group. |
| 07 | [Subspaces, products and quotients](07-topology/01-point-set-topology/subspaces-products-and-quotients.md) | Gluing opposite sides of a square gives the torus, the Klein bottle or the real projective plane, depending on the orientations. |
| 12 | [Ideals and the class group](12-number-theory-and-algebraic-geometry/01-algebraic-number-theory/ideals-and-the-class-group.md) | Fractional ideals modulo principal ones form a finite group, trivial exactly when the ring of integers has unique factorisation. |
| 13 | [Higher categories and homotopy type theory](13-structure-and-metamathematics/01-category-theory/higher-categories-and-homotopy-type-theory.md) | The univalence axiom identifies equivalent types and keeps track of how they are identified. |

**When it misleads.** The commonest failure is a "function" on classes that depends on the representative. Adding fractions by a/b ⊕ c/d = (a + c)/(b + d) gives 1/2 ⊕ 1/3 = 2/5 but 2/4 ⊕ 1/3 = 3/7, though 1/2 = 2/4. Quotienting a group by a subgroup that is not normal gives a set of cosets with no group law. Quotient topologies can be degenerate: every coset of ℚ in ℝ is dense, so ℝ/ℚ has only ∅ and the whole space as open sets, and gluing two copies of ℝ along ℝ ∖ {0} gives the line with two origins, which is not Hausdorff. Choosing one representative from each class is itself a use of the axiom of choice: picking one point of [0, 1] from each coset of ℚ produces Vitali's non-measurable set. Finally, a quotient forgets exactly what it was told to forget, and sometimes that was needed: homotopy equivalence identifies a point with a disc and with ℝⁿ, and the contractible Whitehead manifold has the homotopy type of ℝ³ without being homeomorphic to it. [Invariants](#invariants) are the functions that descend to the quotient, and their limits are the quotient's.

## Invariants

**Claim: a quantity unchanged by every allowed move can never separate objects the moves connect, so two objects with different values are not connected by any sequence of moves; an invariant is complete when equal values also prove the objects equivalent.** The force is that impossibility claims quantify over infinitely many possible move sequences, and an invariant controls them all with one finite computation. In the language of the previous section, an invariant is a function on the quotient by the allowed moves. The pattern runs from parity arguments in puzzles to the determinant, the Euler characteristic, homology, curvature, and the class group.

| Layer | Where it appears | How |
|---|---|---|
| 00 | [Mathematical induction](00-logic-and-proof/02-methods-of-proof/mathematical-induction.md) | A strengthened inductive hypothesis, or a loop invariant, is a property each step preserves, so it holds at the end. |
| 02 | [Modular arithmetic](02-number-systems/02-elementary-number-theory/modular-arithmetic.md) | Every square is 0 or 1 modulo 4, so x² + y² = 4k + 3 has no integer solutions: the residue is the invariant. |
| 03 | [Graphs and connectivity](03-combinatorics-and-graph-theory/02-graph-theory/graphs-and-connectivity.md) | The degrees sum to twice the number of edges, so odd-degree vertices come in pairs; the four odd vertices of Königsberg rule out an Euler walk. |
| 04 | [Determinants](04-linear-algebra/02-determinants-and-eigenvalues/determinants.md) | det(P⁻¹AP) = det A and det(AB) = det A · det B, so det is a property of the map and det A = 0 forbids an inverse. |
| 04 | [Quadratic forms and positive definiteness](04-linear-algebra/03-inner-product-spaces/quadratic-forms-and-positive-definiteness.md) | Sylvester's law of inertia: the numbers of positive and negative squares survive every invertible change of variables. |
| 07 | [The Euler characteristic](07-topology/02-algebraic-topology/the-euler-characteristic.md) | V − E + F = 2 for every polyhedron homeomorphic to a sphere; χ with orientability separates the compact surfaces. |
| 08 | [Surfaces and Gaussian curvature](08-geometry/02-differential-geometry/surfaces-and-gaussian-curvature.md) | Gauss's theorema egregium: curvature is unchanged by bending without stretching, so no map of part of a sphere onto a plane preserves distances. |
| 10 | [Stability and phase portraits](10-differential-equations-and-dynamics/01-ordinary-differential-equations/stability-and-phase-portraits.md) | A conserved energy confines each orbit to a level set, and a Lyapunov function that decreases along orbits proves stability without solving. |

**When it misleads.** Equal invariants prove nothing unless the invariant is complete, and most are not. The torus and the Klein bottle both have χ = 0; the matrices with rows (0, 1), (0, 0) and the zero 2 × 2 matrix share determinant, trace and characteristic polynomial and are not similar; the Conway knot has Alexander polynomial 1, as the unknot does; the Poincaré homology sphere has the homology of S³ but a fundamental group of order 120. The opposite error is a quantity believed invariant that is not, because the class of allowed objects was wider than the proof assumed. Euler's V − E + F = 2 was "proved" for all polyhedra until counterexamples such as a polyhedral picture frame (χ = 0) appeared, the story Lakatos told in *Proofs and Refutations*; the formula is an invariant of the sphere, not of polyhedra. An invariant can also be correct and useless for a question: dimension is a complete invariant of finite-dimensional vector spaces and says nothing about a linear map between them. See [equivalence and quotients](#equivalence-and-quotients) and [impossibility and obstruction](#impossibility-and-obstruction).

## Symmetry

**Claim: the transformations that preserve an object form a group under composition, and the group explains the object: what it conserves, what can be solved, how its solutions look, and how many essentially different configurations it has.** The force is that the group axioms are exactly the properties of structure-preserving bijections: the composite of two is one, the identity is one, the inverse of one is one. So every object with structure has a symmetry group, and the group can be studied separately and brought back. Orbit counting, Klein's definition of a geometry, Galois's criterion for solving equations and Noether's link between symmetries and conservation laws are four uses of this one observation.

| Layer | Where it appears | How |
|---|---|---|
| 04 | [Inner products and orthogonality](04-linear-algebra/03-inner-product-spaces/inner-products-and-orthogonality.md) | The orthogonal and unitary groups are exactly the linear maps that preserve ⟨x, y⟩, and so preserve lengths and angles. |
| 05 | [Group actions](05-abstract-algebra/01-groups/group-actions.md) | Orbit–stabiliser, ∣G∣ = ∣orbit∣ · ∣stabiliser∣, and Burnside's lemma count necklaces and colourings up to rotation. |
| 05 | [Solvability by radicals](05-abstract-algebra/03-galois-theory/solvability-by-radicals.md) | Over a field of characteristic 0, a polynomial is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group is solvable; S₅ is not. |
| 08 | [The Erlangen programme](08-geometry/01-classical-geometry/the-erlangen-programme.md) | Klein: a geometry is a space with a group, and its theorems are the invariants of the group. |
| 08 | [Symmetry groups and tilings](08-geometry/01-classical-geometry/symmetry-groups-and-tilings.md) | There are exactly 7 frieze groups, 17 wallpaper groups and 230 crystallographic space groups. |
| 10 | [Noether's theorem](10-differential-equations-and-dynamics/03-calculus-of-variations/noethers-theorem.md) | Each continuous one-parameter symmetry of an action gives a conserved quantity: time translation gives energy, space translation momentum, rotation angular momentum. |
| 11 | [Bayesian inference](11-probability-and-statistics/04-statistical-inference/bayesian-inference.md) | De Finetti: an infinite sequence invariant under permutations is a mixture of independent and identically distributed sequences, which is what justifies a prior. |
| 12 | [Modular forms](12-number-theory-and-algebraic-geometry/04-elliptic-curves-and-modular-forms/modular-forms.md) | A modular form of weight k satisfies f((az + b)/(cz + d)) = (cz + d)ᵏ f(z) for every matrix in SL₂(ℤ), and this symmetry makes the space of such forms finite-dimensional. |

**When it misleads.** The classic error is a symmetry argument that assumes what it should prove. "The answer must be symmetric" presupposes that an answer exists and is unique; Steiner's symmetrisation shows that any non-circular region can be improved, which proves the circle is optimal only once a maximiser is known to exist. Even when solutions exist they need not be symmetric: the equation x′ = μx − x³ is unchanged by x ↦ −x, yet for μ > 0 its symmetric equilibrium 0 is unstable and the stable equilibria ±√μ break the symmetry, as a buckled beam bends to one side. Symmetry of a problem then forces only a symmetric set of solutions, here a pair. Different symmetry assumptions can give different answers: Bertrand's paradox asks for the probability that a "random chord" of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and three natural invariant choices give 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4. Noether's theorem needs a variational principle, so a dissipative system can be rotation-invariant and conserve nothing. De Finetti's theorem fails for finite exchangeable sequences, such as draws without replacement from an urn. See [existence and construction](#existence-and-construction).

## Duality

**Claim: many structures come in mirror pairs, linked by a pairing or an order-reversing correspondence, so that each theorem on one side has a twin on the other and every feasible object on one side bounds every feasible object on the other.** The force is the pairing ⟨·, ·⟩: V × V∗ → F of a space with its dual, where each object is tested against the objects of the other side. Weak duality, the inequality that any dual-feasible object bounds any primal-feasible one, is usually a one-line computation. Strong duality, that the best bounds meet, is a theorem with hypotheses, and when it holds an optimum comes with a certificate that it is optimal.

| Layer | Where it appears | How |
|---|---|---|
| 00 | [Predicate logic and quantifiers](00-logic-and-proof/01-formal-logic/predicate-logic-and-quantifiers.md) | Negation exchanges ∀ with ∃ and ∧ with ∨: ¬∀x P(x) ⇔ ∃x ¬P(x), so every law has a dual law. |
| 03 | [Matchings and network flows](03-combinatorics-and-graph-theory/02-graph-theory/matchings-and-network-flows.md) | The maximum flow equals the minimum cut capacity; in a bipartite graph the largest matching equals the smallest vertex cover (König). |
| 04 | [Dual spaces](04-linear-algebra/01-vector-spaces-and-linear-maps/dual-spaces.md) | V∗ is the space of linear functionals; annihilators reverse inclusions, the transpose reverses arrows, and V ≅ V∗∗ naturally in finite dimension. |
| 08 | [Projective geometry](08-geometry/01-classical-geometry/projective-geometry.md) | In the projective plane, exchanging "point" and "line" turns every theorem into another: Pascal's theorem becomes Brianchon's. |
| 08 | [Differential forms and de Rham cohomology](08-geometry/02-differential-geometry/differential-forms-and-de-rham-cohomology.md) | Stokes's theorem ∫_∂M ω = ∫_M dω makes the boundary operator on chains dual to the exterior derivative on forms. |
| 09 | [Fourier analysis on groups](09-advanced-analysis/04-harmonic-analysis/fourier-analysis-on-groups.md) | Pontryagin duality pairs a locally compact abelian group with its group of characters: the dual of ℤ is the circle, and ℝ is its own dual. |
| 12 | [The Riemann zeta function](12-number-theory-and-algebraic-geometry/02-analytic-number-theory/the-riemann-zeta-function.md) | The functional equation relates ζ(s) to ζ(1 − s), so the critical line Re s = ½ is the mirror. |
| 14 | [Linear programming](14-applied-and-computational-mathematics/02-optimisation/linear-programming.md) | If a linear programme has a finite optimum, so does its dual, with the same value; the dual solution certifies the primal one. |

**When it misleads.** Finite-dimensional duality is an unreliable guide to infinite dimensions. The algebraic dual of a vector space of countably infinite dimension has dimension 2^ℵ₀, so V and V∗∗ are not even isomorphic. For normed spaces the continuous dual behaves better but still not perfectly: the dual of c₀ is ℓ¹ and the dual of ℓ¹ is ℓ^∞, but the dual of ℓ^∞ is strictly larger than ℓ¹, so these spaces are not reflexive and arguments that pass to the double dual and back fail. Strong duality fails without its hypotheses: integer programmes and non-convex problems have duality gaps, and even convex programmes need a constraint qualification such as Slater's condition. Pontryagin duality requires a locally compact abelian group; a non-abelian group has no dual group, and its harmonic analysis needs whole representations in place of characters. Poincaré duality with integer coefficients needs a closed orientable manifold; for the Klein bottle it holds only with coefficients in ℤ/2. Projective duality is exact only in the projective plane: in the affine plane parallel lines have no intersection point to be dual to. Duality pairs naturally with [extremal principles and optimisation](#extremal-principles-and-optimisation), whose optima it certifies.

## Linearisation

**Claim: near a point, a differentiable map is its derivative plus an error smaller than first order, and when the derivative is non-degenerate the linear map's qualitative behaviour (invertibility, rank, stability) is also the nonlinear map's behaviour near that point.** The force is that linear algebra is the one theory understood completely: a linear map between finite-dimensional spaces is classified by rank, and an operator on one space by its Jordan form. So mathematics reduces to it whenever it can, and Taylor's theorem says how large the error is. The derivative, the tangent space, the Lie algebra, linear stability analysis, Newton's method and group representations are all the same move: replace a curved or non-commutative object by a linear one and control what is lost.

| Layer | Where it appears | How |
|---|---|---|
| 04 | [Eigenvalues and diagonalisation](04-linear-algebra/02-determinants-and-eigenvalues/eigenvalues-and-diagonalisation.md) | The eigenvalues of a linear map decide whether its iterates grow or decay, and so decide the stability of any system it linearises. |
| 05 | [Representations of finite groups](05-abstract-algebra/04-modules-and-representations/representations-of-finite-groups.md) | A group is studied through homomorphisms into matrix groups GL(V), turning group theory into linear algebra. |
| 06 | [The derivative as a linear map](06-real-analysis/04-calculus-of-several-variables/the-derivative-as-a-linear-map.md) | f(x + h) = f(x) + Df(x)h + o(∣h∣): the derivative is the best linear approximation, and the chain rule composes them. |
| 06 | [Inverse and implicit function theorems](06-real-analysis/04-calculus-of-several-variables/inverse-and-implicit-function-theorems.md) | If Df(a) is invertible, f has a smooth inverse near f(a): the linear verdict becomes the nonlinear one, locally. |
| 08 | [Lie groups and Lie algebras](08-geometry/04-lie-theory/lie-groups-and-lie-algebras.md) | A Lie group is replaced by its tangent space at the identity with the bracket [X, Y], and the exponential map carries information back. |
| 10 | [Stability and phase portraits](10-differential-equations-and-dynamics/01-ordinary-differential-equations/stability-and-phase-portraits.md) | Hartman–Grobman: near an equilibrium whose Jacobian has no eigenvalue with zero real part, the flow is topologically conjugate to the linear one. |
| 14 | [Root-finding and Newton's method](14-applied-and-computational-mathematics/01-numerical-analysis/root-finding-and-newtons-method.md) | xₙ₊₁ = xₙ − f(xₙ)/f′(xₙ) solves the linearisation at each step, and converges quadratically near a simple root. |
| 14 | [Mathematical biology and epidemics](14-applied-and-computational-mathematics/04-mathematical-modelling/mathematical-biology-and-epidemics.md) | The disease-free state is stable when the basic reproduction number R₀ is below 1 and unstable above it, a fact read from the Jacobian. |

**When it misleads.** Where the derivative is degenerate the linearisation decides nothing. The equations x′ = x³ and x′ = −x³ have the same linearisation, x′ = 0, at the origin, yet the first equilibrium is unstable and the second asymptotically stable; a linear centre can become a stable or unstable spiral under any nonlinear perturbation; bifurcations happen exactly at these degenerate points. Newton's method is only locally convergent: for f(x) = ∛x it gives xₙ₊₁ = −2xₙ and diverges from every non-zero start, and for x³ − 2x + 2 started at 0 it cycles 0, 1, 0, 1 forever. Linearisation is local by nature: exp: ℂ → ℂ ∖ {0} has non-zero derivative everywhere and is not injective, and SU(2) and SO(3) have the same Lie algebra but are different groups, one the double cover of the other. Freezing time also fails: Markus and Yamabe gave a matrix A(t) whose eigenvalues have negative real part for every t, while x′ = A(t)x has solutions growing like e^(t/2). In infinite dimensions an injective derivative with dense range need not be invertible, which is why some nonlinear problems need the Nash–Moser theorem. See [local to global](#local-to-global).

## Approximation and limits

**Claim: the exact objects of analysis (real numbers, sums of series, derivatives, integrals, solutions of equations) are defined as limits of approximations, so every statement about them is a statement that an error can be made smaller than any ε > 0, and the useful theorems say how fast.** The force is that most exact objects cannot be written down in finitely many symbols, while their approximations can. The ε–δ and ε–N definitions turn "arbitrarily close" into a statement with quantifiers that a finite proof can establish, and completeness guarantees the limit exists. The same structure governs numerical analysis (replace the limit by a finite computation and bound the difference), asymptotic number theory and the limit theorems of probability.

| 06 | [Sequences and limits](06-real-analysis/01-limits-and-convergence/sequences-and-limits.md) | aₙ → a means ∀ε > 0 ∃N ∀n ≥ N: ∣aₙ − a∣ < ε, the definition every later limit copies. |
| 06 | [Uniform convergence](06-real-analysis/01-limits-and-convergence/uniform-convergence.md) | Limits of continuous functions are continuous, and limits pass through integrals, when the convergence is uniform. |
| 09 | [The Lebesgue integral](09-advanced-analysis/01-measure-and-integration/the-lebesgue-integral.md) | The monotone and dominated convergence theorems say exactly when lim ∫ fₙ = ∫ lim fₙ. |
| 11 | [The central limit theorem](11-probability-and-statistics/02-limit-theorems/the-central-limit-theorem.md) | Standardised sums tend to the normal law, and Berry–Esseen bounds the error by C · ρ/(σ³√n), where ρ = E∣X − μ∣³. |

| 14 | [Numerical methods for differential equations](14-applied-and-computational-mathematics/01-numerical-analysis/numerical-methods-for-differential-equations.md) | Lax equivalence: for a consistent scheme approximating a well-posed linear problem, convergence holds if and only if the scheme is stable. |

**When it misleads.** Limits do not commute with each other or with other operations unless a theorem says so. The functions xⁿ on [0, 1] are continuous with a discontinuous pointwise limit; fₙ = n on (0, 1/n) and 0 elsewhere has ∫ fₙ = 1 for every n but tends pointwise to 0; Riemann's rearrangement theorem says a conditionally convergent series such as 1 − ½ + ⅓ − … can be rearranged to sum to any real number. Asymptotic statements mislead at every size anyone can compute: π(x) < li(x) for all x up to 10¹⁹, yet Littlewood proved the difference changes sign infinitely often, with a first crossing below about 1.4 × 10³¹⁶. Asymptotic series such as Stirling's diverge, so adding terms eventually makes the approximation worse. A consistent but unstable numerical scheme converges to nothing: round-off error is amplified at every step. Approximation without [completeness and compactness](#completeness-and-compactness) may approach a hole where the limit should be.

## Completeness and compactness

**Claim: completeness (every Cauchy sequence converges, so there are no holes) and compactness (every sequence has a convergent subsequence, or every open cover a finite subcover, so nothing escapes to infinity) are the two properties that turn approximate solutions into exact ones; the compactness theorem of first-order logic is the same mechanism for consistency.** The force is that existence proofs in analysis build a sequence of better and better approximate solutions. If the sequence is Cauchy, completeness supplies the limit; if it is merely bounded, compactness supplies a limit point; either way something then has to show the limit solves the problem. In logic, a set of sentences each of whose finite subsets has a model has a model, and the proof is a compactness argument in disguise (Tychonoff's theorem for a product of two-point spaces).

|---|---|---|
| 00 | [Soundness, completeness and compactness](00-logic-and-proof/01-formal-logic/soundness-completeness-and-compactness.md) | Gödel's completeness theorem gives the compactness theorem: finitely satisfiable sets of first-order sentences are satisfiable. |
| 02 | [The real numbers](02-number-systems/01-constructing-the-numbers/the-real-numbers.md) | ℝ has the least-upper-bound property; ℚ does not, since {q ∈ ℚ : q² < 2} has no rational supremum. |
| 03 | [Ramsey theory](03-combinatorics-and-graph-theory/04-extremal-combinatorics/ramsey-theory.md) | The infinite Ramsey theorem implies the finite one by a compactness argument. |
| 06 | [Completeness and the contraction mapping principle](06-real-analysis/03-metric-spaces/completeness-and-the-contraction-mapping-principle.md) | A contraction of a complete metric space has exactly one fixed point, the limit of its iterates. |
| 07 | [Compactness](07-topology/01-point-set-topology/compactness.md) | Finite subcovers turn local bounds into global ones, and Tychonoff's theorem makes arbitrary products of compact spaces compact. |
| 09 | [Lᵖ spaces](09-advanced-analysis/01-measure-and-integration/lp-spaces.md) | Riesz–Fischer: Lᵖ is complete, where Riemann-integrable functions under the same norm were not. |
| 10 | [Existence of minimisers](10-differential-equations-and-dynamics/03-calculus-of-variations/existence-of-minimisers.md) | The direct method: a bounded minimising sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence, and lower semicontinuity makes its limit a minimiser. |
| 12 | [p-adic numbers and local fields](12-number-theory-and-algebraic-geometry/01-algebraic-number-theory/p-adic-numbers-and-local-fields.md) | ℚₚ is the completion of ℚ for the p-adic absolute value, and its ring of integers ℤₚ is compact. |

**When it misleads.** Finite-dimensional intuition is the main trap. In ℝⁿ closed and bounded means compact (Heine–Borel); in an infinite-dimensional normed space the closed unit ball is never compact (Riesz's lemma), and the orthonormal vectors e₁, e₂, … of ℓ² are bounded with no convergent subsequence, since ∣eₘ − eₙ∣ = √2. Weak compactness rescues existence but only weak limits, and nonlinear terms need not pass to weak limits: oscillating sequences such as sin(nx) converge weakly to 0 while sin²(nx) converges weakly to ½. Without lower semicontinuity the infimum may not be attained, as in Weierstrass's example of minimising ∫ x²f′(x)² dx over [−1, 1] with f(−1) = −1 and f(1) = 1, whose infimum 0 is approached but never reached. Completeness is a metric property, not a topological one: (0, 1) is homeomorphic to ℝ but not complete. Compactness arguments prove existence and give no bounds: the compactness proof of the finite Ramsey theorem yields no Ramsey number, and the Paris–Harrington variant is true but unprovable in Peano arithmetic. See [existence and construction](#existence-and-construction).

## Local to global

**Claim: many objects are defined or understood locally (near each point, at each prime, on each finite subset), and the global question is whether the local pieces fit together; when they do not, the obstruction to gluing is itself a computable object, often a cohomology group.** The force is that local questions are easier: near a point a manifold is ℝⁿ and a smooth map is linear; modulo a prime, arithmetic is finite. Global facts are what one wants. Between them sit compactness (finitely many local pieces suffice), the mean value theorem (a pointwise sign controls an interval), analytic continuation (a germ determines a function), and cohomology (which measures exactly how locally consistent data fail to come from one global object).

|---|---|---|
| 02 | [Modular arithmetic](02-number-systems/02-elementary-number-theory/modular-arithmetic.md) | The Chinese remainder theorem: ℤ/mn ≅ ℤ/m × ℤ/n for coprime m, n, so compatible residues glue to one integer. |
| 06 | [The derivative and the mean value theorem](06-real-analysis/02-calculus-of-one-variable/the-derivative-and-the-mean-value-theorem.md) | f′ > 0 at every point of an interval forces f to be increasing on the whole interval. |
| 06 | [Vector calculus and the integral theorems](06-real-analysis/04-calculus-of-several-variables/vector-calculus-and-the-integral-theorems.md) | A curl-free field is a gradient on a simply connected domain, but (−y, x)/(x² + y²) is curl-free on ℝ² ∖ {0} with circulation 2π round the hole. |
| 07 | [Covering spaces](07-topology/02-algebraic-topology/covering-spaces.md) | A covering is a product over each small open set, yet globally twisted, and the twist is recorded by the fundamental group. |
| 08 | [The Gauss–Bonnet theorem](08-geometry/02-differential-geometry/the-gauss-bonnet-theorem.md) | For a compact surface without boundary, ∫ K dA = 2πχ: local curvature integrates to a global topological number. |
| 09 | [Analytic continuation and Riemann surfaces](09-advanced-analysis/02-complex-analysis/analytic-continuation-and-riemann-surfaces.md) | A holomorphic germ determines its continuation along every path, and log z and √z show the result may depend on the path. |
| 12 | [p-adic numbers and local fields](12-number-theory-and-algebraic-geometry/01-algebraic-number-theory/p-adic-numbers-and-local-fields.md) | Hasse–Minkowski: a quadratic form over ℚ has a non-trivial zero if and only if it has one over ℝ and over every ℚₚ. |
| 12 | [Elliptic curves](12-number-theory-and-algebraic-geometry/04-elliptic-curves-and-modular-forms/elliptic-curves.md) | The Tate–Shafarevich group Ш collects the curves of genus one that have points everywhere locally and no rational point. |

**When it misleads.** Local data can be everywhere consistent and globally impossible. Selmer's cubic 3x³ + 4y³ + 5z³ = 0 has non-trivial solutions over ℝ and over every ℚₚ but none over ℚ, so the Hasse principle that holds for quadratic forms fails for cubics. The Möbius band is locally a product of an interval and a segment and is not globally one; the Penrose triangle is a picture whose every corner is a consistent drawing of a solid. The rigidity of analytic continuation is special to holomorphic and real-analytic functions: the smooth function equal to e^(−1/x²) for x > 0 and to 0 for x ≤ 0 agrees with 0 on a whole half-line and is not 0, so smooth functions are not determined by germs, which is exactly what makes bump functions and partitions of unity possible. Local invertibility is not global: an everywhere non-singular derivative does not give an injective map, as exp on ℂ shows. And the local pieces may be inadequate: a Lie algebra determines its connected Lie group only up to coverings. [Linearisation](#linearisation) is the local half of this principle, and [compactness](#completeness-and-compactness) is often the bridge.

## Decomposition and classification

**Claim: break an object into pieces that cannot be broken further, prove the decomposition unique in a suitable sense, and list the indecomposable pieces; the object is then described by which pieces occur and how they are assembled.** The force is that a classification must be a finite description of an infinite variety, and unique factorisation reduces the variety to a list plus multiplicities. Integers factor into primes; finite groups have composition series with simple factors; matrices over ℂ split into Jordan blocks; representations of finite groups over ℂ split into irreducibles; compact surfaces are connected sums of a few basic ones; functions split into Fourier modes. The hard part is usually the list, and the second hard part is the assembly.

|---|---|---|
| 02 | [Primes and unique factorisation](02-number-systems/02-elementary-number-theory/primes-and-unique-factorisation.md) | Every integer greater than 1 is a product of primes, uniquely up to order, because a prime dividing ab divides a or b. |
| 04 | [Jordan normal form](04-linear-algebra/02-determinants-and-eigenvalues/jordan-normal-form.md) | Over an algebraically closed field every square matrix is similar to a block-diagonal matrix of Jordan blocks, unique up to order: a complete classification. |
| 05 | [Composition series and simple groups](05-abstract-algebra/01-groups/composition-series-and-simple-groups.md) | Jordan–Hölder makes the simple factors unique; the finite simple groups are the cyclic groups of prime order, the alternating groups Aₙ for n ≥ 5, the groups of Lie type and 26 sporadic groups. |
| 05 | [Representations of finite groups](05-abstract-algebra/04-modules-and-representations/representations-of-finite-groups.md) | Maschke: every complex representation is a direct sum of irreducibles, and there are as many irreducibles as conjugacy classes. |
| 07 | [Classification of surfaces](07-topology/03-manifolds/classification-of-surfaces.md) | Every compact connected surface is a sphere, a connected sum of tori, or a connected sum of projective planes. |
| 08 | [The classification of simple Lie algebras](08-geometry/04-lie-theory/the-classification-of-simple-lie-algebras.md) | The simple complex Lie algebras are the four families Aₙ, Bₙ, Cₙ, Dₙ and the five exceptions E₆, E₇, E₈, F₄, G₂, read off Dynkin diagrams. |
| 09 | [Spectral theory](09-advanced-analysis/03-functional-analysis/spectral-theory.md) | A compact self-adjoint operator on a Hilbert space is diagonal in an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. |
| 12 | [Algebraic curves and Riemann–Roch](12-number-theory-and-algebraic-geometry/03-algebraic-geometry/algebraic-curves-and-riemann-roch.md) | Smooth projective curves are sorted first by genus, and Riemann–Roch computes what each genus allows. |

**When it misleads.** Decomposition can exist without uniqueness. In ℤ[√−5], 6 = 2 · 3 = (1 + √−5)(1 − √−5), with all four factors irreducible and no two associate; the class group measures the failure. Maschke's theorem needs the characteristic of the field not to divide ∣G∣, and in modular representation theory there are indecomposable representations that are not irreducible. The Jordan form needs an algebraically closed field and is numerically useless, since an arbitrarily small perturbation of a Jordan block makes it diagonalisable. Knowing the pieces does not determine the object: ℤ/4 and ℤ/2 × ℤ/2 have the same composition factors, and the extension problem of assembling simple groups is far from solved. Some classifications are impossible: every finitely presented group is the fundamental group of a closed 4-manifold, so because isomorphism of finitely presented groups is undecidable, no algorithm classifies 4-manifolds. The more general the class, the less hope of a list; see [abstraction and generalisation](#abstraction-and-generalisation).

## Induction and recursion

**Claim: to prove a statement for every element of a well-founded structure, it suffices to prove it for each element assuming it for all smaller ones; to define a function on such a structure, it suffices to say how each value is computed from values at smaller elements.** The force is well-foundedness: a relation with no infinite descending chain has a least counterexample to any false claim, and the step shows there is none. The natural numbers are the prototype, formulas and proofs (defined by recursion on their construction) the logician's case, and the ordinals the general one. Proof by induction and definition by recursion are the same theorem seen from two sides, and infinite descent is induction read backwards.

| 00 | [Formal proof systems](00-logic-and-proof/01-formal-logic/formal-proof-systems.md) | Formulas and derivations are defined recursively, and soundness is proved by induction on the length of a derivation. |
| 01 | [Ordinal numbers and transfinite induction](01-sets-and-foundations/02-infinity-and-cardinality/ordinal-numbers-and-transfinite-induction.md) | Induction along any well-order, with a separate step at limit ordinals. |
| 02 | [Natural numbers and the Peano axioms](02-number-systems/01-constructing-the-numbers/natural-numbers-and-the-peano-axioms.md) | The induction axiom characterises ℕ, and the recursion theorem defines + and × from the successor function. |
| 02 | [Divisibility and the Euclidean algorithm](02-number-systems/02-elementary-number-theory/divisibility-and-the-euclidean-algorithm.md) | gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a mod b) is a recursion that terminates because the second argument strictly decreases. |
| 03 | [Recurrence relations](03-combinatorics-and-graph-theory/01-enumeration/recurrence-relations.md) | A recursive description of a set of objects becomes a recurrence for their number, such as Fₙ₊₂ = Fₙ₊₁ + Fₙ. |
| 03 | [Trees](03-combinatorics-and-graph-theory/02-graph-theory/trees.md) | Removing a leaf leaves a smaller tree, so induction shows a tree on n vertices has n − 1 edges. |
| 13 | [Proof theory and reverse mathematics](13-structure-and-metamathematics/02-computability-and-incompleteness/proof-theory-and-reverse-mathematics.md) | Gentzen proved Peano arithmetic consistent using transfinite induction up to the ordinal ε₀, which Peano arithmetic cannot prove. |

**When it misleads.** The step must hold at every size, including the smallest transition. The "proof" that all horses have the same colour uses two overlapping groups of n horses, which fails when n + 1 = 2 and the groups do not overlap. Induction needs a well-founded order: the integers and the positive rationals under ≤ do not have one, and a descent argument on them proves nothing. Induction verifies a formula but does not find it, and a statement too weak to carry its own step must first be strengthened; choosing the strengthening is the creative part. Recursion may define nothing if termination is not proved: whether the Collatz map n ↦ n/2 or 3n + 1 always reaches 1 is open. Total computable functions can outgrow every primitive recursion (the Ackermann function), and true statements about recursion can be unprovable in the induction that seems natural for them: Goodstein's theorem holds but is unprovable in Peano arithmetic, as Kirby and Paris showed. At limit ordinals transfinite induction needs its own argument, which is where most transfinite proofs actually do their work. See [extremal principles and optimisation](#extremal-principles-and-optimisation), whose least counterexample is induction in disguise.

## Existence and construction

**Claim: proving that an object exists and producing it are different achievements; arguments by contradiction, choice, compactness, counting or probability settle existence while giving no instance, and constructive proofs give an algorithm or a formula but demand more.** The force is that classical logic makes existence cheap: the law of the excluded middle lets a proof of "not every x fails" stand for a proof of "some x succeeds", and the axiom of choice supplies objects no rule describes. Constructive logic, computability theory and numerical practice make existence expensive again, and the distance between the two is a measurable thing: reverse mathematics asks exactly which axioms each existence theorem needs.

|---|---|---|

| 01 | [The axiom of choice](01-sets-and-foundations/03-axiomatic-set-theory/the-axiom-of-choice.md) | Choice gives a basis of ℝ over ℚ, a well-order of ℝ and a non-measurable set, none of which can be explicitly defined. |
| 03 | [The probabilistic method](03-combinatorics-and-graph-theory/03-discrete-probability/the-probabilistic-method.md) | A random 2-colouring of the edges of the complete graph shows the Ramsey number R(k, k) exceeds 2^(k/2) for k ≥ 3; no explicit colouring comes close. |
| 06 | [Completeness and the contraction mapping principle](06-real-analysis/03-metric-spaces/completeness-and-the-contraction-mapping-principle.md) | Iterating a contraction constructs its fixed point, with an explicit bound on the error after n steps. |
| 07 | [Fixed-point theorems](07-topology/02-algebraic-topology/fixed-point-theorems.md) | Brouwer: every continuous map of a closed disc to itself has a fixed point, and the proof does not locate it. |
| 10 | [Existence and uniqueness](10-differential-equations-and-dynamics/01-ordinary-differential-equations/existence-and-uniqueness.md) | Picard iteration builds the solution it proves exists; under continuity alone, Peano's theorem gives existence without uniqueness, as x′ = 3x^(2/3), x(0) = 0 has both 0 and t³. |
| 14 | [Game theory](14-applied-and-computational-mathematics/05-games-decisions-and-finance/game-theory.md) | Nash proved by a fixed-point theorem that every finite game has an equilibrium in mixed strategies; computing one is believed intractable in general. |
| 15 | [Philosophy of mathematics](15-mathematics-in-context/philosophy-of-mathematics.md) | Brouwer's intuitionism rejected existence without construction, and the debate shaped what counts as a proof. |

**When it misleads.** Non-constructive existence can deliver monsters. Choice gives the Banach–Tarski decomposition of a ball into five pieces that reassemble into two balls of the same size, and a Hamel basis of ℝ over ℚ yields additive functions f(x + y) = f(x) + f(y) that are not continuous, and whose graphs are dense in the plane. Worse is a proof that assumes the existence it should establish. Perron's paradox "proves" that 1 is the largest positive integer: if N is the largest, then N² ≤ N, so N = 1. Dirichlet's principle assumed that the energy integral has a minimiser, and Weierstrass's counterexample forced the modern theory of weak solutions. A construction is not the same as a feasible one: brute-force search decides any finite Ramsey question in principle and none of the interesting ones in practice, and a Nash equilibrium exists for every finite game without any known efficient way to find one. Existence in one model of set theory need not be definable in another: whether a definable well-order of ℝ exists is independent of the usual axioms. See [symmetry](#symmetry) and [extremal principles](#extremal-principles-and-optimisation), whose arguments both presuppose existence.

## Infinity and size

**Claim: "how big is this set?" has several exact answers (cardinality, measure, dimension, Baire category, growth rate, Vapnik–Chervonenkis dimension), each invariant under a different class of maps, and a set can be large by one measure and negligible by another.** The force is that each notion of size is the invariant of some equivalence: cardinality is preserved by bijections, measure by measure-preserving maps, Hausdorff dimension by bi-Lipschitz maps, category by homeomorphisms. Since these classes differ, so do the verdicts. The Cantor set is uncountable but has length zero; ℚ is dense but countable, null and meagre; almost every real number in the sense of measure belongs to a set that is small in the sense of category.

| 01 | [Cardinal numbers](01-sets-and-foundations/02-infinity-and-cardinality/cardinal-numbers.md) | ℵ₀ < 2^ℵ₀, and whether any cardinal lies strictly between them is the continuum hypothesis. |
| 09 | [Measure and σ-algebras](09-advanced-analysis/01-measure-and-integration/measure-and-sigma-algebras.md) | Every countable set has Lebesgue measure zero, and so does the uncountable Cantor set. |
| 09 | [Hausdorff dimension and fractals](09-advanced-analysis/01-measure-and-integration/hausdorff-dimension-and-fractals.md) | The Cantor set has dimension log 2/log 3 ≈ 0.63, a size that neither cardinality nor measure sees. |
| 09 | [Hahn–Banach and Baire category](09-advanced-analysis/03-functional-analysis/hahn-banach-and-baire-category.md) | A complete metric space is not a countable union of nowhere dense sets, so "generic" has a meaning: most continuous functions are nowhere differentiable. |
| 13 | [Models and the Löwenheim–Skolem theorems](13-structure-and-metamathematics/03-model-theory-and-independence/models-and-the-lowenheim-skolem-theorems.md) | A first-order theory with an infinite model has models of every infinite cardinality, so first-order logic cannot pin down the size of an infinite structure. |
| 14 | [Statistical learning theory](14-applied-and-computational-mathematics/06-data-and-learning/statistical-learning-theory.md) | The Vapnik–Chervonenkis dimension measures the effective size of an infinite class of classifiers, and controls how much data learning needs. |

**When it misleads.** The verdicts conflict, so "almost all" means nothing until the notion of size is named. ℝ is the union of a set of measure zero and a meagre set (Oxtoby): almost every real number is normal in base 10 in the sense of measure, while the non-normal numbers form a comeagre set. Finite intuition that a proper part is smaller than the whole fails for every infinite set, as Hilbert's hotel shows. Continuous surjections do not preserve dimension: a space-filling curve maps [0, 1] onto the square [0, 1]², and only for homeomorphisms does invariance of domain restore it. Probability zero is not impossibility: each point of [0, 1] has probability zero under the uniform law, yet one of them occurs. Cardinality questions can be undecidable from the usual axioms: the continuum hypothesis is independent of Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory with the axiom of choice (ZFC). And size is relative to the model: by Skolem's paradox, if ZFC has a model it has a countable one, in which the set that the model calls ℝ is uncountable from inside and countable from outside. See [randomness and averaging](#randomness-and-averaging), which depends on measure and not on cardinality.

## Randomness and averaging

**Claim: the average of many independent, or weakly dependent, random quantities is close to its expectation with probability exponentially near 1, so randomness in the parts produces near-certainty in the whole; and a quantity whose average is large must somewhere be large.** The force is arithmetic. For independent terms the variances add, so the standard deviation of a mean of n terms shrinks like 1/√n; for bounded terms, exponential moments give exponential tails. Hoeffding's inequality says that for independent Xᵢ with values in [0, 1] and mean μ, P(∣X̄ − μ∣ ≥ t) ≤ 2 exp(−2nt²). The second half, the averaging principle, needs no independence at all: if the expected number of monochromatic cliques is less than 1, some colouring has none.

| 14 | [High-dimensional geometry and random matrices](14-applied-and-computational-mathematics/06-data-and-learning/high-dimensional-geometry-and-random-matrices.md) | Concentration of measure: Lipschitz functions on a high-dimensional sphere are nearly constant, and Johnson–Lindenstrauss projects n points into O(ε⁻² log n) dimensions with distortion at most 1 ± ε. |

**When it misleads.** Every hypothesis matters. Without a finite mean there is no law of large numbers: the mean of n independent standard Cauchy variables is again standard Cauchy, so it never settles. Strong dependence defeats averaging: a thousand measurements that share one systematic error average to that error. The ergodic theorem needs ergodicity: on a non-ergodic system, time averages converge to different limits on different invariant pieces, and one long run sees only the piece it started in. Monte Carlo needs finite variance, and importance sampling with a poorly chosen proposal can make the variance infinite while each estimate still looks plausible. The rate 1/√n is slow: one more decimal digit costs a hundred times as many samples. Heuristic randomness is not randomness: Cramér's model of the primes as random with density 1/log n predicts many statistics correctly, yet Maier showed it predicts the wrong count of primes in certain short intervals. See [infinity and size](#infinity-and-size) for what "almost surely" does not exclude.

## Extremal principles and optimisation

**Claim: an object that is extreme in some measurable way (the least counterexample, the largest set avoiding a pattern, the shortest path, the path of stationary action) has properties that ordinary objects lack, and first-order conditions turn "best" into an equation that can be solved.** The force is that at an interior optimum every allowed small variation changes the objective by zero to first order, which gives an equation (f′ = 0, ∇f = λ∇g, the Euler–Lagrange equation); and at a least counterexample every smaller case already works, which gives a contradiction. The same pattern underlies the Rayleigh quotient for eigenvalues, Turán's theorem, the principle of least action, maximum likelihood and the training of a neural network.

| Layer | Where it appears | How |
|---|---|---|
| 03 | [Extremal graph theory](03-combinatorics-and-graph-theory/04-extremal-combinatorics/extremal-graph-theory.md) | Mantel: a triangle-free graph on n vertices has at most ⌊n²/4⌋ edges, and the balanced complete bipartite graph is the unique extremal graph. |
| 03 | [Posets and extremal set theory](03-combinatorics-and-graph-theory/04-extremal-combinatorics/posets-and-extremal-set-theory.md) | Sperner: the largest family of subsets of an n-set with none containing another has C(n, ⌊n/2⌋) members. |
| 04 | [The spectral theorem](04-linear-algebra/03-inner-product-spaces/the-spectral-theorem.md) | For a symmetric matrix A, the largest eigenvalue is the maximum of ⟨Ax, x⟩ over unit vectors x, and Courant–Fischer gives the others as min–max values. |
| 06 | [Extrema and Lagrange multipliers](06-real-analysis/04-calculus-of-several-variables/extrema-and-lagrange-multipliers.md) | At a constrained extremum with ∇g ≠ 0, ∇f = λ∇g: the gradient of the objective is normal to the constraint surface. |
| 08 | [Lattices and sphere packing](08-geometry/03-convex-and-discrete-geometry/lattices-and-sphere-packing.md) | The E₈ lattice and the Leech lattice give the densest sphere packings in dimensions 8 and 24, proved by Viazovska and coauthors in 2016–2017. |
| 10 | [The Euler–Lagrange equation](10-differential-equations-and-dynamics/03-calculus-of-variations/the-euler-lagrange-equation.md) | Stationary action turns Newton's laws, geodesics and minimal surfaces into one differential equation. |
| 11 | [Estimation and likelihood](11-probability-and-statistics/04-statistical-inference/estimation-and-likelihood.md) | The maximum likelihood estimator chooses the parameter that makes the observed data most probable, and least squares is the Gaussian case. |
| 14 | [Convex optimisation and duality](14-applied-and-computational-mathematics/02-optimisation/convex-optimisation-and-duality.md) | For a convex objective on a convex set every local minimum is global, so first-order conditions are also sufficient. |

**When it misleads.** The extremal object may not exist, and an argument that assumes it does can prove anything, as Perron's "proof" that 1 is the largest integer shows. Steiner's proof of the isoperimetric inequality and Dirichlet's principle both assumed a maximiser or minimiser; Weierstrass's example, minimising ∫ x²f′(x)² dx over [−1, 1] with f(−1) = −1 and f(1) = 1, has infimum 0 and no minimiser. First-order conditions find stationary points, not optima: the "least" action is often only stationary, and a great-circle arc on a sphere stops being the shortest path once it passes the antipodal point. Lagrange multipliers need ∇g ≠ 0 at the point, and a minimum at a cusp of the constraint set can be missed. Outside convex problems, gradient methods find local minima and saddle points, with no certificate of global optimality. Extremal arguments in combinatorics can give the right extremal number with the wrong extremal structure when there are many near-optimal configurations. See [completeness and compactness](#completeness-and-compactness), which is how existence of the extremum is usually secured, and [duality](#duality), which certifies it.

## Dictionaries between fields

**Claim: a correspondence that translates the objects and questions of one field into those of another, while preserving enough structure that theorems travel with them, lets a problem hard in one language be solved in the other; the strongest dictionaries are equivalences of categories or order-reversing bijections.** The force is that different fields have developed different tools. Algebra can count and compute, analysis can estimate, geometry can be pictured, logic can be mechanised. A faithful translation imports all of one side's tools to the other at once, and an inclusion-reversing correspondence (subgroups to fields, ideals to varieties) turns a question about one lattice into a question about another. Several of the largest results of the last century, including Fermat's last theorem, were proved by building such a dictionary.

| Layer | Where it appears | How |
|---|---|---|
| 00 | [Constructive and intuitionistic logic](00-logic-and-proof/03-the-axiomatic-method/constructive-and-intuitionistic-logic.md) | Curry–Howard: propositions correspond to types and constructive proofs to programs, so a proof checker is a type checker. |
| 03 | [Generating functions](03-combinatorics-and-graph-theory/01-enumeration/generating-functions.md) | A sequence becomes a power series; disjoint union becomes addition and pairing becomes multiplication, so counting becomes algebra and analysis. |
| 05 | [The Galois correspondence](05-abstract-algebra/03-galois-theory/the-galois-correspondence.md) | For a finite Galois extension, intermediate fields correspond to subgroups of the Galois group, with inclusions reversed. |
| 07 | [Covering spaces](07-topology/02-algebraic-topology/covering-spaces.md) | For a connected, locally path-connected, semi-locally simply connected space, connected covers correspond to conjugacy classes of subgroups of π₁, as field extensions correspond to subgroups. |
| 09 | [Analytic continuation and Riemann surfaces](09-advanced-analysis/02-complex-analysis/analytic-continuation-and-riemann-surfaces.md) | Compact Riemann surfaces are the same objects as smooth projective algebraic curves over ℂ. |
| 10 | [The heat equation](10-differential-equations-and-dynamics/02-partial-differential-equations/the-heat-equation.md) | The solution of ∂u/∂t = ½Δu is the expected value of the initial data at the end of a Brownian path, so diffusion is a random walk. |
| 12 | [Varieties and the Nullstellensatz](12-number-theory-and-algebraic-geometry/03-algebraic-geometry/varieties-and-the-nullstellensatz.md) | Over an algebraically closed field, radical ideals of k[x₁, …, xₙ] correspond to affine varieties, with inclusions reversed. |
| 12 | [Fermat's last theorem](12-number-theory-and-algebraic-geometry/04-elliptic-curves-and-modular-forms/fermats-last-theorem.md) | A solution of aᵖ + bᵖ = cᵖ gives an elliptic curve that cannot be modular, and Wiles proved that such curves are modular. |

**When it misleads.** Every dictionary has a domain of validity, and translating outside it gives nonsense. The Nullstellensatz needs an algebraically closed field: over ℝ the proper ideal (x² + 1) has empty zero set, so the ideal–variety correspondence collapses. The Galois correspondence needs the extension to be normal and separable, and the covering correspondence needs the local hypotheses on the space; without them some subgroups have no matching object. A dictionary can be conjectural or ineffective: most of the Langlands correspondence is still conjecture, and a translation that exists in principle may give no algorithm. The most tempting failure is an analogy treated as a dictionary. Number fields and function fields of curves over finite fields share much structure, and the Riemann hypothesis was proved for the second (Weil for curves, Deligne in general), yet the proof does not transfer. Matrices and operators on infinite-dimensional spaces look alike, but the right shift on ℓ² has no eigenvalues at all, and the position operator of quantum mechanics is unbounded and has no eigenvectors in L²(ℝ). See [structure and morphism](#structure-and-morphism) for what a dictionary must preserve.

## Impossibility and obstruction

**Claim: to prove that something cannot be done, describe everything that could be done, find a property all of it shares (an invariant, a degree, a parity, a count, a diagonal) and show that the target lacks it; a sharp impossibility result also says exactly which hypothesis must be dropped to make the task possible.** The force is that an impossibility claim quantifies over all procedures, infinitely many, and no search can establish it; only a structural property can control them all. Irrationality, the unsolvability of the quintic, the three Greek construction problems, the halting problem, incompleteness and Arrow's theorem all follow this design: a precise description of the means, an invariant of those means, and a target outside its range.

| Layer | Where it appears | How |
|---|---|---|
| 00 | [Consistency and independence](00-logic-and-proof/03-the-axiomatic-method/consistency-and-independence.md) | A model in which an axiom fails shows that no proof of it from the others can exist; non-Euclidean geometry settled the parallel postulate this way. |
| 01 | [Cantor's diagonal argument](01-sets-and-foundations/02-infinity-and-cardinality/cantors-diagonal-argument.md) | Given any f: X → P(X), the set {x : x ∉ f(x)} is not in the image, the template for later impossibility proofs. |
| 02 | [Algebraic and transcendental numbers](02-number-systems/01-constructing-the-numbers/algebraic-and-transcendental-numbers.md) | √2 is irrational, and e and π satisfy no polynomial equation with integer coefficients (Hermite, Lindemann). |
| 05 | [Ruler-and-compass constructions](05-abstract-algebra/03-galois-theory/ruler-and-compass-constructions.md) | Constructible numbers have degree a power of 2 over ℚ; ∛2 and cos 20°, a root of 8x³ − 6x − 1, have degree 3, so the cube cannot be doubled nor 60° trisected. |
| 07 | [Fixed-point theorems](07-topology/02-algebraic-topology/fixed-point-theorems.md) | A retraction of the disc onto its boundary circle would give a homomorphism from the trivial group onto π₁(S¹) = ℤ, so none exists. |
| 13 | [Undecidable problems](13-structure-and-metamathematics/02-computability-and-incompleteness/undecidable-problems.md) | No program decides whether an arbitrary program halts, and so (Matiyasevich) none decides whether a polynomial equation has integer solutions. |
| 13 | [Gödel's incompleteness theorems](13-structure-and-metamathematics/02-computability-and-incompleteness/godels-incompleteness-theorems.md) | Every consistent, effectively axiomatised theory containing enough arithmetic has true sentences it cannot prove, including its own consistency. |
| 14 | [Social choice and voting](14-applied-and-computational-mathematics/05-games-decisions-and-finance/social-choice-and-voting.md) | Arrow: with three or more alternatives, every rule that aggregates rankings and satisfies unanimity and independence of irrelevant alternatives is a dictatorship. |

**When it misleads.** An impossibility result is relative to precisely stated means, and is routinely misquoted without them. An angle can be trisected with a marked ruler or by paper folding; the general quintic can be solved with elliptic modular functions (Hermite), so Abel–Ruffini rules out only formulas in radicals. Undecidability applies to the general problem, not to restricted classes: Presburger arithmetic (the natural numbers with addition only) and the theory of real closed fields are decidable. Incompleteness needs all its hypotheses: the set of all true sentences of arithmetic is a complete consistent theory, but it is not effectively axiomatisable, and the theorems say nothing about whether mathematics is inconsistent or unknowable. Arrow's theorem concerns rules that aggregate rankings; rules that use scores escape its hypotheses, though Gibbard's theorem still makes every non-dictatorial deterministic rule with three or more outcomes open to strategic voting. The opposite error is to confuse failure to find with impossibility: no proof is known that e + π is irrational, and that is not evidence that it is rational. See [invariants](#invariants), the usual tool, and [existence and construction](#existence-and-construction), its mirror.

## Principles that pull against each other

These principles are not a checklist to apply all at once. Several pull in opposite directions, and many hard choices of method, and many famous errors, come from standing too close to one side of a pair.

| Tension | Why they conflict | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|
| [Abstraction and generalisation](#abstraction-and-generalisation) vs [decomposition and classification](#decomposition-and-classification) | The wider the class a theorem covers, the less it can say about each member, and the less hope of listing them. | Finite simple groups are classified, finitely presented groups cannot be; compact surfaces are listed, 4-manifolds cannot be. |
| [Existence and construction](#existence-and-construction) vs [extremal principles and optimisation](#extremal-principles-and-optimisation) | Extremal arguments are powerful only once the extremum is known to exist, and securing that is often the whole difficulty. | Dirichlet's principle; Steiner's isoperimetric proof; Perron's paradox; the direct method of the calculus of variations. |
| [Symmetry](#symmetry) vs [existence and construction](#existence-and-construction) | Symmetry identifies the answer if there is a unique one, and says nothing if there is none or several. | Pitchfork bifurcations and buckling; Bertrand's paradox; Noether's theorem in dissipative systems. |
| [Linearisation](#linearisation) vs [local to global](#local-to-global) | The derivative is a local object, and global behaviour can differ from every local picture. | exp on ℂ is locally invertible and not injective; Hartman–Grobman holds only near the equilibrium; SU(2) and SO(3) share a Lie algebra. |
| [Approximation and limits](#approximation-and-limits) vs [completeness and compactness](#completeness-and-compactness) | Approximations converge only where completeness supplies a limit, and in infinite dimensions compactness often supplies only a weak one. | Minimising sequences with no minimiser; weak solutions of partial differential equations; Galerkin methods. |
| [Equivalence and quotients](#equivalence-and-quotients) vs [invariants](#invariants) | A coarse equivalence has simple complete invariants; a fine one is rarely classifiable by computable invariants. | Homotopy equivalence against homeomorphism; knot polynomials; similarity of matrices against unitary equivalence. |
| [Randomness and averaging](#randomness-and-averaging) vs [infinity and size](#infinity-and-size) | "Almost every" in measure and "generic" in category disagree, so a typical object depends on how typicality is measured. | Normal numbers; nowhere-differentiable functions; Oxtoby's splitting of ℝ into a null set and a meagre set. |
| [Structure and morphism](#structure-and-morphism) vs [equivalence and quotients](#equivalence-and-quotients) | Identifying isomorphic objects forgets which isomorphism was used, and some arguments need it. | V ≅ V∗ without a natural map; the algebraic closure unique up to non-unique isomorphism; univalence in homotopy type theory. |

[Impossibility and obstruction](#impossibility-and-obstruction) referees most of these: each tension is a place where a hypothesis cannot be dropped, and the counterexample that shows it is itself an impossibility result. [Dictionaries between fields](#dictionaries-between-fields) are how mathematics escapes a tension it cannot resolve on its own ground, by moving the problem to a field where the competing principles no longer conflict, and the history of the subject is largely the history of finding them.
