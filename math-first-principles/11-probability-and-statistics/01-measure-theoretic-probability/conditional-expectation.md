## Essence

Elementary probability conditions on an event B of positive probability: P(A ∣ B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B). That fails exactly where it is most needed, when conditioning on the value of a continuous random variable Z, because {Z = z} has probability 0. Kolmogorov's solution is to condition on a σ-algebra 𝒢, the available information, rather than on one event: E[X ∣ 𝒢] is the 𝒢-measurable random variable with the same averages as X over every event in 𝒢. Geometrically it is the orthogonal projection of X onto the 𝒢-measurable variables, the best prediction of X from that information; for merely integrable X its existence is the Radon–Nikodym theorem.

## Core tenets

1. **The definition: the same averages on every observable event.** For X with E∣X∣ < ∞ and a σ-algebra 𝒢 ⊆ ℱ, Y = E[X ∣ 𝒢] is a 𝒢-measurable random variable with E[Y·1_G] = E[X·1_G] for every G ∈ 𝒢, where 1_G is the indicator of G. If 𝒢 is generated by a partition into events B₁, B₂, … of positive probability, Y is the elementary answer: on Bᵢ it equals E[X·1_(Bᵢ)]/P(Bᵢ). Conditioning on a random variable Z means taking 𝒢 = σ(Z); then E[X ∣ Z] = g(Z) for some Borel function g, and "E[X ∣ Z = z]" means g(z), which is pinned down only for almost every z under the law of Z.

2. **In L² it is the orthogonal projection, and so the best predictor.** With ⟨U, V⟩ = E[UV], square-integrable random variables form a Hilbert space L², and the 𝒢-measurable ones a closed subspace L²(𝒢). The projection Y of X is characterised by X − Y ⊥ L²(𝒢), that is, E[(X − Y)·W] = 0 for every 𝒢-measurable W in L²; taking W = 1_G recovers the definition. Pythagoras gives E[(X − W)²] = E[(X − Y)²] + E[(Y − W)²], so Y minimises the mean squared error among all predictions based on 𝒢.

3. **For integrable X, existence is the Radon–Nikodym theorem.** For X ≥ 0, ν(G) = E[X·1_G] defines a measure on 𝒢 that vanishes on the null events of 𝒢, so it has a density with respect to P restricted to 𝒢, and that density is E[X ∣ 𝒢]. Uniqueness holds only almost surely: two versions may differ on a null event. This is not pedantry; it is why conditioning on a single null event has no meaning of its own.

4. **The rules are the rules of projections.** Tower property: if ℋ ⊆ 𝒢, then E[E[X ∣ 𝒢] ∣ ℋ] = E[X ∣ ℋ], since projecting onto a subspace and then onto a smaller one is projecting onto the smaller one; with ℋ trivial, E[E[X ∣ 𝒢]] = E[X]. Taking out what is known: E[Z·X ∣ 𝒢] = Z·E[X ∣ 𝒢] when Z is 𝒢-measurable and Z·X is integrable. Independence: if X is independent of 𝒢, then E[X ∣ 𝒢] = E[X]. Conditional Jensen: f(E[X ∣ 𝒢]) ≤ E[f(X) ∣ 𝒢] for convex f.

5. **Variance splits into explained and unexplained parts.** Pythagoras for the projection gives Var(X) = E[Var(X ∣ 𝒢)] + Var(E[X ∣ 𝒢]), so conditioning never increases variance. This is the engine of the Rao–Blackwell theorem in estimation, and the share Var(E[X ∣ 𝒢])/Var(X) is the population version of R² in regression. For a jointly normal pair the projection is linear, E[X ∣ Y] = E[X] + (Cov(X, Y)/Var(Y))·(Y − E[Y]), which is the formula behind linear regression and the Kalman filter.

6. **The Borel–Kolmogorov paradox: a null event has no conditional law of its own.** Choose a point uniformly on a sphere and condition on its lying on a given great circle. Treat the circle as the equator, the limit of the bands ∣latitude∣ < ε, and the conditional law is uniform. Treat the same circle as a pair of opposite meridians, the limit of thin wedges between nearby meridians, and the conditional density is proportional to ∣cos(latitude)∣, because the wedges are wider at the equator than at the poles. Both answers are correct: E[X ∣ Z] depends on the whole σ-algebra σ(Z), of which {Z = z} is one member, not on the null event alone. Kolmogorov drew exactly this conclusion in 1933.

7. **Regular conditional distributions turn conditioning into a kernel.** On spaces such as ℝⁿ one can choose versions of P(X ∈ B ∣ Z = z) that form, for each z, a genuine probability measure in B. This kernel is the posterior distribution of Bayesian statistics and the transition probability of a Markov process; its existence needs a theorem about the topology of the space, not elementary division.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sub-σ-algebra 𝒢 | A σ-algebra contained in ℱ, representing the information available |
| E[X ∣ 𝒢] | The 𝒢-measurable variable with the same integral as X over every G ∈ 𝒢 |
| Version | One choice of E[X ∣ 𝒢]; any two agree almost surely |
| L² | The random variables with E[X²] < ∞, a Hilbert space under ⟨U, V⟩ = E[UV] |
| Orthogonal projection | The closest point of a closed subspace, characterised by an error orthogonal to it |
| Tower property | Conditioning on more information and then on less equals conditioning on less |
| Conditional variance | Var(X ∣ 𝒢) = E[(X − E[X ∣ 𝒢])² ∣ 𝒢] |
| Regular conditional distribution | Conditional probabilities chosen to form a probability measure for each value of the condition |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Condition on {Z = z} by dividing"**: P(Z = z) = 0 for continuous Z, and the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox shows that any answer depends on how the null event is approximated. Only σ(Z) gives a meaning.
- **"A conditional expectation is a number"**: E[X ∣ 𝒢] is a random variable, the prediction as a function of the information; E[X ∣ Z = z] is that function evaluated at z.
- **"Uncorrelated means E[X ∣ Y] = E[X]"**: for a standard normal Y and X = Y², Cov(X, Y) = E[Y³] = 0, yet E[X ∣ Y] = Y² is not constant. Zero correlation means only that the best *linear* predictor is constant.
