## Essence

Mathematics began as calculation for practical ends and became, in Greece, a deductive science in which results follow from stated assumptions by proof. Its later history is a repeated cycle: new methods race ahead of their justification, paradoxes appear, and a period of rigour rebuilds the foundations so that the methods can go further. The calculus outran its logic for 150 years until limits were defined; set theory produced paradoxes until it was axiomatised; the search for certain foundations ended in Gödel's theorems. This leaf traces that through-line with well-established dates only.

## Core tenets

1. **The earliest mathematics was computational and sophisticated.** Old Babylonian scribes (about 1800–1600 BCE) wrote in base 60 with place value; the tablet YBC 7289 gives √2 as 1;24,51,10, correct to about six decimal places, and they solved quadratic problems by completing the square. The Egyptian Rhind papyrus, copied about 1550 BCE, works with unit fractions and areas. The algorithms were reliable but justified by examples, not proof.

2. **Greece made proof the standard.** Greek mathematicians found that the side and diagonal of a square have no common measure, so ratios of whole numbers cannot describe all lengths; Eudoxus answered with a theory of proportion that anticipates Dedekind's cuts. Euclid's *Elements* (about 300 BCE) derived hundreds of results from a few definitions, postulates and common notions, the model of the axiomatic method for two thousand years. Archimedes (died 212 BCE) computed areas and volumes by the method of exhaustion, squeezing a figure between inscribed and circumscribed shapes: a limit argument without the word.

3. **India, China and the Islamic world built the arithmetic and algebra that Europe inherited.** Indian mathematicians developed decimal place value with a symbol for zero; Aryabhata wrote in 499 and Brahmagupta in 628 gave rules for computing with zero and negative numbers. In Kerala, Madhava (fourteenth century) found infinite series for sine, cosine and arctangent. The Chinese *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art* solved linear systems by what is now called Gaussian elimination, and Liu Hui's commentary (263) approximated π by polygons. In Baghdad around 820, al-Khwārizmī wrote the book whose title gave "algebra" and whose author's name gave "algorithm". Fibonacci's *Liber Abaci* (1202) carried Hindu–Arabic numerals to Europe; Cardano's *Ars Magna* (1545) published the solution of the cubic and quartic.

4. **Coordinates and the calculus gave unprecedented power without secure foundations.** Descartes's *La Géométrie* (1637) turned curves into equations. Pascal and Fermat founded probability in letters of 1654. Newton and Leibniz independently created the calculus; Leibniz published first in 1684, Newton's *Principia* appeared in 1687. Both used infinitely small quantities, which George Berkeley mocked in *The Analyst* (1734) as "ghosts of departed quantities". Euler and others nevertheless produced a flood of correct results by manipulations no one could justify.

5. **The nineteenth century rebuilt analysis on limits and the real numbers.** Cauchy's *Cours d'analyse* (1821) defined continuity and convergence by limits; Weierstrass made them precise with ε and δ. Dedekind (1872) and Cantor constructed the real numbers from the rationals. Geometry was liberated at the same time: Lobachevsky (1829) and Bolyai (1832) published consistent geometries in which Euclid's parallel postulate fails, and Riemann's lecture of 1854 founded the geometry of curved spaces. Klein's Erlangen programme (1872) classified geometries by their symmetry groups. Axioms ceased to be self-evident truths and became chosen assumptions.

6. **Set theory produced paradoxes and a foundations crisis.** Cantor proved in 1874 that the real numbers are uncountable and in 1891, by the diagonal argument, that there is no largest infinity. Frege tried to derive arithmetic from logic; Russell's paradox (1901) of the set of all sets that are not members of themselves showed his system inconsistent. Responses multiplied: Zermelo's axioms (1908), Russell and Whitehead's *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913), Brouwer's intuitionism, and Hilbert's programme to prove mathematics consistent by finitary means. Hilbert had also set the century's agenda with his list of problems in Paris in 1900.

7. **Gödel and Turing set the limits; abstraction and the computer reshaped the field.** Gödel (1931) showed that any consistent, effectively axiomatised system containing arithmetic leaves some true statements unprovable and cannot prove its own consistency. Turing (1936) defined computation and showed some problems undecidable. Mathematics turned to structures defined by axioms: the Bourbaki group's volumes began appearing in 1939, Eilenberg and Mac Lane introduced categories in 1945, and Cohen (1963) proved the continuum hypothesis independent of the standard axioms. The computer entered proof with the four colour theorem (Appel and Haken, 1976). Long-standing problems still fell to human insight: Wiles proved Fermat's last theorem (completed with Taylor, 1994–1995) and Perelman the Poincaré conjecture (2002–2003).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Place-value notation | A digit's value depends on its position, as in base 60 or base 10 |
| Incommensurable | Two lengths with no common unit, such as a square's side and diagonal |
| Method of exhaustion | Squeezing an area between known shapes; a precursor of limits |
| Axiomatic method | Deriving all results from explicitly stated assumptions |
| Infinitesimal | A quantity smaller than every positive number, used informally in early calculus |
| Arithmetisation of analysis | Founding calculus on the real numbers and ε–δ limits |
| Foundations crisis | The paradoxes and disputes over set theory and logic around 1900–1931 |
| Hilbert's programme | The plan to prove mathematics consistent by finitary methods |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Mathematics developed in Greece and then in Europe."** Decimal place value, zero, algebra, linear elimination and power series came from India, China and the Islamic world.
- **"Rigour came first and applications later."** The calculus was used for 150 years before it was rigorous; rigour came when its gaps caused errors.
- **"Gödel showed mathematics is unreliable."** He showed that no single formal system captures all arithmetic truth; ordinary proofs are as reliable as before.
