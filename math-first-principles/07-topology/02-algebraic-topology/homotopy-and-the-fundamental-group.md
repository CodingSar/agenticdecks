## Essence

Two loops in a space are homotopic if one can be deformed continuously into the other, and the homotopy classes of loops at a base point form a group, the fundamental group π₁, under "go round one loop, then the other". It is the first algebraic invariant of a space: spaces with different groups cannot be homeomorphic. The key computation is π₁(S¹) ≅ ℤ, where the integer counts how often a loop winds round the circle; it is proved by lifting loops to the real line. The Seifert–van Kampen theorem then computes π₁ of spaces glued from pieces, which turns topology into group presentations.

## Core tenets

1. **Homotopy classes of loops form a group.** A loop at x₀ is a path γ: [0, 1] → X with γ(0) = γ(1) = x₀. Two loops are homotopic if a continuous H: [0, 1] × [0, 1] → X deforms one into the other with the endpoints held at x₀. Concatenation (run γ at double speed, then δ) is associative only up to reparametrisation, which is a homotopy; the constant loop is the identity and the reversed loop the inverse. Passing to classes gives a genuine group, π₁(X, x₀).

2. **π₁ is a functor and a homotopy invariant.** A continuous f: X → Y with f(x₀) = y₀ induces a homomorphism f_*: [γ] ↦ [f∘γ], with (g∘f)_* = g_*∘f_* and id_* = id. Hence homeomorphic spaces have isomorphic groups, and so do homotopy-equivalent ones, such as ℝⁿ and a point, or a solid torus and a circle. In a path-connected space, changing the base point changes the group only up to isomorphism.

3. **π₁(S¹) ≅ ℤ, by lifting to the real line.** The map p: ℝ → S¹, p(t) = (cos 2πt, sin 2πt), wraps the line round the circle, and each small arc of S¹ has as preimage disjoint copies of itself. So a path in S¹ lifts uniquely to ℝ once a starting point is chosen: cut it, using a Lebesgue number of [0, 1], into pieces each inside a small arc, and lift piece by piece. Homotopies lift the same way. A loop at (1, 0) lifts to a path from 0 to an integer n, homotopic loops give the same n, and concatenation adds the integers, so this degree is an isomorphism π₁(S¹) → ℤ.

4. **The degree proves existence theorems.** No retraction of the disc D² onto its boundary circle exists: the loop round S¹ would be the image of a loop in the convex disc, which contracts, so its class would be both a generator and zero. Brouwer's fixed-point theorem in dimension 2 follows (see [fixed-point theorems](fixed-point-theorems.md)). So does the fundamental theorem of algebra: if a polynomial p of degree n ≥ 1 had no root, the loop t ↦ p(R·e^(2πit))/|p(R·e^(2πit))| would have degree n for large R, since p behaves like zⁿ there, but degree 0 at R = 0, and varying R is a homotopy.

5. **Spheres of dimension at least 2 are simply connected.** A loop in Sⁿ with n ≥ 2 can be deformed to a piecewise smooth loop, which misses a point, and Sⁿ minus a point is homeomorphic to ℝⁿ, where every loop contracts. So π₁(Sⁿ) = 0 for n ≥ 2, and π₁ cannot distinguish S² from S³. Higher holes need [homology](homology.md) or the higher homotopy groups πₙ, which are abelian and hard to compute: π₃(S²) ≅ ℤ, generated by the Hopf map S³ → S².

6. **The Seifert–van Kampen theorem computes π₁ of a union.** Let X = U ∪ V with U and V open and path-connected, and U ∩ V path-connected and containing x₀. With i and j the homomorphisms induced by the inclusions of U ∩ V into U and V, and ∗ the free product,

   ```
   π₁(X) ≅ π₁(U) ∗ π₁(V) / N,   N = normal subgroup generated by i(w)·j(w)⁻¹, w ∈ π₁(U ∩ V)
   ```

   The proof cuts any loop or homotopy in X, by a Lebesgue number, into pieces each in U or in V. The connected overlap is needed: S¹ is the union of two contractible arcs, but their intersection has two components, and the formula would wrongly give the trivial group.

7. **Gluing turns spaces into group presentations.** Two circles joined at a point (the figure-eight) have π₁ the free group F₂ on generators a and b. Attaching a disc along a loop adds one relation, the word that loop spells. So the torus, a square with sides glued in the pattern a b a⁻¹ b⁻¹, has π₁ = ⟨a, b ∣ aba⁻¹b⁻¹⟩ ≅ ℤ², the orientable surface of genus g has 2g generators and one relation, and ℝP² has π₁ = ℤ/2. Every finitely presented group arises this way, even as π₁ of a closed 4-manifold; since no algorithm decides whether a presentation defines the trivial group, none decides whether two closed 4-manifolds are homeomorphic.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Homotopy of paths | A continuous deformation between two paths with the endpoints fixed |
| π₁(X, x₀) | The group of homotopy classes of loops at x₀ under concatenation |
| Simply connected | Path-connected with trivial π₁ |
| Homotopy equivalence | Maps f: X → Y and g: Y → X with g∘f and f∘g homotopic to the identities |
| Retraction | A map r: X → A onto a subspace with r(a) = a for every a ∈ A |
| Degree | The integer n by which a loop winds round S¹ |
| Free product G ∗ H | The group of words in elements of G and H, with no relations between them |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Spaces with the same π₁ are homeomorphic."**: A disc and a point are homotopy equivalent, both with trivial π₁, but not homeomorphic. π₁ sees loops, not dimension.
- **"π₁ detects every hole."**: π₁(S²) is trivial although S² encloses a void; such holes need homology or higher homotopy groups.
