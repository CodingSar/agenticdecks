## Essence

Quantum mechanics is a probability theory whose events are closed subspaces of a complex Hilbert space rather than subsets of a sample space. A state is a unit vector (up to a phase), an observable is a self-adjoint operator, and the spectral theorem turns the pair into a probability distribution of measurement outcomes: the Born rule. Time evolution is a one-parameter group of unitary operators, and Stone's theorem says every such group is generated by a self-adjoint Hamiltonian, which is the Schrödinger equation. The distinctively quantum features (uncertainty, spin, entanglement) follow from non-commuting operators, from the group SU(2), and from tensor products. The mathematics, set out by John von Neumann in 1932, is uncontroversial; what it means is not.

## Core tenets

1. **States are rays in a Hilbert space, and probabilities come from inner products.** A pure state is a unit vector ψ in a separable complex Hilbert space H, with ψ and e^(iα)ψ describing the same state. Superposition is linearity: if ψ and φ are states, so is any normalised aψ + bφ. Ignorance about which pure state was prepared is described by a density operator ρ, positive with trace 1, and pure states are the ρ that are rank-one projections.

2. **Observables are self-adjoint operators, and the spectral theorem gives the Born rule.** A self-adjoint A has a projection-valued spectral measure E with A = ∫ λ dE(λ). In state ψ, the probability that a measurement of A lands in a Borel set B is ⟨ψ, E(B)ψ⟩, and the mean is ⟨ψ, Aψ⟩. For an eigenbasis eₙ with eigenvalues λₙ this reads P(λₙ) = ∣⟨eₙ, ψ⟩∣². Self-adjointness makes the outcomes real and the probabilities sum to 1.

3. **Symmetric is not enough: the domain is physics.** Position and momentum are unbounded, so they are defined only on dense domains, and an operator with ⟨φ, Aψ⟩ = ⟨Aφ, ψ⟩ on its domain may still lack a spectral theorem. Momentum −i·d/dx on functions vanishing at both ends of [0, 1] has a family of self-adjoint extensions, fixed by the boundary condition ψ(1) = e^(iθ)ψ(0), each with a different spectrum. On the half-line [0, ∞) it has none, so a particle in a half-infinite box has no momentum observable. Choosing an extension is a physical choice of boundary behaviour.

4. **Unitary evolution has a self-adjoint generator (Stone, 1932).** Evolution must preserve probabilities, hence inner products, so it is a family of unitaries with U(s + t) = U(s)U(t). Stone's theorem says each strongly continuous such group equals e^(−itH) for a unique self-adjoint H, and conversely. Differentiating gives the Schrödinger equation i·dψ/dt = Hψ (in units with ħ = 1). The same theorem makes momentum the generator of translations and angular momentum the generator of rotations.

5. **Uncertainty is a theorem about commutators.** For self-adjoint A and B, write σ for standard deviations in the state ψ. Applying Cauchy–Schwarz to (A − ⟨A⟩)ψ and (B − ⟨B⟩)ψ and keeping the imaginary part gives the Robertson inequality σ_A·σ_B ≥ ½∣⟨ψ, [A, B]ψ⟩∣. Position and momentum satisfy [x, p] = iħ, so σ_x·σ_p ≥ ħ/2. No bounded operators satisfy that relation: in dimension n, taking traces gives 0 = iħn, and a norm argument extends this to all bounded operators. So quantum mechanics needs unbounded operators, and the Stone–von Neumann theorem says the relation in its exponentiated form has, up to unitary equivalence, only one irreducible solution.

6. **Spin is a representation of SU(2), not of the rotation group.** SU(2), the unit quaternions, maps two-to-one onto the rotation group SO(3) with kernel ±I. Its irreducible representations have dimension 2j + 1 for j = 0, ½, 1, 3⁄2, …, and those with half-integer j do not descend to SO(3): a rotation by 2π acts on a spin-½ state as −1. Because states are rays, a sign is allowed, so physics sees the projective representations of SO(3), which are the representations of SU(2). Spin ½ lives on ℂ², with the Pauli matrices as its observables.

7. **Composite systems use tensor products, which produce entanglement.** Two systems in H₁ and H₂ combine in H₁ ⊗ H₂, which contains states that are not products, such as (∣0⟩⊗∣0⟩ + ∣1⟩⊗∣1⟩)/√2. John Bell (1964) proved that the correlations of such states violate inequalities satisfied by every local hidden-variable model, and experiments agree with quantum mechanics. Entanglement is the resource behind quantum computing.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ray | A unit vector up to a phase factor; a pure state |
| Density operator | Positive trace-one operator describing a mixed state |
| Self-adjoint operator | A = A* including equality of domains; the condition for a spectral theorem |
| Spectral measure | Projection-valued measure E with A = ∫ λ dE(λ) |
| Born rule | Probability of outcome in B is ⟨ψ, E(B)ψ⟩ |
| Commutator | [A, B] = AB − BA; its expectation bounds joint uncertainty |
| Stone's theorem | Unitary groups e^(−itH) correspond to self-adjoint H |
| SU(2) | 2 × 2 unitary matrices of determinant 1; the double cover of SO(3) |
| Entangled state | A vector of H₁ ⊗ H₂ that is not a product φ ⊗ χ |

## How it fits

- **Below:** Hilbert spaces and spectral theory supply states and observables; the finite-dimensional spectral theorem is the model case; Lie group representations classify spin; measure-theoretic probability interprets the Born rule.
- **Above:** Shor's quantum factoring algorithm threatens [cryptography](../03-information-codes-and-cryptography/cryptography.md); the [philosophy of mathematics](../../15-mathematics-in-context/philosophy-of-mathematics.md) cites quantum theory as the sharpest case of "unreasonable effectiveness".
- **Siblings:** [general relativity](the-geometry-of-general-relativity.md) is the other fundamental theory in this area, and no accepted theory yet unites the two. [Modelling](modelling-and-dimensional-analysis.md) places the quantum regime at the scale set by ħ.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Uncertainty comes from clumsy measurement."** It is a property of the state: no state has small spreads in two observables whose commutator has large expectation.
- **"Hermitian and self-adjoint are the same."** For unbounded operators, symmetry on a domain does not give real spectrum or a spectral theorem; the domain must match.
- **"The wave function is a probability."** It is an amplitude; probabilities are squared moduli of inner products, and amplitudes interfere.
