## Essence

Lipids are the molecules that water rejects, and that rejection is what makes cells possible. Phospholipids have a water-loving head and two water-hating tails, so in water they assemble spontaneously into a bilayer that closes on itself, producing a sealed compartment with no energy spent and no template needed. The bilayer is two molecules thick, fluid in the plane and almost impermeable to ions and polar solutes, so a cell can keep an interior different from its surroundings and every crossing must go through a protein. Lipids are also the densest fuel store, the precursor of steroid and eicosanoid signals, and, when carried in the blood as lipoproteins, the material of atherosclerosis.

## Core tenets

1. **Amphipathic molecules self-assemble, and their shape decides what they build.** Removing nonpolar tails from water frees ordered water molecules, so assembly is driven by entropy ([the hydrophobic effect](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/02-chemistry-of-life/water-solutions-and-concentration.md)). Single-tailed fatty acids and bile salts are cones and form micelles; two-tailed phospholipids are cylinders and form bilayers; a bilayer edge exposes tails, so it seals into a vesicle. Membranes therefore form and heal themselves, and detergents disrupt them by inserting cones.

2. **The bilayer is a two-dimensional fluid with a maintained asymmetry.** Lipids diffuse laterally about a micrometre per second but flip between leaflets only over hours, because the polar head must cross the hydrophobic core. Cells exploit this: flippases keep phosphatidylserine on the inner leaflet, and its appearance outside marks a cell for phagocytosis (apoptosis) or provides the surface on which clotting factors assemble (activated platelets). Fluidity is set by chain length, double bonds (which kink the chain) and cholesterol, and cells adjust the mix to keep it constant.

3. **Permeability follows solubility in oil.** A molecule crosses a bilayer by dissolving in it, so the permeability is proportional to its oil–water partition coefficient (Overton's rule) and inversely to size. Oxygen, carbon dioxide, steroid hormones, ethanol and general anaesthetics cross freely; glucose, amino acids and ions do not (sodium's permeability is about 10^9 times lower than water's). This single rule explains why ions need channels and pumps, why lipid-soluble drugs are absorbed from the gut and enter the brain, and why the blood–brain barrier excludes polar ones.

4. **Membrane proteins float in the bilayer, and they are most of pharmacology.** The fluid mosaic model (Singer and Nicolson, 1972) has proteins embedded by hydrophobic alpha helices of about 20 residues that span the core. Roughly a quarter of human genes encode membrane proteins, and surface receptors, channels and transporters make up over half of all drug targets, because they are reachable from the blood.

5. **Triglycerides are the fuel store because they are anhydrous.** Fat yields about 37 kJ/g against 17 kJ/g for carbohydrate, and it is stored without water, whereas glycogen binds two to three times its weight in water. A 70-kg adult carries roughly 15 kg of fat, enough for one to two months of fasting, and under a kilogram of glycogen, enough for a day. Fatty acids are insoluble, so in blood they ride on albumin and triglycerides ride inside lipoproteins.

6. **Lipids are signals.** Phosphatidylinositol bisphosphate in the inner leaflet is split by phospholipase C into inositol trisphosphate and diacylglycerol, two second messengers. Arachidonic acid freed from membranes becomes prostaglandins, thromboxane and leukotrienes, the mediators of pain, fever, platelet aggregation and bronchoconstriction; aspirin and the non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs act by blocking cyclo-oxygenase. Cholesterol is the parent of every steroid hormone, of vitamin D and of the bile acids.

7. **Cholesterol must be transported in particles, and that is the root of atherosclerosis.** Cholesterol stiffens membranes and cannot be broken down by human cells; it leaves the body only as bile. Being insoluble, it travels in lipoproteins: a surface of phospholipid and apolipoprotein around a core of cholesteryl ester and triglyceride. Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) delivers cholesterol to cells through the LDL receptor (Brown and Goldstein, 1970s–80s); when the receptor is defective (familial hypercholesterolaemia) or LDL is simply abundant, particles are retained in artery walls, oxidised, and eaten by macrophages that become foam cells.

8. **Polyunsaturated chains are vulnerable to oxidation.** A radical can abstract a hydrogen from the carbon between two double bonds, and the resulting lipid radical attacks the next chain, so peroxidation is a chain reaction that destroys membranes. Vitamin E breaks the chain; glutathione peroxidases repair it; the iron-dependent, peroxidation-driven form of cell death (ferroptosis) and the oxidised LDL of atheroma are its pathology.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Amphipathic | Having a polar head and a nonpolar tail in one molecule |
| Bilayer | Two leaflets of phospholipid, tails inward; ~5 nm thick |
| Micelle | Sphere of single-tailed lipids, tails inward; how bile salts solubilise fat |
| Partition coefficient | Ratio of a solute's solubility in oil to that in water; predicts membrane permeability |
| Fluid mosaic | Proteins diffusing within a fluid lipid sheet |
| Lipoprotein | Particle with a phospholipid/apolipoprotein surface carrying insoluble lipid in blood |
| Eicosanoid | Twenty-carbon signalling lipid from arachidonic acid (prostaglandins, leukotrienes) |
| Lipid peroxidation | Radical chain oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids |

## Common misconceptions

- **"The membrane is a rigid wall."** It is a liquid; proteins and lipids move within it, and it can be deformed, pinched and fused at no great cost.
- **"Cholesterol is a toxin."** Every animal membrane needs it and every steroid is made from it; the problem is its retention in arteries when transport is overloaded.
- **"A more lipid-soluble drug is always better absorbed."** Very lipophilic drugs dissolve poorly in gut fluid and bind avidly to fat and protein; absorption needs both solubilities.
