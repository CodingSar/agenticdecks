## Essence

Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes and ketones, and their many hydroxyl groups give them three roles that look unrelated but share one chemistry. As fuel, glucose is the molecule every tissue can burn and the brain depends on. As structure, chains of sugars bind water to make the gels of cartilage, mucus and the extracellular matrix. As identity, short branched sugar chains on proteins and lipids form a code that blood groups, immune cells and pathogens all read. The same reactivity that makes sugars useful makes them dangerous: a reducing sugar reacts spontaneously with proteins, so chronic hyperglycaemia slowly rewrites the proteome, which is the chemistry behind the long-term complications of diabetes.

## Core tenets

1. **Stereochemistry is destiny, because enzymes are stereospecific.** Glucose, galactose and mannose differ only in the orientation of one hydroxyl, yet each needs its own enzymes; a missing galactose enzyme (galactosaemia) is lethal if milk is not withdrawn. Glucose dominates metabolism partly because, in its chair form, every hydroxyl sits in the stable equatorial position, so it spends the least time as the reactive open-chain aldehyde and is the least prone to damaging proteins by glycation.

2. **The glycosidic bond's geometry decides what we can digest.** Starch and glycogen link glucose by alpha(1→4) bonds with alpha(1→6) branches; cellulose links the same glucose by beta(1→4), which human enzymes cannot cleave, so it is fibre. Lactose is a beta(1→4) galactose–glucose disaccharide that needs lactase, an enzyme most adults worldwide switch off after weaning; persistence is a recent mutation in dairying populations, and lactose intolerance is the ancestral state.

3. **Glycogen is the fast store and fat the deep one.** Glycogen's branching gives many ends for simultaneous release and keeps it soluble, but it is hydrated (two to three grams of water per gram), so the liver holds about 100 g and muscle about 400 g, a day's supply. Liver glycogen is for the blood glucose of others; muscle glycogen is for the muscle itself. Enzyme defects at any step give a glycogen storage disease with hypoglycaemia, muscle cramps or liver enlargement depending on which enzyme and which tissue.

4. **Sugars react with proteins without any enzyme.** The open-chain aldehyde condenses with lysine amines to form a Schiff base that rearranges into a stable Amadori product, at a rate proportional to glucose concentration and time. Glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) therefore integrates glucose over the ~120-day life of the red cell and is the standard measure of diabetic control. Over years, further rearrangements yield advanced glycation end-products that cross-link collagen, stiffen vessels and the lens, and activate inflammatory receptors; this is one of the mechanisms behind retinopathy, nephropathy and arterial disease in diabetes.

5. **Polyanionic sugar chains hold water and build tissue.** Glycosaminoglycans (hyaluronan, chondroitin sulfate, heparan sulfate) are long, sulfated, highly charged chains that attract counter-ions and hence water, so proteoglycans form the hydrated gel that lets cartilage bear load and lets the matrix resist compression. Heparan sulfate on cell surfaces stores growth factors; the related heparin accelerates antithrombin a thousandfold, which is its anticoagulant mechanism.

6. **Glycans are an identity code read by lectins.** The ABO blood groups are terminal sugars: an H antigen that is left alone (O), capped with N-acetylgalactosamine (A) or galactose (B) by one glycosyltransferase, so a transfusion reaction is sugar recognition by pre-formed antibody. Selectins on endothelium catch leukocytes by their sialylated sugars and make them roll, the first step of inflammation. Influenza binds sialic acid to enter cells and cuts it with neuraminidase to leave; oseltamivir blocks the cutting.

7. **Glycosylation has no template, so it is heterogeneous.** Sugars are added to proteins in the endoplasmic reticulum (N-linked, at asparagine) and Golgi (O-linked, at serine or threonine) by competing transferases, giving a population of glycoforms rather than one structure. Glycans direct folding, lysosomal targeting (mannose-6-phosphate) and plasma half-life (desialylated proteins are cleared by the liver), and the sugar on an antibody's constant region tunes its immune effector functions, which matters for therapeutic antibodies.

8. **Microbial sugars are what innate immunity recognises.** Bacterial peptidoglycan, the beta-glucans of fungal walls and the lipopolysaccharide of Gram-negative bacteria are carbohydrates humans do not make, so receptors that bind them cannot misfire on self. The same wall chemistry is a drug target: beta-lactams block peptidoglycan cross-linking, echinocandins block beta-glucan synthesis.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Monosaccharide | Single sugar (glucose, fructose, galactose); cyclises in solution |
| Reducing sugar | Sugar with a free aldehyde or ketone; reacts with amines (glycation) |
| Glycosidic bond | Link between sugars; alpha or beta configuration decides digestibility |
| Glycogen | Branched alpha-glucose polymer; the short-term glucose store |
| Glycation | Non-enzymatic reaction of sugar with protein; basis of HbA1c and of diabetic tissue damage |
| Glycosylation | Enzymatic attachment of sugar chains to protein or lipid |
| Glycosaminoglycan | Long, sulfated polyanionic chain that binds water; on proteoglycans |
| Lectin | Protein that recognises a specific sugar structure (selectins, mannose-binding lectin) |
| Glycocalyx | Sugar coat on the outer face of every cell |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Carbohydrates are only fuel."** Sugar chains are the cell's surface identity and the matrix's water reservoir; most secreted and surface proteins are glycosylated.
- **"Glycation and glycosylation are the same."** Glycosylation is enzymatic and intended; glycation is a spontaneous side reaction and a measure of damage.
- **"All sugars are metabolically equivalent."** Fructose bypasses the main control point of glycolysis in the liver, and galactose needs its own pathway; the body treats them differently.
