## Essence

A protein is a chain of amino acids that folds into a specific three-dimensional shape, and the shape is the function. Twenty side chains of different size, charge and affinity for water give enough variety to build enzymes, pumps, receptors, motors, fibres and antibodies. The chain folds because water drives nonpolar side chains together while the backbone satisfies its hydrogen bonds in helices and sheets; the sequence therefore encodes the structure, and the structure is only marginally stable, so it can move, switch and be regulated. The same marginal stability makes proteins vulnerable to heat, pH and mutation, and misfolded proteins that clump together are the cause of a whole class of disease.

## Core tenets

1. **Sequence determines structure; the native fold is a free-energy minimum.** Anfinsen showed in the 1960s that a denatured ribonuclease refolds spontaneously into its active shape when the denaturant is removed, so the information needed to fold is in the sequence and folding is a [thermodynamic](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/energy-and-thermodynamics.md) descent, not a construction process. Chaperones prevent wrong turns; they do not supply the blueprint.

2. **The hydrophobic effect is the main driving force; hydrogen bonding sets the architecture.** Burying nonpolar side chains frees ordered water molecules, a gain in entropy that pays for the loss of chain entropy. The polar backbone cannot be buried without pairing its N-H and C=O groups, so the interior is built from the two arrangements that satisfy every backbone hydrogen bond: the alpha helix and the beta sheet.

3. **Folded proteins are marginally stable, and that is a feature.** The net stability of a typical domain is only 20 to 60 kJ/mol, the equivalent of a few hydrogen bonds, because large favourable and unfavourable terms nearly cancel. Marginal stability lets a protein change conformation when a ligand binds (allostery), be switched by phosphorylation, and be unfolded for degradation; it also means fever, a pH shift or a single substitution can tip the balance toward unfolding.

4. **Structure is hierarchical and modular.** Primary (sequence), secondary (helix, sheet, turn), tertiary (the fold of one chain) and quaternary (assembly of chains) levels nest. Long chains fold as separate domains, and evolution reuses domains by recombining the exons that encode them, so a kinase domain or an immunoglobulin domain appears in hundreds of proteins.

5. **Folding is fast because it is cooperative, not a random search.** A 100-residue chain has more conformations than could be sampled in the age of the universe (Levinthal's paradox), yet folds in milliseconds to seconds, because local structure forms first and each correct contact makes the next more likely, a funnel rather than a maze. The hazard is the partly folded intermediate, whose exposed hydrophobic patches stick to other chains; chaperones such as Hsp70 and the chaperonins bind those patches and give the chain another chance.

6. **Function is motion.** Haemoglobin binds oxygen cooperatively because binding at one haem shifts the quaternary structure and raises the affinity of the other three. Enzymes close over substrates, motors walk by cycling between conformations, and receptors carry a signal across a membrane by changing shape. A crystal structure is one frame of a film.

7. **Misfolding and aggregation cause disease.** Sickle cell disease is a single substitution (glutamate to valine at position 6 of beta-globin) that creates a hydrophobic patch on the surface, so deoxygenated haemoglobin polymerises into fibres. In cystic fibrosis, the commonest variant (deletion of phenylalanine 508) is a folding defect: the channel would work, but the cell's quality control destroys it. Amyloid diseases (Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, transthyretin amyloidosis, prion disease) share one physics: a cross-beta fibril is often more stable than the native fold, so once a seed exists normal molecules are recruited and the fibril grows; prions add transmissibility.

8. **Covalent chemistry fixes and tunes the fold.** Disulfide bonds stabilise extracellular proteins (antibodies, insulin, digestive enzymes) because the extracellular space is oxidising while the cytosol is kept [reduced](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/02-chemistry-of-life/redox-and-electron-transfer.md), so a cytosolic protein cannot rely on them. Glycosylation, phosphorylation and proteolytic trimming after synthesis alter charge, solubility and shape.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Peptide bond | Planar amide link between amino acids; rotation is restricted to two backbone angles per residue |
| Alpha helix, beta sheet | Backbone arrangements that satisfy every hydrogen bond; the building blocks of folds |
| Hydrophobic effect | Entropy-driven tendency of nonpolar groups to cluster away from water |
| Domain | An independently folding unit of ~50–200 residues, often with its own function |
| Denaturation | Loss of the native fold by heat, pH, urea or detergent; may be reversible |
| Chaperone | Protein that binds exposed hydrophobic patches to prevent aggregation during folding |
| Allostery | A change in binding or activity at one site caused by binding at another |
| Amyloid | Ordered cross-beta fibril formed by many unrelated proteins; stable, self-seeding |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Proteins are rigid machines."** They fluctuate continuously; binding, catalysis and signalling depend on movement between conformations.
- **"Chaperones fold proteins."** They prevent aggregation and give misfolded chains another chance; the fold itself is encoded in the sequence.
- **"Every protein has one fixed structure."** About a third of human proteins contain long disordered regions that fold only on binding a partner, and some sequences adopt more than one stable fold (prions are the extreme case).
