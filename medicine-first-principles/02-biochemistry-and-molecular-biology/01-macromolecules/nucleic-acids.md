## Essence

Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) and Ribonucleic Acid (RNA) are polymers of four nucleotides whose order carries information and whose chemistry lets that information be copied, read and corrected. The one idea that makes it work is complementarity: adenine pairs with thymine (or uracil), guanine with cytosine, so each strand of a double helix is a template for the other, and any single strand can find its partner in a mixture of millions. Copying, transcription, molecular diagnostics and sequencing all apply the same rule. DNA is the archive, built for stability and repair; RNA is the working copy, built for versatility and disposability.

## Core tenets

1. **The structure is a template for its own copying.** Watson and Crick (1953) saw that the two antiparallel strands are held by base pairs of fixed geometry (A–T with two hydrogen bonds, G–C with three) and that any base sequence fits the same helix, because the pairs are the same size. Separate the strands and each dictates a new partner. Proteins read the sequence without unwinding it, through the pattern of hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors exposed in the major groove.

2. **Stacking, not hydrogen bonding, holds the helix together; charge keeps it soluble.** The flat aromatic bases stack like coins, excluding water in a [hydrophobic effect](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/02-chemistry-of-life/water-solutions-and-concentration.md), and this stacking provides most of the stability. Hydrogen bonds provide specificity. The backbone carries one negative charge per nucleotide, so DNA is a polyanion that must be neutralised by cations and basic histones to be packed, and it admits flat cationic drugs between its bases (intercalators such as doxorubicin).

3. **Hybridisation is tunable and is the basis of every molecular test.** Strands separate (melt) at a temperature set by length, G–C content and salt, and re-anneal below it. A 20-nucleotide probe or primer finds its one complementary site in a 3-billion-base genome because a single mismatch measurably lowers the melting temperature; the stringency of the conditions sets the balance between sensitivity and specificity. The polymerase chain reaction, in situ hybridisation, microarrays and sequencing all rest on this.

4. **RNA's extra hydroxyl makes it reactive, foldable and catalytic.** The 2'-hydroxyl of ribose can attack the adjacent phosphate, so RNA hydrolyses in alkali and is short-lived; DNA lacks it and survives. The same group lets single-stranded RNA fold into hairpins, loops and pockets, so RNA can bind ligands and catalyse reactions: the ribosome's peptidyl transferase and the spliceosome's core are RNA. Uracil in RNA versus thymine in DNA is a repair strategy: cytosine deaminates spontaneously to uracil, and in DNA a uracil is unambiguously a lesion to be excised.

5. **DNA must be packed and untangled.** Each human cell carries about 2 metres of DNA in a nucleus a few micrometres across, wound on nucleosomes and folded into loops. Replication and transcription twist the helix, generating supercoils that topoisomerases relieve by cutting and resealing strands. Fluoroquinolone antibiotics trap bacterial gyrase and topoisomerase IV; etoposide, irinotecan and doxorubicin trap human topoisomerases with the DNA cut open, which is lethal to a dividing cell.

6. **The polymer decays continuously, so repair is compulsory.** Water depurinates roughly 10,000 bases per cell per day, cytosine deaminates, reactive oxygen species oxidise guanine, ultraviolet light fuses adjacent pyrimidines, and alkylating agents add methyl groups. Each lesion has a characteristic consequence if uncorrected, so the mutations in a tumour carry a record of the chemistry that caused them.

7. **Nucleotides are also currency, coenzymes and signals.** Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) and Guanosine Triphosphate (GTP) carry energy; Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide (NAD), Flavin Adenine Dinucleotide (FAD) and coenzyme A are nucleotide-based; cyclic Adenosine Monophosphate (cAMP) and cyclic GMP are second messengers; UDP-glucose activates sugars. Because polymerases accept analogues, nucleoside mimics are drugs: aciclovir, zidovudine, cytarabine and 5-fluorouracil enter the pathway and terminate or poison it. Purine breakdown ends in poorly soluble uric acid, so gout and tumour lysis are nucleic-acid chemistry too.

8. **The human genome is mostly not protein-coding.** The haploid genome is about 3.1 billion base pairs and about 20,000 protein-coding genes, yet coding sequence is ~1.5% of it; the rest is introns, regulatory elements, repeats and relics of transposons, much of it transcribed. A separate 16.6-kilobase circular mitochondrial genome, inherited only from the mother, encodes 37 genes, 13 of them subunits of the respiratory chain.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Nucleotide | Base + pentose sugar + phosphate; the monomer of DNA and RNA |
| Complementarity | A–T (A–U in RNA) and G–C pairing, so one strand specifies the other |
| Base stacking | Face-to-face packing of bases; the main source of helix stability |
| Melting temperature (Tm) | Temperature at which half the duplex has separated; set by length, G–C content and salt |
| Supercoiling | Over- or under-winding of the helix; managed by topoisomerases |
| Nucleosome | ~147 base pairs wound around a histone octamer; the first level of packing |
| Ribozyme | An RNA with catalytic activity; the ribosome is the largest |
| Nucleoside analogue | A drug that mimics a nucleotide and subverts a polymerase or synthetic pathway |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Hydrogen bonds hold the double helix together."** Stacking does most of the holding; hydrogen bonds supply the specificity of pairing.
- **"DNA is a stable molecule."** It is chemically fragile and damaged thousands of times a day per cell; genomes persist because repair is continuous.
- **"Most of the genome codes for protein."** About 1.5% does; a large fraction of the rest regulates when and where that 1.5% is used.
