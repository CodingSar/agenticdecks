## Essence

A lipid bilayer lets almost nothing charged or polar through, so every ion, sugar and amino acid that enters or leaves a cell does so through a protein: a channel that opens a pore, a carrier that changes shape around its cargo, or a pump that spends ATP. One pump above all, the sodium–potassium ATPase, builds the gradients of sodium and potassium across every cell membrane, and almost everything else is powered by letting those gradients run down in a controlled way: the resting membrane potential, the uptake of glucose from the gut, the reabsorption of salt by the kidney, the regulation of cell volume. The same proteins, arranged on opposite faces of an epithelium, move substances across whole tissues. They are the targets of diuretics, acid suppressants, antidiabetics, anaesthetics and the commonest drug-safety test in development, and their inherited defects are the channelopathies.

## Core tenets

1. **Three kinds of protein, three speeds, one direction unless energy is spent.** Channels are gated pores through which ions flow down their gradient at up to 10^8 per second; selectivity comes from a filter whose geometry matches one dehydrated ion (potassium passes, the smaller sodium does not, because the filter's oxygens replace exactly potassium's water shell). Carriers bind a solute and flip between inward- and outward-facing states, so they saturate like enzymes and move 10^2–10^4 per second; the renal glucose carriers saturate at a plasma glucose around 10 mmol/L, which is why diabetes spills glucose into urine. Pumps are carriers coupled to ATP hydrolysis and move cargo uphill.

2. **An ion's gradient is electrical as well as chemical, and the Nernst equation prices it.** The equilibrium potential at which the two balance is E = (RT/zF) ln([out]/[in]), about 61 mV per tenfold ratio at 37 °C: roughly −90 mV for potassium (4 mmol/L outside, 140 inside), +65 mV for sodium, +120 mV for calcium. A membrane's potential settles near the equilibrium potential of whichever ion it is most permeable to; at rest that is potassium, so cells sit at −70 to −90 mV. A gradient is stored free energy, available to any protein that can couple to it.

3. **The sodium–potassium pump builds the gradient that everything else spends.** It exports three sodium and imports two potassium per ATP and consumes 20–30% of resting energy, up to 70% in neurons. It does not itself create most of the resting potential (its direct contribution is a few millivolts); it creates the potassium gradient whose leak does. Digoxin inhibits it, raising intracellular sodium, which slows calcium export and strengthens the heartbeat. Insulin stimulates it, which is why insulin lowers plasma potassium and why its absence raises it. Other primary pumps include the gastric proton pump (blocked irreversibly by proton pump inhibitors), the calcium pumps of the reticulum and plasma membrane, and the ABC transporters, of which P-glycoprotein expels drugs from the gut, brain and tumour cells.

4. **Secondary active transport couples one downhill ion to another uphill cargo.** Sodium entering down its gradient drags glucose (SGLT1 in gut, SGLT2 in kidney), amino acids, phosphate, chloride (the NKCC2 cotransporter of the loop of Henle and the NCC of the distal tubule) or exchanges for protons and calcium. The loop and thiazide diuretics are inhibitors of named cotransporters, the SGLT2 inhibitors treat diabetes and heart failure, and oral rehydration solution works because cholera toxin leaves sodium–glucose cotransport intact: glucose pulls sodium and water back in.

5. **Water follows solute, and the pump is the osmotic engine.** Water crosses through aquaporins (the vasopressin-regulated aquaporin-2 sets urine concentration; its loss is nephrogenic diabetes insipidus) and by leak, always toward the side with more solute. A cell is full of impermeant anions and would swell and burst if sodium leaked in unchecked; the pump's continuous export is what holds cell volume. When ATP fails in ischaemia the pump stops, sodium and water enter, and cell swelling is the first visible sign of injury.

6. **An epithelium transports by putting different proteins on its two faces.** Tight junctions seal the gap between cells and separate an apical membrane facing the lumen from a basolateral membrane facing blood. Sodium enters the apical side through a cotransporter or channel and is pumped out of the basolateral side, so salt, and with it water, crosses the sheet; secretion runs the arrangement in reverse. The CFTR chloride channel on the apical face is what fails in cystic fibrosis, leaving secretions dehydrated, and what cholera toxin locks open through cyclic AMP, producing litres of secretory diarrhoea.

7. **Channels are gated, and their mutations and drugs are the channelopathies.** Gates respond to voltage, ligands, stretch or temperature. Local anaesthetics block sodium channels; benzodiazepines enhance the GABA-A chloride channel; calcium-channel blockers relax vessels; sulfonylureas close the ATP-sensitive potassium channel of the beta cell, the channel through which glucose metabolism normally triggers insulin release, so some neonatal diabetes due to that channel's mutation is treated with a tablet. Inherited variants in cardiac potassium and sodium channels cause the long QT syndromes, and because many unrelated drugs block the hERG potassium channel, every new drug is screened for it.

8. **Transporters are the pharmacokinetics of layer 09.** Uptake carriers in the liver (OATP1B1 for statins; a common variant causes simvastatin myopathy), efflux pumps at the blood–brain barrier, organic anion transporters that secrete penicillins and diuretics into urine, and the cation transporter that takes metformin into hepatocytes decide where a drug goes and how long it stays, and are the sites of many drug interactions.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Channel | Gated pore; passive, fast, selective by filter geometry |
| Carrier (transporter) | Protein that binds solute and alternates conformation; saturable |
| Primary active transport | Pumping directly coupled to ATP hydrolysis |
| Secondary active transport | Uphill transport powered by another solute's downhill gradient |
| Electrochemical gradient | Combined concentration and voltage driving force on an ion |
| Nernst (equilibrium) potential | Voltage at which an ion's net flux is zero |
| Pump–leak balance | Steady state between active export and passive entry that sets cell volume |
| Transcellular transport | Movement across an epithelium via distinct apical and basolateral proteins |
| Channelopathy | Disease caused by a mutated ion channel |

## Common misconceptions

- **"The sodium pump generates the resting potential."** It generates the gradients; the potential arises from potassium leaking down its gradient, and the pump's own electrogenic contribution is small.
- **"Water is pumped."** No water pump exists; water follows solute through aquaporins and leak, so all water movement is solute movement in disguise.
- **"Channels and transporters are the same thing."** A channel is an open pore and moves millions of ions a second; a carrier never forms a continuous pore and moves thousands.
