## Essence

A lipid bilayer lets almost nothing charged or polar through, so every ion, sugar and amino acid that enters or leaves a cell does so through a protein: a channel that opens a pore, a carrier that changes shape around its cargo, or a pump that spends Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP). One pump above all, the sodium–potassium ATPase, builds the sodium and potassium gradients across every cell membrane, and almost everything else is powered by letting those gradients run down in a controlled way: the resting potential, glucose uptake from the gut, salt reabsorption by the kidney, cell volume. The same proteins on opposite faces of an epithelium move substances across whole tissues. They are the targets of diuretics, acid suppressants, antidiabetics and anaesthetics, and their inherited defects are the channelopathies.

## Core tenets

1. **Three kinds of protein, three speeds, one direction unless energy is spent.** Channels are gated pores through which ions flow down their gradient at up to 10^8 per second; selectivity comes from a filter whose geometry matches one dehydrated ion (the potassium filter's oxygens replace exactly potassium's water shell, so the smaller sodium does not fit). Carriers bind a solute and flip between inward- and outward-facing states, so they saturate like enzymes and move 10^2–10^4 per second; the renal glucose carriers saturate at a plasma glucose near 10 mmol/L, which is why diabetes spills glucose into urine. Pumps are carriers coupled to ATP hydrolysis and move cargo uphill.

2. **An ion's gradient is electrical as well as chemical, and the Nernst equation prices it.** The equilibrium potential at which the two balance is E = (RT/zF) ln([out]/[in]), about 61 mV per tenfold ratio at 37 °C: roughly −90 mV for potassium (4 mmol/L outside, 140 inside), +65 mV for sodium, +120 mV for calcium. A membrane's potential settles near the equilibrium potential of the ion it is most permeable to; at rest that is potassium, so cells sit at −70 to −90 mV. A gradient is stored free energy.

3. **The sodium–potassium pump builds the gradient that everything else spends.** It exports three sodium and imports two potassium per ATP and consumes 20–30% of resting energy, up to 70% in neurons. It creates the potassium gradient whose leak sets the resting potential; its own direct contribution is a few millivolts. Digoxin inhibits it, raising intracellular sodium, which slows calcium export and strengthens the heartbeat. Insulin stimulates it, which is why insulin lowers plasma potassium. Other primary pumps include the gastric proton pump (blocked irreversibly by proton pump inhibitors), the calcium pumps, and the ABC transporters, of which P-glycoprotein expels drugs from gut, brain and tumour cells.

4. **Secondary active transport couples one downhill ion to another uphill cargo.** Sodium entering down its gradient drags glucose (Sodium–Glucose coTransporter 1 (SGLT1) in gut, SGLT2 in kidney), amino acids or chloride (Sodium–Potassium–2-Chloride cotransporter 2 (NKCC2) in the loop of Henle, Sodium–Chloride Cotransporter (NCC) in the distal tubule), or exchanges for protons and calcium. Loop and thiazide diuretics inhibit named cotransporters, SGLT2 inhibitors treat diabetes and heart failure, and oral rehydration solution works because cholera leaves sodium–glucose cotransport intact: glucose pulls sodium and water back in.

5. **Water follows solute, and the pump is the osmotic engine.** Water crosses through aquaporins (the vasopressin-regulated aquaporin-2 sets urine concentration) and by leak, always toward the side with more solute. A cell full of impermeant anions would swell and burst if sodium leaked in unchecked; the pump's continuous export holds cell volume. When ATP fails in ischaemia the pump stops, sodium and water enter, and swelling is the first visible sign of injury.

6. **An epithelium transports by putting different proteins on its two faces.** Tight junctions separate an apical membrane facing the lumen from a basolateral membrane facing blood. Sodium enters apically through a cotransporter or channel and is pumped out basolaterally, so salt and water cross the sheet; secretion runs the arrangement in reverse. The apical Cystic Fibrosis Transmembrane conductance Regulator (CFTR) chloride channel fails in cystic fibrosis, leaving secretions dehydrated, and is locked open by cholera toxin through cyclic Adenosine Monophosphate (AMP), producing litres of diarrhoea.

7. **Channels are gated, and their mutations and drugs are the channelopathies.** Local anaesthetics block sodium channels; benzodiazepines enhance the Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid (GABA)-A chloride channel; calcium-channel blockers relax vessels; sulfonylureas close the ATP-sensitive potassium channel through which glucose metabolism triggers insulin release, so neonatal diabetes from that channel's mutation is treated with a tablet. Variants in cardiac potassium and sodium channels cause the long QT syndromes, and because many unrelated drugs block the human Ether-à-go-go-Related Gene (hERG) potassium channel, every new drug is screened for it.

8. **Transporters are the pharmacokinetics of layer 09.** Hepatic uptake carriers (Organic Anion-Transporting Polypeptide 1B1 (OATP1B1) for statins; a common variant causes simvastatin myopathy), efflux pumps at the blood–brain barrier, and the renal transporters that secrete penicillins and diuretics decide where a drug goes and how long it stays, and are the sites of many interactions.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Channel | Gated pore; passive, fast, selective by filter geometry |
| Carrier (transporter) | Protein that binds solute and alternates conformation; saturable |
| Primary active transport | Pumping directly coupled to ATP hydrolysis |
| Secondary active transport | Uphill transport powered by another solute's downhill gradient |
| Electrochemical gradient | Combined concentration and voltage driving force on an ion |
| Nernst (equilibrium) potential | Voltage at which an ion's net flux is zero |
| Channelopathy | Disease caused by a mutated ion channel |

## Common misconceptions

- **"The sodium pump generates the resting potential."** It generates the gradients; the potential arises from potassium leaking down its gradient, and the pump's own electrogenic contribution is small.
- **"Water is pumped."** No water pump exists; water follows solute through aquaporins and leak, so all water movement is solute movement in disguise.
- **"Channels and transporters are the same thing."** A channel is an open pore and moves millions of ions a second; a carrier never forms a continuous pore and moves thousands.
