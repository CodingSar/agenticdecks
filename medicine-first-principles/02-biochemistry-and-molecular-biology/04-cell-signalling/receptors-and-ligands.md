## Essence

A receptor is a protein that binds a specific molecule and changes shape, and in that change a chemical message becomes a cellular response. Binding is reversible and obeys the law of mass action, so the fraction of receptors occupied depends on ligand concentration relative to the receptor's dissociation constant; activation is a separate property, because a ligand may bind and stabilise the active conformation (agonist), bind without doing so (antagonist), or do something in between. Four receptor families, each with its time scale, carry nearly all hormonal, neural and immune messages. About a third of approved drugs act on one family, the G protein-coupled receptors, and disorders of receptors explain diseases from familial hypercholesterolaemia to Graves' disease to myasthenia gravis.

## Core tenets

1. **Occupancy follows mass action, and affinity is tuned to the ligand's concentration.** For receptor R and ligand L, the dissociation constant KD = [R][L]/[RL], and occupancy = [L]/([L] + KD); KD is the concentration at which half the receptors are filled. Hormones circulate at picomolar to nanomolar concentrations and their receptors have KDs to match; neurotransmitters reach millimolar concentrations in a synapse for a millisecond, and their receptors have micromolar KDs so that they let go fast.

2. **Binding and activation are different things.** Receptors exist in an equilibrium of conformations. A full agonist shifts it strongly to the active state, a partial agonist weakly, an antagonist binds without shifting it and merely blocks, and an inverse agonist shifts it toward inactive, which matters for receptors with constitutive activity. Efficacy and affinity are independent, so a drug can bind tightly and do nothing, and a receptor can be fully activated at a fraction of occupancy if the cell has spare receptors. [Receptor theory](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/02-pharmacodynamics/receptor-theory-agonists-and-antagonists.md) quantifies this.

3. **Specificity is complementary shape and charge, and it is never perfect.** A pocket matches its ligand's shape, hydrogen-bond pattern and charge, so adrenaline and noradrenaline are distinguished by a single methyl group. But related receptors share pockets: a beta-1-selective blocker still binds beta-2 receptors at higher concentration, and any drug's selectivity is a ratio of affinities, not an absolute. Off-target binding is where most side effects come from.

4. **Four families, four time scales, predictable from the ligand's chemistry.** Ligand-gated ion channels (nicotinic acetylcholine, Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid (GABA)-A, glutamate receptors) open in a millisecond. G protein-coupled receptors (about 800 genes: adrenoceptors, muscarinic, opioid, dopamine, histamine, most peptide hormone receptors) act within seconds by swapping Guanosine Diphosphate (GDP) for Guanosine Triphosphate (GTP) on a G protein. Enzyme-linked receptors (insulin and growth factor receptor tyrosine kinases, cytokine receptors coupled to Janus Kinases (JAKs), the Transforming Growth Factor beta (TGF-beta) serine kinases) act over minutes to hours. Nuclear receptors for steroids, thyroid hormone, vitamin D and retinoids act over hours to days by changing transcription. Hydrophilic ligands cannot cross the membrane and use the first three; lipophilic ones diffuse in and use the fourth.

5. **Receptor number is regulated, so the response to a constant signal is not constant.** Prolonged agonist exposure causes internalisation and degradation of receptors (down-regulation), the basis of tolerance to opioids and beta-agonists; prolonged blockade raises receptor number, so stopping a beta-blocker abruptly produces rebound tachycardia. The cell measures change, not level.

6. **Receptor disease comes in three kinds.** Loss of function: a defective Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL) receptor causes familial hypercholesterolaemia, a defective vasopressin receptor nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, a defective androgen receptor androgen insensitivity. Gain of function: constitutively active growth-factor receptors (Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR) in lung cancer, Human Epidermal growth factor Receptor 2 (HER2) in breast cancer, KIT in gastrointestinal stromal tumours) and thyrotropin receptor in toxic thyroid adenoma. Autoantibodies acting as ligands: stimulating antibodies to the thyrotropin receptor cause Graves' disease, blocking and destroying antibodies to the acetylcholine receptor cause myasthenia gravis, and antibodies to the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) glutamate receptor cause an encephalitis. Pathogens use receptors as doors: Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) binds Cluster of Differentiation 4 (CD4) and CCR5, so people lacking CCR5 are resistant; Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) binds Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme 2 (ACE2).

7. **Receptor–ligand pairs are the main surface drugs act on.** Small molecules fit pockets on channels, G protein-coupled receptors and kinases; monoclonal antibodies block ligands (adalimumab binds Tumour Necrosis Factor (TNF)) or receptors (trastuzumab binds HER2; checkpoint inhibitors block Programmed cell Death protein 1 (PD-1) so tumours can no longer switch T cells off). Because tissues differ in which receptor subtypes they express, a drug's effects and side effects are a map of its target's distribution: a beta-agonist dilates bronchi and also races the heart.

8. **Not every receptor binds a molecule, and not every drug target is a receptor.** Mechanosensitive channels (Piezo) respond to pressure, and Transient Receptor Potential (TRP) channels to heat, cold, capsaicin and menthol; they are receptors for physical stimuli. Conversely, statins, Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme (ACE) inhibitors and antibiotics act on enzymes, SSRIs on a transporter, and taxanes on tubulin. The binding logic is the same; only the consequence differs.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ligand | Any molecule that binds a receptor: hormone, transmitter, drug, antibody |
| Dissociation constant (KD) | Ligand concentration at half occupancy; lower means higher affinity |
| Agonist, antagonist | Ligand that activates the receptor; ligand that binds without activating |
| Efficacy | A ligand's ability to shift the receptor toward its active conformation |
| G protein-coupled receptor | Seven-transmembrane receptor signalling through heterotrimeric G proteins |
| Receptor tyrosine kinase | Receptor whose cytoplasmic domain phosphorylates tyrosines when ligand binds |
| Nuclear receptor | Intracellular receptor that acts as a ligand-regulated transcription factor |
| Down-regulation | Reduction in receptor number after sustained stimulation |

## Common misconceptions

- **"An antagonist has the opposite effect to the agonist."** It has no effect of its own; it prevents the agonist's. Only an inverse agonist reduces a receptor's baseline activity.
- **"A selective drug acts only on its target."** Selectivity is a ratio; at a high enough concentration every drug reaches its second and third targets.
- **"More receptors always mean a bigger response."** Many cells have spare receptors, so adding more changes sensitivity to the ligand, not the maximal response.
