## Essence

Meals are intermittent and the brain's demand for glucose is continuous, so the body must store fuel after eating and release it in the right form between meals. The whole blood volume holds only about 4 g of glucose, enough for minutes, yet its concentration is held near 4–6 mmol/L through feasts and days of fasting. This is achieved by a small number of hormones, chiefly insulin against glucagon, that flip the direction of the pathways in the previous three leaves, by assigning each tissue a role, and by changing the fuel mix over hours to weeks so that glucose is reserved for the brain and protein is spared. Diabetes is what happens when the switch fails; starvation, critical illness and refeeding are what happen when the switch is driven to its limits.

## Core tenets

1. **One ratio sets the direction of every pathway.** Insulin, released when glucose rises, promotes glucose uptake, glycogen synthesis, lipogenesis and protein synthesis and suppresses lipolysis and hepatic glucose output. Glucagon, released when glucose falls, does the reverse in the liver. Both act largely through the phosphorylation state of the same handful of enzymes (glycogen synthase and phosphorylase, the bifunctional enzyme that makes fructose-2,6-bisphosphate, pyruvate kinase, acetyl-CoA carboxylase, hormone-sensitive lipase): glucagon phosphorylates via cyclic AMP, insulin dephosphorylates. One signal flips opposing pathways in opposite directions, so there are no futile cycles.

2. **Each tissue has a job.** The liver is the glucose buffer: it takes up glucose when high (glucokinase, high Km), stores it as glycogen, and releases it when low by glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, being the only major organ with glucose-6-phosphatase. Muscle keeps its glycogen for itself and exports lactate and alanine. Adipose tissue stores triglyceride and exports fatty acids and glycerol. The brain burns about 120 g of glucose a day, roughly a fifth of resting energy, and can switch partly to ketones. Red cells burn glucose to lactate. The kidney makes glucose in prolonged fasting.

3. **The fed state is about disposal.** A meal's glucose is cleared by insulin-driven translocation of the GLUT4 transporter to the surface of muscle and fat cells, by hepatic glycogen synthesis and, once glycogen is full, by conversion to fat. Amino acids are used for protein synthesis and the surplus deaminated. Dietary fat arrives in chylomicrons and is unloaded by insulin-activated lipoprotein lipase into adipocytes.

4. **Fasting unfolds in stages determined by what is left.** For the first hours after absorption the liver's ~100 g of glycogen holds glucose. By 12–24 hours it is nearly spent and gluconeogenesis from lactate, glycerol and amino acids takes over; early on this costs about 75 g of muscle protein a day. Over the first week ketogenesis rises, the brain takes most of its energy from ketones, and protein loss falls to about 20 g a day. Survival, typically six to ten weeks in a lean adult, is set by fat stores; death comes when protein loss reaches the respiratory muscles and heart.

5. **Gluconeogenesis is glycolysis run backwards at three bypasses, paid for by burning fat.** Pyruvate carboxylase and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase bypass pyruvate kinase; fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase bypasses phosphofructokinase; glucose-6-phosphatase bypasses hexokinase. Six ATP equivalents per glucose come from fatty-acid oxidation, and acetyl-CoA from that oxidation activates pyruvate carboxylase, so fat burning licenses sugar making. Acetyl-CoA itself cannot enter. Ethanol oxidation floods the liver with NADH and diverts pyruvate to lactate, which is why a fasting drinker becomes hypoglycaemic.

6. **Stress and injury impose a fasting-like state in the presence of food.** Cortisol, adrenaline, glucagon and inflammatory cytokines raise glucose output, induce insulin resistance and accelerate protein breakdown, so sepsis, trauma, burns and surgery produce hyperglycaemia and muscle wasting whatever is eaten. Refeeding a starved patient reverses this abruptly: insulin drives phosphate, potassium and magnesium into cells and demands thiamine, and the resulting hypophosphataemia can cause heart failure and death. This refeeding syndrome is the reason nutrition is restored slowly.

7. **Diabetes is the integration failing in two ways.** In type 1 there is no insulin, so a fed body behaves as if starving: unrestrained lipolysis and ketogenesis, hepatic glucose output, proteolysis, and glucose spilling into urine with its water. In type 2 insulin is present but tissues resist it, largely through ectopic fat and inflammation interfering with the insulin receptor's signal, and the beta cell eventually fails to compensate. Chronic hyperglycaemia then injures tissues by glycation, by overloading the sorbitol and hexosamine pathways and by mitochondrial oxidant production. Metformin lowers hepatic glucose output; drugs that make the kidney excrete glucose or that amplify the gut's insulin-releasing hormones work on other nodes of the same circuit.

8. **Whole-body energy balance is the same logic over months.** A 70-kg adult at rest spends about 7 MJ (1,700 kcal) a day; a sustained surplus of a few per cent accumulates as fat, and leptin from adipose tissue reports the store to the hypothalamus. Inside cells the same decision is taken by AMP-activated protein kinase (scarcity: oxidise) and mTOR (plenty: build), so the fed–fasted switch exists at every scale from enzyme to organism.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Insulin/glucagon ratio | The signal that sets storage versus mobilisation |
| GLUT4 | Insulin-responsive glucose transporter of muscle and fat |
| Glycogenolysis | Release of glucose from glycogen; hepatic in the first day of fasting |
| Gluconeogenesis | Glucose synthesis from lactate, glycerol and amino acids |
| Cori cycle | Lactate from muscle and red cells remade into glucose by the liver |
| Ketosis | Adaptive rise of ketone bodies in fasting; distinct from ketoacidosis |
| Insulin resistance | Reduced tissue response to insulin, typically from ectopic lipid and inflammation |
| Counter-regulatory hormones | Glucagon, adrenaline, cortisol, growth hormone: raise glucose |
| Refeeding syndrome | Electrolyte and thiamine crisis when insulin returns after starvation |

## Common misconceptions

- **"The brain can use only glucose."** After a few days of fasting ketones supply most of its energy; what it cannot use is fatty acid.
- **"Fasting burns muscle before fat."** Protein loss is highest in the first days and then falls sharply as ketosis spares it; fat is burned throughout.
- **"Insulin's job is to lower blood glucose."** That is one consequence; it is the general signal of plenty, suppressing lipolysis, ketogenesis and proteolysis and driving potassium into cells, which is why insulin treats hyperkalaemia.
