## Essence

A living cell is an open system held far from equilibrium, and bioenergetics is the accounting of how it pays to stay there. Energy enters as electrons in food, falls through a series of redox reactions to oxygen, and on the way is captured in two portable forms: the phosphoryl group of Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP), which drives synthesis, transport and movement, and the hydride carried by Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide (NAD) and its phosphate (NADP), which carries reducing power between pathways. The single idea is coupling: an unfavourable reaction is driven by sharing an intermediate with a favourable one, so free energy, not heat, is the currency. When oxygen fails, electron flow stops, ATP falls within minutes, and every ATP-dependent process fails with it.

## Core tenets

1. **Free energy, not energy, decides what happens, and concentrations matter as much as bonds.** For any reaction ΔG = ΔG°' + RT ln Q, where Q is the actual ratio of products to reactants. The standard free energy of ATP hydrolysis is about −30.5 kJ/mol, but a cell holds the ratio [ATP]/([Adenosine Diphosphate (ADP)][Pi]) some 10^8-fold away from equilibrium, so the real ΔG is about −50 to −60 kJ/mol. ATP is useful because the cell keeps it displaced, not because its bonds are special.

2. **Coupling through common intermediates makes the unfavourable favourable.** Glutamine synthesis from glutamate and ammonia is uphill (ΔG°' +14 kJ/mol); the enzyme first phosphorylates glutamate with ATP, and the phosphorylated intermediate reacts downhill, net −16 kJ/mol. Every biosynthesis, pump and motor is a variation: ATP transfers a group whose departure drags the reaction forward. ATP hydrolysed in isolation yields only heat.

3. **ATP sits in the middle of a phosphoryl-transfer hierarchy, which is why it is the currency.** Phosphoenolpyruvate (−62 kJ/mol), 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate (−49) and phosphocreatine (−43) can donate a phosphoryl group to ADP; ATP (−30.5) in turn donates to glucose (−14). Being intermediate lets ATP be both made and spent. The pool is small, roughly 50 g in an adult, and turns over so fast that a resting human regenerates about its own body weight of ATP each day.

4. **ATP hydrolysis is favourable for chemical reasons and slow for kinetic ones.** The phosphates repel each other and the products are better stabilised by resonance and solvation. Yet ATP in water lasts for days because the barrier is high; only an enzyme releases the free energy, so the cell decides where and when. Glucose and oxygen in a flask are the same: thermodynamically a bomb, kinetically inert.

5. **Redox carriers move electrons in pairs, and the cell keeps two separate pools.** NAD+ accepts a hydride (two electrons and a proton) from a substrate being oxidised; the cytosolic NAD+/NADH ratio is kept highly oxidised (~500–1000) so that catabolic dehydrogenations run downhill. NADP+/NADPH is kept highly reduced (~0.01) so that biosynthesis and antioxidant defence can run downhill in the opposite direction. One extra phosphate lets enzymes tell the pools apart; without it, a cell could not oxidise glucose and reduce fatty-acid precursors in the same cytosol. Flavins (Flavin Adenine Dinucleotide (FAD), Flavin Mononucleotide (FMN)) are protein-bound and can take electrons one at a time.

6. **The energy of a redox reaction is set by the difference in reduction potential.** ΔG°' = −nFΔE°'. NADH (E°' −0.32 V) donating two electrons to oxygen (+0.82 V) releases about −220 kJ/mol, enough for several ATP. Oxygen is the terminal acceptor because it has the highest potential available, which is why aerobic metabolism yields about fifteen times more ATP per glucose than fermentation. Without oxygen NADH accumulates, and the only way to regenerate NAD+ is to dump electrons onto pyruvate, making lactate: a rising lactate is the biochemical signature of tissue hypoxia in [shock](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/hemorrhage-shock-and-sepsis.md).

7. **Chemiosmosis stores redox energy as a proton gradient.** Mitchell (1961) proposed, against most opinion, that the respiratory chain pumps protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane and that ATP synthase lets them back through a rotary motor; the proton-motive force is about 200 mV (around 20 kJ/mol of protons). Anything that lets protons leak back past the motor (dinitrophenol, high-dose salicylate, the uncoupling protein of brown fat) converts food energy to heat: how newborns keep warm and how uncouplers kill.

8. **The cell senses its energy state through ratios, and electron flow leaks.** Adenylate kinase keeps 2 ADP in equilibrium with ATP + Adenosine Monophosphate (AMP), so AMP rises steeply when ATP falls slightly; AMP-activated protein kinase reads it and switches off synthesis, switches on oxidation. About 1–2% of electrons escape the chain to oxygen prematurely, forming superoxide; the defences that remove it run on NADPH, so a red cell that cannot make NADPH (glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency) haemolyses when an oxidant drug or infection raises the load.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ΔG, ΔG°' | Free-energy change under actual conditions; under standard biochemical conditions (pH 7) |
| Coupling | Linking an uphill reaction to a downhill one through a shared intermediate |
| Phosphoryl-transfer potential | How strongly a phosphorylated compound donates its phosphoryl group |
| Reduction potential (E°') | Tendency to accept electrons; electrons flow from low to high |
| NAD+/NADH | Catabolic electron carrier, kept oxidised |
| NADP+/NADPH | Anabolic and antioxidant electron carrier, kept reduced |
| Proton-motive force | Electrochemical proton gradient across a membrane that drives ATP synthase |
| Uncoupler | Agent that dissipates the proton gradient as heat |

## Common misconceptions

- **"ATP stores energy in a high-energy bond."** The free energy comes from the whole reaction, products included, and from how far the cell holds it from equilibrium; the phosphoanhydride bond is not unusually strong.
- **"Cells stockpile ATP."** The pool lasts seconds; what is stored is glycogen, fat and phosphocreatine, and ATP is regenerated continuously.
- **"Energy is used up."** Energy is conserved; what is consumed is free energy, degraded to heat and entropy, which is why metabolism warms the body.
