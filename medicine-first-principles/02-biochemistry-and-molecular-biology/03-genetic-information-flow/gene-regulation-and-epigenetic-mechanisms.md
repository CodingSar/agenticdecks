## Essence

Every nucleated cell in a body carries the same genome, so what makes a neuron differ from a hepatocyte is which genes are read, how much, and when. Regulation acts at every step from chromatin to protein degradation, but the cheapest and most used lever is the start of transcription, where combinations of transcription factors bind short sequences near and far from a gene and recruit or repel the polymerase. Epigenetic mechanisms, chemical marks on DNA and histones that are copied when the cell divides, give this control a memory, so a cell's identity persists without any change in sequence. The same marks go wrong in cancer, mediate the slow action of steroid hormones, and are now read from blood as a diagnostic.

## Core tenets

1. **Identity is a pattern of expression, maintained by feedback.** About 1,600 human genes encode transcription factors, and a cell type is defined by the set that is active. Factors that activate their own genes create positive feedback, which makes a state self-sustaining: once a muscle programme is switched on by MyoD it stays on. That stability is why differentiated cells rarely change type, and why forcing four factors into a fibroblast (Takahashi and Yamanaka, 2006) can nonetheless reset it to a pluripotent stem cell: the network has several stable states and a large enough push moves it between them.

2. **Specificity comes from combinations, not from single factors.** A transcription factor recognises a short, degenerate motif of 6–12 base pairs that occurs by chance thousands of times in the genome; specificity arises because a gene fires only when several factors bind together at an enhancer and interact cooperatively. Cooperative binding also makes the response to a factor's concentration sigmoidal, so a graded signal (a morphogen gradient in the embryo) is converted into sharp on/off boundaries.

3. **Chromatin decides what is reachable.** DNA wrapped in nucleosomes is largely inaccessible; remodelling complexes slide or evict nucleosomes, and enzymes add or remove marks on histone tails that are read by other proteins. Acetylation neutralises lysine's positive charge, loosens DNA–histone contact and marks active genes; trimethylation of lysine 27 on histone H3 by Polycomb complexes marks silenced developmental genes; lysine 9 methylation marks permanently compacted heterochromatin. The marks are copied, imperfectly, through division, so chromatin is the cell's record of signals it received long ago.

4. **DNA methylation is the most stable mark, and it is inherited through mitosis.** Cytosine in CpG pairs is methylated by methyltransferases; a maintenance enzyme recognises the half-methylated DNA after replication and copies the pattern, which is what makes methylation epigenetic in the strict sense. Methylated promoters are silenced. It silences one X chromosome in every female cell (so carriers of X-linked disease are mosaics), silences transposons, and marks about a hundred genes by parent of origin (imprinting), so that losing the paternal copy of one region gives Prader–Willi syndrome and losing the maternal copy gives Angelman syndrome. Methylated cytosine deaminates readily to thymine, which is why CpG is both rare in the genome and a mutational hotspot.

5. **Cancer is epigenetic as well as genetic.** Tumours commonly show genome-wide loss of methylation with dense methylation and silencing of specific tumour suppressors; in colorectal cancer a methylated mismatch-repair gene causes microsatellite instability as surely as a mutation would. Mutations in the writers and erasers of marks (IDH, DNMT3A, TET2, EZH2, SWI/SNF subunits) are drivers in leukaemias and solid tumours. Hypomethylating agents (azacitidine) treat myelodysplasia, methylation patterns classify brain tumours, and cell-free DNA methylation identifies a tumour's tissue of origin in blood. A methylation clock also tracks biological age.

6. **Nuclear receptors are transcription factors with a ligand pocket, which explains the time course of steroid hormones.** Steroids, thyroid hormone, vitamin D and retinoic acid are lipid-soluble, cross the membrane and bind receptors that then bind DNA and recruit co-activators or co-repressors. Their effects take hours to days and persist after the hormone has gone, because the changed protein population takes time to turn over; a glucocorticoid's anti-inflammatory effect is largely transrepression of the inflammatory factor NF-kappaB. Tamoxifen blocks the oestrogen receptor in breast tissue and activates it in bone, because co-regulator availability differs by cell.

7. **Signals end at transcription factors, and oxygen is a worked example.** Most signalling cascades terminate in the phosphorylation, release or stabilisation of a factor (CREB, STAT, NF-kappaB, beta-catenin, SMAD). Hypoxia-inducible factor is hydroxylated by oxygen-dependent enzymes and destroyed when oxygen is adequate; when it falls, the factor survives and switches on erythropoietin, glycolytic enzymes and vessel growth. Drugs that inhibit the hydroxylases treat renal anaemia; tumours exploit the same switch.

8. **Control continues after transcription.** MicroRNAs, about 22 nucleotides long, pair imperfectly with hundreds of target mRNAs each and dampen their translation, buffering noise and sharpening transitions. RNA-binding proteins read cell state: when iron is low, iron-regulatory proteins bind ferritin mRNA to block its translation and transferrin-receptor mRNA to stabilise it, a complete homeostatic circuit in two mRNAs. Small interfering RNA drugs (inclisiran against PCSK9, patisiran against transthyretin) use the same machinery to silence a chosen gene for months.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Transcription factor | DNA-binding protein that raises or lowers a gene's transcription |
| Enhancer | Cluster of factor-binding sites that controls a gene from a distance |
| Cooperativity | Mutual stabilisation of bound factors; produces switch-like responses |
| Histone modification | Acetyl, methyl or other marks on histone tails that alter accessibility and recruit readers |
| Polycomb | Complexes that silence developmental genes via H3K27 methylation |
| CpG methylation | Methyl group on cytosine in CpG; heritable through replication; silences promoters |
| Imprinting | Parent-of-origin-specific silencing of a gene by methylation |
| X inactivation | Silencing of one X chromosome per female cell; makes females mosaic |
| Nuclear receptor | Ligand-activated transcription factor for steroids and related hormones |
| MicroRNA | Small RNA that represses translation of partially complementary mRNAs |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Epigenetics means the environment changes your genes."** The sequence is unchanged; what changes is heritable marks on it. In mammals most marks are erased between generations, and evidence for transgenerational epigenetic inheritance in humans is weak.
- **"Genes are on or off."** Expression is graded and bursty, and single-cell measurements show continua of states, which is one reason inherited mutations have variable penetrance.
- **"A transcription factor controls one gene."** Each binds hundreds to thousands of sites; the outcome at any gene depends on the combination present.
