## Essence

Transcription copies a gene into Ribonucleic Acid (RNA), a disposable working copy that can be made in whatever amount the cell needs and discarded when it is not. In eukaryotes the primary transcript is then capped, spliced and tailed before it leaves the nucleus, and splicing is where a single gene becomes many proteins and where a surprising fraction of disease-causing mutations act. Bacterial and human polymerases differ, so transcription is an antibiotic target; messenger RNA (mRNA) can be made in a factory, so it has become a drug and a vaccine; and the transcriptome reports what a cell is doing, so it is read to classify tumours and quantify viruses.

## Core tenets

1. **RNA polymerase copies the template strand without a primer, at lower fidelity than DNA polymerase, and initiation is the controlled step.** The enzyme reads the template 3'→5' and makes RNA 5'→3' at about 20–50 nucleotides a second with roughly one error in 10^4–10^5, tolerable because many copies are made and none is inherited. Eukaryotes use three polymerases: I for ribosomal RNA, II for mRNA and most regulatory RNAs, III for transfer RNA and small RNAs. Bacteria use one, whose beta subunit rifampicin blocks selectively; the death cap mushroom's alpha-amanitin blocks human polymerase II, which is why it destroys the liver.

2. **Promoters start transcription; enhancers decide how often, from a distance.** A promoter is where general transcription factors position the polymerase. Enhancers are short sequences bound by regulatory proteins that can lie hundreds of kilobases away and act by looping the Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) to the promoter, which is why a rearrangement that moves an enhancer next to the wrong gene (MYC under an immunoglobulin enhancer in Burkitt lymphoma) causes cancer.

3. **A cap and a tail mark an mRNA as finished and as self.** The 5' end receives a 7-methylguanosine cap that protects it from nucleases, directs it out of the nucleus and recruits the ribosome. The 3' end is cut and given a poly(A) tail that governs stability and translation. Viral RNAs lacking the cap are recognised by innate sensors, so capping is also a self/non-self signal.

4. **Splicing removes introns by RNA catalysis and must be exact.** Human genes average eight or nine exons separated by introns that make up around 90% of the primary transcript. The spliceosome, five small nuclear RNAs with proteins, recognises the GU at the start, the AG at the end and a branch-point adenosine and performs two transesterifications that release the intron as a lariat; its catalytic core is RNA. A mutation at a splice site shifts the reading frame or deletes an exon, and such mutations account for roughly 10–15% of disease-causing variants, including common forms of beta-thalassaemia and cystic fibrosis.

5. **Alternative splicing multiplies the proteome and is druggable.** About 95% of multi-exon human genes are spliced in more than one way, so some 20,000 genes yield well over 100,000 proteins, chosen by tissue-specific splicing regulators that bind enhancer and silencer sequences within the transcript. In spinal muscular atrophy the SMN1 gene is lost and the near-identical SMN2 skips exon 7 because of a single-base difference; nusinersen is an antisense oligonucleotide that blocks the silencer and restores inclusion, and risdiplam is a small molecule that does the same.

6. **Modified bases change how RNA is read by the cell and by the immune system.** Adenosine-to-inosine editing marks endogenous double-stranded RNA as self; its loss triggers an interferon disease. Replacing uridine with N1-methylpseudouridine in synthetic mRNA suppresses innate recognition and increases translation (Karikó and Weissman, 2005), the modification that made mRNA vaccines workable.

7. **An mRNA's lifetime is regulated, and nonsense codons shorten it.** Human mRNAs have half-lives from minutes to a day; bacterial ones last minutes. Nonsense-mediated decay destroys transcripts with a premature stop codon more than about 50 nucleotides upstream of the last exon junction, so most nonsense mutations yield no protein at all rather than a truncated one; a truncating mutation in the last exon escapes decay, and the shortened protein may act dominantly. The rule predicts whether a mutation causes loss or gain of function.

8. **The transcriptome is a readout of cell state.** Ribosomal RNA is about 80% of a cell's RNA; transfer, small nuclear, micro- and long non-coding RNAs make up most of the rest, and about three quarters of the genome is transcribed at some point. Measuring which mRNAs a cell contains, by reverse transcription and amplification or by sequencing, identifies fusion transcripts (BCR-ABL in chronic myeloid leukaemia), classifies tumours by expression pattern and prognosis, and quantifies viral load in blood.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Promoter | Sequence where polymerase and general factors assemble; sets the start site |
| Enhancer | Distant regulatory sequence that raises transcription by DNA looping |
| 5' cap, poly(A) tail | Modifications marking a mature mRNA; protect, export, recruit ribosomes |
| Intron, exon | Removed and retained segments of the primary transcript |
| Spliceosome | RNA–protein machine that excises introns by two transesterifications |
| Alternative splicing | Producing different mRNAs from one gene by exon choice |
| Antisense oligonucleotide | Synthetic nucleic acid that binds an RNA to alter splicing or trigger degradation |
| Nonsense-mediated decay | Destruction of mRNAs bearing a premature stop codon |

## Common misconceptions

- **"One gene, one protein."** Alternative splicing, alternative promoters and alternative poly(A) sites give most human genes several products.
- **"Introns are junk."** They carry splicing signals and enhancers, allow exon shuffling in evolution, and their misreading is a major cause of genetic disease.
- **"A nonsense mutation produces a truncated protein."** Usually the mRNA is destroyed and no protein is made; truncated proteins appear only when the stop codon is near the end.
