## Essence

A physical sign is a change in the body that an examiner can detect with the senses, sometimes aided by a simple instrument. Each sign is the surface expression of a mechanism: a raised jugular venous pressure is right atrial pressure transmitted up a valveless vein, a heart murmur is turbulent flow across a narrowed or leaking valve, dullness to percussion is fluid or solid where air should be. The examination is therefore a set of physical measurements made with hands, eyes and ears, and like any test each sign has a sensitivity, a specificity and a likelihood ratio. Its value is that it is immediate, repeatable, free and available anywhere, and its limit is that most signs are insensitive, many are unreliable between observers, and few are decisive alone.

## Core tenets

1. **Every sign has a physical mechanism, and knowing it tells you when the sign will be present and when it will be absent.** Oedema pits because interstitial fluid can be displaced; it collects at the ankles because hydrostatic pressure is highest there ([fluids and pressure](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/fluids-pressure-and-flow.md)). Cyanosis appears when more than roughly 50 g/L of haemoglobin is deoxygenated, so an anaemic patient can be severely hypoxaemic without it. A sign absent for a mechanical reason (the anaemic patient, the obese abdomen that cannot be palpated) carries no information.

2. **Inspection, palpation, percussion and auscultation are four physical measurements.** Inspection reads colour, shape, movement and effort. Palpation measures temperature, texture, tenderness, size and pulsation. Percussion sends a sound wave into the body and reads the resonance: air resonates, fluid and solid do not ([waves and sound](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/waves-sound-light-and-radiation.md)). Auscultation listens to the sounds of flow and of air moving through tubes, so heart sounds, murmurs, breath sounds and bowel sounds are all acoustics of flow. Laennec's stethoscope (1816) was the first diagnostic instrument, and the examination's physics has not changed since.

3. **Vital signs are the examination's quantitative core and the most powerful predictors.** Pulse rate and rhythm, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature, oxygen saturation and conscious level are numbers with reference ranges and well-studied prognostic value. Respiratory rate is the most neglected and the most predictive of deterioration; early-warning scores built on vital signs are the prediction models of the ward. Where the rest of the examination asks "what is it," the vital signs ask "how sick is this person now," which is the question the [ABCDE approach](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/triage-and-the-abcde-approach.md) puts first.

4. **Signs have likelihood ratios, and most of them are modest.** Evidence-based physical diagnosis has measured hundreds of signs against reference standards. A few are strong: a third heart sound for raised ventricular filling pressure, asymmetric calf swelling for venous thrombosis, a particular pattern of abdominal tenderness for peritonitis. Many beloved signs have likelihood ratios near 1 and discriminate little. Treating each sign as a [test with a likelihood ratio](using-tests-pretest-probability-and-thresholds.md) rather than as a verdict is what distinguishes examination from ritual.

5. **Inter-observer agreement limits the sign before accuracy does.** Two experienced clinicians asked whether a murmur is present, a liver is enlarged or a chest is dull often disagree, and the kappa statistic (agreement beyond chance) for many signs is poor. A sign that observers cannot agree on cannot have a stable likelihood ratio. Signs with a physical anchor (a measured pulse, a palpable mass of stated size) are more reliable than graded impressions (mild versus moderate).

6. **The examination is hypothesis-driven, and the screening examination is a different instrument.** After a history, the examination tests specific predictions: if this is heart failure, the venous pressure should be raised and there should be crackles at the lung bases. Each finding, present or absent, updates the probability. The head-to-toe screening examination of a patient without complaints has low yield for most findings and is mostly useful for the vital signs, for conditions with silent signs (hypertension, a mass, a skin lesion) and for establishing a baseline.

7. **Some signs are patterns that name a syndrome, and some are the whole of the specialty.** Clustered findings carry more information than any one: the triad of fever, neck stiffness and altered consciousness; the cerebellar signs that localise a lesion; the pattern of weakness, reflexes and sensory loss that separates nerve from cord from brain. In [dermatology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/11-dermatology/skin-lesion-morphology-and-diagnosis.md), neurology and the assessment of [frailty](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/geriatric-syndromes.md), the examination remains the primary diagnostic instrument rather than a prelude to imaging.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sign | An objective finding detected by the examiner, as distinct from a symptom reported by the patient |
| Vital signs | Pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature, oxygen saturation, conscious level |
| Inspection, palpation, percussion, auscultation | The four modes of examination: looking, feeling, tapping for resonance, listening |
| Likelihood ratio of a sign | How much a sign's presence or absence shifts the odds of the target condition |
| Inter-observer agreement (kappa) | Agreement between examiners beyond that expected by chance |
| Pathognomonic sign | A sign so specific that its presence alone establishes the diagnosis; rare |
| Red flag sign | A finding that greatly raises the probability of serious disease and demands action |
| Early-warning score | A points system built on vital signs that predicts deterioration |
| Syndrome | A recognisable cluster of symptoms and signs that may have several causes |

- **Above:** the findings feed [clinical reasoning](clinical-reasoning-and-cognitive-bias.md); vital signs are the first step of [triage](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/triage-and-the-abcde-approach.md); every specialty leaf names the signs its diseases produce, and [dermatology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/11-dermatology/skin-lesion-morphology-and-diagnosis.md) and [geriatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/geriatric-syndromes.md) depend on examination above all.

## Common misconceptions

- **"If the sign is absent, the disease is absent."**: most signs are insensitive; a normal examination lowers probability only by the negative likelihood ratio, which for many signs is close to 1.
- **"A classical sign is a reliable one."**: many eponymous signs were described in single cases and have never been measured; several have likelihood ratios near 1 when tested.
- **"Imaging has made examination obsolete."**: imaging answers the question examination poses; without a hypothesis and a pre-test probability, an incidental finding on a scan cannot be interpreted, and the vital signs still decide who needs the scan first.
