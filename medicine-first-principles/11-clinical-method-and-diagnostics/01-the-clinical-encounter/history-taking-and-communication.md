## Essence

The history is the patient's account of what has happened to them, obtained and structured by the clinician. It is the most informative test in medicine: in studies of outpatients, the diagnosis was evident from the history alone in roughly three quarters of cases, with examination and investigations serving mostly to confirm it. It works because symptoms are the output of pathophysiology filtered through a person's attention, memory and language, so each feature of a symptom (site, onset, character, timing, what provokes and relieves it) is a probabilistic clue to a mechanism. The encounter is also where trust, consent and the patient's own goals are established, so the history is simultaneously a data-gathering instrument and a relationship.

## Core tenets

1. **A symptom is a signal from a mechanism, and its features encode which mechanism.** Pain from a hollow organ obstructing is colicky because peristalsis is periodic; pain from an inflamed peritoneum is constant and worse on movement because the parietal peritoneum is somatically innervated ([pain](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/pain.md)). Breathlessness on lying flat points to pulmonary venous congestion; breathlessness with a wheeze to airway narrowing. The clinician asks about each dimension of a symptom (onset, duration, character, radiation, associations, timing, exacerbating and relieving factors, severity) because each dimension discriminates between mechanisms.

2. **The time course is the single most discriminating feature.** Onset over seconds means something mechanical or electrical (occlusion, rupture, arrhythmia); over minutes to hours, inflammation or infection beginning; over weeks to months, growth or degeneration; episodic with full recovery between, a reversible process. The same chest pain has a different differential at each tempo. This is why the history dwells on "when exactly did it start, and what has it done since," and why the [natural history](../../08-pathology/05-disease-as-process/etiology-pathogenesis-and-natural-history.md) of each disease is learned as a time course.

3. **Open questions first, then closed; the order changes what you learn.** An open invitation ("tell me what has been happening") lets the patient volunteer the features they noticed, which are less contaminated by the clinician's hypothesis. Closed questions then test specific hypotheses, and are prone to leading: a patient asked "is the pain crushing?" tends to agree. Interrupting early truncates the account; studies have found clinicians interrupt within about twenty seconds, and that uninterrupted patients rarely talk for more than two minutes.

4. **The history is the patient's reconstruction, with the errors of memory and meaning built in.** Recall is reshaped by what has happened since and by what the patient thinks matters. Words mean different things ("dizzy" covers vertigo, presyncope and unsteadiness, three different systems). Symptoms are under-reported when embarrassing (alcohol, continence, sexual function) and over-weighted when frightening. The clinician's task is to translate the account into physiological terms by asking what the word means to this patient, and to recognise that the account itself is data about the patient's beliefs and fears.

5. **Each part of the structured history is a different kind of prior.** Past history and family history shift the base rate of each diagnosis; drug history supplies candidates for adverse effects and interactions; social history (occupation, housing, smoking, alcohol, who is at home) supplies exposures and the context in which any plan must work; a systems review catches what the presenting complaint hid. The structure exists so that nothing that changes probability is left unasked, not as a form to be completed.

6. **The patient's ideas, concerns and expectations are part of the data and part of the decision.** What the patient fears the symptom means decides what reassurance would require; what they expect from the visit decides whether the plan will be followed. Eliciting these is not courtesy but method: the disease the patient fears is often the one that explains why they came today rather than last week, and a plan the patient does not agree with is not a plan.

7. **Communication determines what the patient tells you and what they do afterwards.** Rapport, eye contact, listening without interruption, summarising back and checking understanding each measurably increase the information obtained and the adherence that follows. Breaking bad news, disagreeing with a request, and discussing uncertainty are skills with learnable structure. Communication failure is the commonest root of complaints and a frequent root of error, because the information was available and was not transferred, which [patient safety](../../15-medicine-in-society/01-health-systems/patient-safety-and-quality-improvement.md) studies repeatedly.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Presenting complaint | The symptom or problem that brought the patient, in their words |
| History of presenting complaint | The structured characterisation of each symptom: site, onset, character, radiation, associations, timing, exacerbating and relieving factors, severity |
| Past medical, drug, family and social history | The components that shift base rates and supply exposures and context |
| Systems review | Screening questions across organ systems for symptoms not volunteered |
| Open and closed questions | Invitations to narrate versus tests of a specific hypothesis |
| Ideas, concerns and expectations | The patient's own understanding of the illness and what they want from the encounter |
| Collateral history | Account from a relative, carer or witness when the patient cannot give one |
| Red flag | A feature in the history that markedly raises the probability of serious disease |

## How it fits

- **Below:** the history is a noisy channel from mechanism to clinician ([signals and noise](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/03-systems-and-models/information-signals-and-noise.md)); each feature is evidence that updates a probability ([Bayesian reasoning](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/01-probability-and-measurement/bayesian-reasoning.md)); the symptom patterns themselves come from physiology and the [natural history](../../08-pathology/05-disease-as-process/etiology-pathogenesis-and-natural-history.md) of disease.
- **Above:** the history generates the hypotheses that [clinical reasoning](clinical-reasoning-and-cognitive-bias.md) tests; the [mental state examination](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/03-psychiatry/the-mental-state-and-psychiatric-formulation.md) is a history in which the manner of telling is itself the finding; the generalist's long relationship in [family medicine](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/family-medicine-and-the-generalist.md) accumulates a history over years; [consent](../../15-medicine-in-society/02-ethics-law-and-the-profession/consent-capacity-and-confidentiality.md) is obtained through the same conversation.
- **Siblings:** [physical examination](physical-examination-and-signs.md) tests the hypotheses the history produced; [tests](using-tests-pretest-probability-and-thresholds.md) start from the probability the history set; [shared decision-making](prognosis-and-shared-decision-making.md) returns to the patient's values elicited here.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The history is the soft part; the tests are the real data."**: the history settles most diagnoses and sets the pre-test probability without which no test result can be interpreted.
- **"A good history is a complete history."**: a history is good when it discriminates between the hypotheses that matter for this patient; completeness without direction is a form that nobody reads.
- **"Patients report symptoms accurately if asked."**: recall, meaning and embarrassment all distort the account; the clinician's questions shape the answers.
