## Essence

Clinical reasoning is the process by which a clinician moves from a patient's presentation to a working diagnosis and a decision, under uncertainty and usually under time pressure. Formally it is Bayesian: a set of candidate diagnoses with prior probabilities set by prevalence and the patient's features, updated by each finding according to how much more likely that finding is under one hypothesis than another. In practice it is performed by two cognitive systems: fast pattern recognition that matches the case to stored illness scripts, and slow analytic reasoning that generates and tests hypotheses deliberately. Expertise lies mostly in the first; safety lies in knowing when to switch to the second, because the fast system's shortcuts are also its characteristic errors.

## Core tenets

1. **Diagnosis is probability revision, whether or not the clinician does the arithmetic.** Each diagnosis starts with a prior (how common is this in a person like this, presenting like this) and each finding multiplies the odds by its likelihood ratio ([Bayesian reasoning](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/01-probability-and-measurement/bayesian-reasoning.md)). A finding is useful in proportion to how differently the competing hypotheses predict it, not to how dramatic it is. This is why the same chest pain is treated differently in a 25-year-old and a 65-year-old smoker: the data are the same, the priors are not.

2. **Hypothesis generation happens early and limits everything after.** Experienced clinicians form two to five candidate diagnoses within the first minute and spend the rest of the encounter testing them. A diagnosis that was never generated cannot be confirmed by any later finding; most missed diagnoses were never on the list. The differential is therefore deliberately widened by structure: "what is the most likely, what is the most dangerous, what else could this be," and by organising candidates by mechanism (vascular, infective, inflammatory, neoplastic, metabolic, structural) rather than recalling them by name.

3. **Pattern recognition is fast, accurate for typical cases, and the main mode of expert practice.** Experts store illness scripts: compiled representations of a disease's typical patient, presentation, course and findings. A new case is matched against them in parallel, which is why an expert "just knows." The accuracy of this mode depends entirely on the breadth and calibration of the stored scripts, which is what years of varied cases build, and it fails for atypical presentations, rare diseases and cases outside the clinician's experience.

4. **Analytic reasoning is slow, effortful and the only defence when the pattern is wrong.** Explicit listing of hypotheses, estimation of probabilities, deliberate search for disconfirming features and consideration of base rates can catch what pattern recognition missed. It is too slow for routine use and too demanding under fatigue or interruption, so it must be triggered: by atypical features, by a finding that does not fit, by failure to improve, by high stakes, or by a forced pause. Diagnostic checklists and "diagnostic time-outs" are devices to trigger it.

5. **The heuristics that make fast reasoning possible produce predictable errors.** *Anchoring*: fixing on the first diagnosis and adjusting insufficiently. *Confirmation bias*: seeking and weighting findings that fit it. *Premature closure*: stopping the search once a plausible answer is found; the commonest cognitive cause of diagnostic error in reviews of cases. *Availability*: judging a diagnosis likely because a recent or vivid case comes to mind. *Representativeness*: matching to the typical picture and neglecting base rates, so a rare disease with a classic presentation is overdiagnosed and a common disease presenting atypically is missed. *Diagnostic momentum*: a label attached by one clinician is carried forward unexamined by the next.

6. **Good reasoning tolerates uncertainty and plans for being wrong.** Not every encounter yields a diagnosis; often the valid output is a probability, a safety net and a plan to review. Stating uncertainty explicitly ("most likely X, but Y has not been excluded; return if Z") is more accurate than a confident label and is the basis of safe discharge. The clinician who can say "I do not yet know" and specify what would change their mind is reasoning well; the one who cannot has closed prematurely.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Differential diagnosis | The set of candidate diagnoses under consideration, ideally ranked by probability and danger |
| Illness script | A stored representation of a disease's typical presentation, used for pattern matching |
| Dual-process reasoning | Fast, intuitive pattern recognition (System 1) alongside slow, analytic reasoning (System 2) |
| Anchoring | Over-reliance on an initial impression, with insufficient adjustment |
| Confirmation bias | Seeking or weighting evidence that supports the current hypothesis |
| Premature closure | Accepting a diagnosis before it has been adequately verified |
| Availability and representativeness | Judging likelihood by ease of recall, or by resemblance to a typical case, neglecting base rates |
| Diagnostic momentum | A provisional label becomes fixed as it passes between clinicians |
| Safety-netting | Explicit instructions on what should prompt the patient to return, and planned review |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Experts reason more carefully than novices."**: experts mostly reason faster, by pattern, and are right more often because their patterns are better; they are not immune to the biases, and over-confidence rises with experience.
- **"Ordering more tests compensates for uncertain reasoning."**: a test ordered without a hypothesis and a pre-test probability cannot be interpreted, and incidental findings generate new uncertainty.
- **"Knowing the list of biases prevents them."**: awareness alone has little effect; what helps is structure (differentials organised by mechanism, forced pauses, checklists, second opinions) and feedback on outcomes.
