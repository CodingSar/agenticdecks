# Prognosis and shared decision-making

## Essence

Diagnosis says what is wrong; prognosis says what will happen, with and without each possible action. It is a probability statement about the future, derived from cohorts of similar patients and adjusted to the person in front of you. Shared decision-making is what a clinician and patient do with that statement: the clinician supplies the options and their probable outcomes, the patient supplies what those outcomes are worth to them, and the decision is the product of both. The idea that unites prognosis with decision is that, once the evidence is in, the right choice between options depends on values the evidence cannot supply; and the idea that makes prognosis hard is that a population probability must be conveyed honestly to one person who will experience only one outcome.

## Core tenets

1. **Prognosis is a conditional probability over time, estimated from a cohort.** Survival curves from [survival analysis](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/02-inference-and-causation/regression-adjustment-and-survival-analysis.md) give the probability of being alive, or event-free, at each time after a defined starting point. Median survival, five-year survival and hazard ratios are summaries of the curve, each hiding something: a median of 12 months may contain a long tail of survivors. Staging systems ([tumour classification](../../08-pathology/03-neoplasia/tumour-classification-grading-and-staging.md)), risk scores and biomarkers refine the cohort to one more like the patient.

2. **Natural history is the comparator that gives treatment its meaning.** The benefit of an intervention is the difference between prognosis with it and prognosis without it, which is why the untreated course matters even when nobody intends to leave the disease untreated. A condition that resolves spontaneously in 90% of cases leaves at most 10% for any treatment to improve; one that is fatal untreated makes a modest absolute gain large in relative terms. Absolute risk reduction and the number needed to treat, not relative reduction, are what the patient needs ([measures of association](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/01-epidemiology/measures-of-frequency-and-association.md)).

3. **Communicating probability is a skill with known failure modes.** People understand natural frequencies ("of 100 people like you, about 20 will have a stroke in ten years; with treatment, about 15") better than percentages or relative risks ("25% reduction"). Framing changes choices: 90% survival and 10% mortality are chosen differently. Pictographs of 100 faces outperform numbers. Verbal labels ("rare," "common") are interpreted across a range so wide as to be useless. Clinicians who present only the relative risk reduction overstate benefit, whether they mean to or not.

4. **The right decision depends on values when options differ in what they trade.** Surgery with a small risk of death against a lifetime of symptoms; chemotherapy that adds months at the cost of their quality; anticoagulation that trades stroke against bleeding. Evidence gives the probabilities; only the patient knows how they weigh the outcomes, and patients differ widely. Such preference-sensitive decisions are where shared decision-making is necessary rather than courteous, and where [guidelines](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/02-evidence-based-medicine/grading-evidence-and-guidelines.md) issue weak recommendations precisely because the answer varies by person.

5. **Shared decision-making has a structure: options, outcomes, preferences, decision.** The clinician makes clear that a choice exists, lays out the reasonable options including doing nothing, describes the likely outcomes of each in numbers the patient can use, explores what matters to this patient, and reaches a decision together with a plan to revisit it. Decision aids (leaflets, pictographs, structured conversations) improve knowledge and accuracy of risk perception and reduce decisional conflict in trials; they do not systematically push patients toward or away from treatment, but they often reduce uptake of elective procedures.

6. **Consent is the legal form of the same conversation, and capacity is its precondition.** A valid decision requires that the patient understands the information, retains it, weighs it and communicates a choice; where capacity is lacking, decisions are made in the person's best interests or by a nominated proxy ([consent and capacity](../../15-medicine-in-society/02-ethics-law-and-the-profession/consent-capacity-and-confidentiality.md)). Prognosis conversations near the end of life, including whether to attempt resuscitation and when to stop treatment aimed at cure, are the hardest instance, and the evidence is that patients who understand their prognosis choose less aggressive care and are not harmed by knowing ([end-of-life decisions](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/death-dying-and-end-of-life-decisions.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Prognosis | The probable course and outcome of a condition, with or without intervention |
| Survival curve, median survival | Probability of surviving to each time; the time by which half have died |
| Hazard ratio | Ratio of instantaneous event rates between groups, from a survival model |
| Natural history | The course of the untreated disease; the comparator for every treatment |
| Absolute risk reduction and Number Needed to Treat (NNT) | The difference in event probability between options; its reciprocal |
| Natural frequency | Risk expressed as "x of 100 people like you," the format people understand best |
| Framing effect | The same probability chosen differently when stated as gain or loss |
| Preference-sensitive decision | A choice whose best answer depends on how the patient values the outcomes |
| Decision aid | A tool presenting options and outcomes to support a shared decision |
| Capacity | Ability to understand, retain and weigh information and communicate a decision |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A median survival of a year means the patient has a year."**: half live longer, some much longer; the distribution, not the median, is the prognosis.
- **"Patients do not want to know bad prognoses."**: most do, and knowing changes decisions toward care they value more; what they want is the truth delivered with support, at a pace they control.
- **"Shared decision-making means the patient decides alone."**: the clinician's judgement about options and probabilities is half of the decision; abandoning the patient to a menu is not sharing.
