## Essence

Histopathology is the examination of tissue under the microscope, and for most cancers and many inflammatory diseases it is the diagnosis: the final word on whether a lesion is malignant, what kind, how aggressive, and whether it has been removed. Cytology does the same with loose cells, obtained less invasively and read with less context. Molecular diagnostics adds the genome and the proteins of the tissue, and has shifted the question from "what does it look like" to "what is driving it," which is now what decides the treatment. The organising idea is that structure reveals process: the architecture of a tissue and the appearance of its cells record what the cells have been doing, and a pathologist reads that record against the normal tissue it should have been.

## Core tenets

1. **Diagnosis is departure from normal architecture, so normal histology is the reference.** Each tissue has a pattern (the layered epithelium, the lobule, the glomerulus) that reflects its function ([epithelium](../../05-tissues-and-anatomy/01-tissues/epithelium.md)). Inflammation, repair, dysplasia and invasion each disturb the pattern in characteristic ways: dysplasia is disordered maturation within the epithelium; carcinoma in situ is full-thickness disorder with the basement membrane intact; invasion is breach of that membrane. The pathologist's eye is trained on the normal so that the abnormal declares itself.

2. **The specimen passes through fixation, embedding, sectioning and staining, and each step shapes what can be seen.** Formalin cross-links proteins and halts autolysis but degrades nucleic acids and some antigens; paraffin embedding permits sections a few micrometres thick; haematoxylin stains nuclei blue and eosin stains cytoplasm pink. Frozen sections skip fixation to give an answer during surgery at the cost of quality. Sampling is the limiting step: a biopsy is a few milligrams from a lesion of grams, and a report can describe only the tissue it received.

3. **Grade describes the cells; stage describes the extent; both are prognosis.** Grade is how far the tumour has departed from the tissue of origin (differentiation, nuclear atypia, mitotic rate) and predicts behaviour. Stage is how far it has spread, assessed by the pathologist on the resected specimen (size, depth, margins, nodes) and by imaging beyond it ([classification, grading and staging](../../08-pathology/03-neoplasia/tumour-classification-grading-and-staging.md)). A clear margin is the surgeon's success measured in millimetres by the pathologist, and the lymph node count and status is the strongest single prognostic variable in most solid tumours.

4. **Immunohistochemistry identifies what a cell is and what it expresses.** Antibodies against lineage-specific proteins, visualised by a coloured reaction, tell an undifferentiated tumour's origin (cytokeratins for epithelium, lymphoid markers for lymphoma) and detect therapeutic targets: oestrogen receptor and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 in breast cancer, programmed death-ligand 1 in several tumours. It turns the microscope into a protein assay with spatial resolution, which is what makes a biopsy predictive of response to a targeted drug ([anticancer drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/anticancer-drugs.md)).

5. **Molecular diagnostics reads the genome of the lesion and classifies by driver.** Fluorescence in situ hybridisation shows translocations and amplifications; sequencing panels detect the mutations that define entities and predict drug response ([oncogenes and tumour suppressors](../../08-pathology/03-neoplasia/oncogenes-tumour-suppressors-and-genomic-instability.md)): a particular kinase mutation in lung adenocarcinoma, a signalling-pathway mutation in colorectal cancer that predicts failure of antibody therapy. Tumour classifications now include molecular criteria, and the same morphology may be two diseases. Circulating tumour Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) in blood extends the method to a non-invasive sample, with the sampling problem reversed: the whole tumour burden is represented, diluted.

6. **Cytology trades context for access.** Cells scraped (cervical screening), aspirated through a fine needle (thyroid, breast, lymph node) or shed into fluid (urine, effusions) can be examined without a tissue biopsy. Nuclear features reveal malignancy, but without architecture the pathologist cannot judge invasion, and cellularity depends on who took the sample. Cervical cytology is the model [screening test](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/03-screening-and-prevention/screening-principles-and-biases.md): moderately sensitive, repeatable, and effective because the disease has a long preclinical phase.


## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Biopsy | Tissue removed for diagnosis; a sample, not the lesion |
| Haematoxylin and eosin | The standard stain: nuclei blue, cytoplasm and matrix pink |
| Frozen section | Rapid unfixed section for an answer during surgery |
| Dysplasia and carcinoma in situ | Disordered maturation within an epithelium; full-thickness disorder without invasion |
| Grade | Degree of departure from the tissue of origin; a predictor of behaviour |
| Margin | The distance from tumour to the cut edge of a resection |
| Immunohistochemistry | Antibody staining to identify proteins in tissue sections |
| Fluorescence in situ hybridisation | Labelled probes showing specific DNA sequences in cells |
| Cytology | Examination of individual cells from scrapes, aspirates or fluids |
| Synoptic report | Structured report listing every prognostic element required for a tumour type |

## How it fits

- **Below:** the reference is normal [tissue structure](../../05-tissues-and-anatomy/01-tissues/epithelium.md); what is seen is [cell injury](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/cell-injury-adaptation-and-death.md), [inflammation](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/acute-inflammation.md) and [neoplasia](../../08-pathology/03-neoplasia/tumour-classification-grading-and-staging.md); the molecular methods are [molecular techniques](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/03-genetic-information-flow/molecular-techniques.md) applied to a lesion.
- **Above:** the tissue diagnosis is the entry point to [cancer care](../../12-organ-system-specialties/07-oncology/principles-of-cancer-care.md) and the predictive marker for [systemic therapy](../../12-organ-system-specialties/07-oncology/systemic-anticancer-therapy.md); [skin cancer](../../12-organ-system-specialties/11-dermatology/skin-cancer-and-skin-infections.md), [glomerular disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/03-nephrology/glomerular-and-tubular-diseases.md), inflammatory bowel disease and liver disease are classified by biopsy; the autopsy remains the audit of clinical diagnosis for [patient safety](../../15-medicine-in-society/01-health-systems/patient-safety-and-quality-improvement.md).
- **Siblings:** [haematology](haematology-and-coagulation-testing.md) examines the marrow with the same methods; [microbiology](microbiological-and-serological-diagnosis.md) and histopathology share molecular platforms; all are read against [reference values](clinical-chemistry-and-reference-ranges.md) of a kind, the normal tissue being the pathologist's reference range.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A negative biopsy excludes cancer."**: a biopsy reports the tissue sampled; a lesion missed by the needle or a focus outside the sample is not excluded, and the report should be read with the pre-test probability.
- **"The pathologist's diagnosis is objective."**: for clear cases it is highly reproducible; for borderline lesions and grading, inter-observer variation is substantial and second opinions change management.
- **"Molecular testing replaces morphology."**: it refines a morphological diagnosis and selects treatment; the same mutation occurs in different tumours with different meanings, and the tissue context still decides.
