# Microbiological and serological diagnosis

## Essence

To treat an infection well you need to know which organism is present, whether it is the cause or a bystander, and what will kill it. The microbiology laboratory answers these by three strategies: see or grow the organism (microscopy and culture), detect its molecules (antigen tests and nucleic acid amplification), or detect the host's response to it (serology). Each strategy has a different relation to time: culture takes days but yields susceptibility, molecular tests take hours but detect dead organisms and colonisers as readily as pathogens, and antibodies appear weeks after infection and persist for years. The organising idea is that a positive result is evidence of presence, and presence is not causation; interpreting the result requires knowing where the sample came from, what normally lives there, and when in the illness it was taken.

## Core tenets

1. **The specimen decides the meaning of the result before any test is run.** Blood, cerebrospinal fluid and joint fluid are normally sterile, so any growth is significant unless it is a skin contaminant introduced at sampling. Sputum, urine, skin and stool carry a [resident flora](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/01-microbes-and-infection/the-human-microbiome.md), so growth must be interpreted by species, quantity and the presence of inflammatory cells. A specimen taken after antibiotics have started is often falsely negative. Most of the laboratory's diagnostic errors are specimen errors: the wrong site, the wrong time, the wrong container, or contamination.

2. **Microscopy is fast and cheap, and its yield depends on organism load.** The Gram stain divides bacteria by cell-wall structure in minutes and can direct therapy from a positive cerebrospinal fluid or joint fluid before culture; acid-fast stains show mycobacteria; wet preparations show parasites and fungi. Sensitivity is limited by concentration: a Gram stain needs roughly 10^5 organisms per millilitre to be reliably positive, so a negative stain excludes little in fluids where the load is low.

3. **Culture remains the reference because it yields a living organism and its susceptibilities.** Growth on selective and differential media, identification by biochemical profile or mass spectrometry, then antimicrobial susceptibility testing by measuring the minimum inhibitory concentration against breakpoints, is the only route to knowing what will work. Its costs are time (18–48 hours for most bacteria, weeks for mycobacteria), failure for organisms that will not grow (many viruses, some fastidious bacteria), and loss of sensitivity after antibiotics. Blood culture positivity rises with the volume of blood taken and the number of sets, which is why technique matters more than the instrument.

4. **Nucleic acid amplification detects the organism's genome rather than the organism, with consequences in both directions.** The Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) and its relatives ([molecular techniques](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/03-genetic-information-flow/molecular-techniques.md)) amplify a target sequence a billion-fold in hours, detect organisms that cannot be cultured and remain positive after antibiotics. But the genome of a dead or colonising organism is amplified as readily as that of an invading one, so a positive PCR from a non-sterile site may not be the cause of the illness, and the quantitative cycle threshold is a rough guide to load. Resistance genes can be detected directly, but a gene present is not always expressed.

5. **Serology reads the host's memory, so timing is everything.** Immunoglobulin M (IgM) appears days to weeks after infection and fades; Immunoglobulin G (IgG) follows, persists and marks past exposure or immunity ([antibodies](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/03-adaptive-immunity/b-cells-and-antibodies.md)). A single IgG result cannot distinguish current from old infection; seroconversion or a four-fold rise between paired samples can. Antibodies are absent in the window before they form (the first weeks of Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection), in immunodeficiency, and after passive transfer (maternal IgG in infants). Serology is the method of choice for organisms that are hard to culture and for population surveys of exposure, and the wrong method for an acute diagnosis.

6. **The laboratory serves the patient and the population at once.** Every susceptibility result feeds a cumulative antibiogram that guides empirical therapy locally; every isolate typed by whole-genome sequencing can be placed in a transmission chain for [outbreak investigation](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/01-epidemiology/surveillance-and-outbreak-investigation.md) and [infection control](../../12-organ-system-specialties/08-infectious-diseases/infection-control-and-healthcare-associated-infection.md). Diagnostics are the instrument of [antimicrobial stewardship](../../12-organ-system-specialties/08-infectious-diseases/antimicrobial-stewardship.md): the faster a specific diagnosis arrives, the sooner broad empirical treatment can be narrowed or stopped.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sterile versus non-sterile site | Whether any growth is significant or must be interpreted against resident flora |
| Gram stain | Rapid classification of bacteria by cell-wall structure |
| Culture and susceptibility | Growing the organism and measuring the minimum inhibitory concentration of antimicrobials |
| Breakpoint | The concentration separating "susceptible" from "resistant" for a drug–organism pair |
| Nucleic acid amplification (PCR) | Detecting an organism's genome by amplifying a target sequence |
| Cycle threshold | Amplification cycles needed to detect the target; inversely related to load |
| IgM and IgG | Early, transient antibody and later, persistent antibody; the basis of serological timing |
| Seroconversion | Appearance of antibody between paired samples; evidence of recent infection |
| Antigen test | Detection of a microbial protein or polysaccharide, often at the point of care |
| Antibiogram | Local cumulative susceptibility data used to guide empirical therapy |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A positive culture means infection."**: from a non-sterile site it may be colonisation or contamination; the clinical picture, the specimen quality and the organism decide.
- **"PCR is more accurate than culture, so it replaces it."**: it is more sensitive and faster but detects dead and colonising organisms and gives no susceptibility; the two answer different questions.
- **"A positive antibody test means current infection."**: IgG marks exposure at any time in the past; only IgM, seroconversion or a rising titre point to recent infection, and each has exceptions.
