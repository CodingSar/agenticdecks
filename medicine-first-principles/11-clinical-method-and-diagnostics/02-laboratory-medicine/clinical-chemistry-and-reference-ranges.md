## Essence

Clinical chemistry measures the concentrations of substances in blood, urine and other fluids, and most of medicine's routine numbers (sodium, potassium, creatinine, glucose, bilirubin, troponin) come from it. Each analyte is a window on a physiological process: creatinine on glomerular filtration, bilirubin on hepatic conjugation and excretion, troponin on myocyte death. Interpreting a result requires knowing what the number reflects, how much it varies in health and in the assay, and that "normal" is a statistical convention: a reference range is the central 95% of a reference population, so one healthy person in twenty falls outside it on every test. The idea that organises the field is that a laboratory value is a sample from a distribution, not a fact, and that its meaning lies in the change from the patient's own baseline and in the mechanism that moved it.

## Core tenets

1. **A reference range is the central 95% of a reference population, which is a convention, not a boundary of health.** The range is set by measuring a reference group (usually healthy adults) and taking the 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles. By construction 5% of healthy people are "abnormal" on each test, and across a panel of twenty independent tests about 64% of healthy people will have at least one flagged result. The range also depends on who was measured: age, sex, pregnancy and ancestry shift many analytes, and a range derived from young adults misclassifies the elderly.

2. **Decision limits are different from reference ranges.** For some analytes the clinically important threshold is not where healthy people end but where risk changes: fasting glucose and glycated haemoglobin thresholds for diabetes, cholesterol targets, and troponin's 99th percentile for infarction were set from outcome studies, not from the distribution in the well. A result can be inside the reference range and above the decision limit, or vice versa. Which kind of threshold a laboratory report prints should be known before it is read.

3. **Every result carries analytical and biological variation, and a change is real only if it exceeds both.** The assay's imprecision (coefficient of variation of a few percent for most chemistry analytes) adds to within-person day-to-day variation (small for sodium, large for triglycerides and many enzymes). The reference change value, roughly 2.8 times the combined coefficient of variation, is the smallest difference between two results that is unlikely to be noise ([measurement error](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/01-probability-and-measurement/measurement-error-and-biological-variation.md)). A creatinine moving from 80 to 90 µmol/L may be nothing; from 80 to 160 µmol/L is halved filtration.

4. **Pre-analytical error outnumbers analytical error.** Haemolysis releases potassium from red cells; a tourniquet left on concentrates proteins; a sample drawn above an intravenous infusion is diluted; glucose falls in a tube left standing as cells consume it; a mislabelled sample is the right result on the wrong patient. Most "wrong" laboratory results are right for the specimen that was analysed. An unexpected result is first a question about the sample.

5. **Each analyte is read through the physiology that sets its concentration.** Plasma sodium reflects water balance relative to solute, not total sodium, so hyponatraemia is usually a disorder of water ([body fluid compartments](../../06-physiology/01-integrative-principles/body-fluid-compartments-and-electrolytes.md)). Creatinine rises only when filtration has fallen substantially, because the relation to glomerular filtration rate is hyperbolic: half the nephrons can be lost before creatinine leaves the reference range ([glomerular filtration](../../06-physiology/05-renal-system/glomerular-filtration-and-clearance.md)). Enzymes in plasma report cell injury in the tissue that holds them; proteins made by the liver report synthetic function with a lag set by their half-life (albumin weeks, clotting factors hours).

6. **Patterns across analytes carry more information than any one.** The liver panel separates hepatocellular injury (transaminases high, out of proportion to alkaline phosphatase) from cholestasis (the reverse); the anion gap separates acidoses that add an unmeasured acid from those that lose bicarbonate ([acid–base](../../06-physiology/01-integrative-principles/acid-base-physiology.md)); urea rising faster than creatinine suggests volume depletion or gut bleeding. The panel exists because the analytes are coupled by physiology, and the clinician reads the coupling.


## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Analyte | The substance measured (an ion, metabolite, enzyme, protein, hormone or drug) |
| Reference range | Central 95% of results in a defined reference population |
| Decision limit | A threshold set by outcome data rather than by the healthy distribution |
| Analytical variation | Imprecision of the assay, as a coefficient of variation |
| Biological variation | Within-person fluctuation in health around a set point |
| Reference change value | Smallest difference between serial results unlikely to be due to variation alone |
| Pre-analytical error | Error introduced before analysis: collection, handling, labelling, transport |
| Anion gap | Sodium minus (chloride plus bicarbonate); unmeasured anions in acidosis |
| Point-of-care testing | Analysis at the bedside, trading precision and quality control for speed |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A result in the reference range is normal, and outside it is disease."**: 5% of healthy people fall outside every range; a value inside the range can still be abnormal for that patient, and a decision limit may sit inside the range.
- **"Creatinine in the normal range means normal kidneys."**: the hyperbolic relation means filtration can halve before creatinine leaves the range; estimated glomerular filtration rate and the trend matter more than one value.
- **"The laboratory made an error."**: usually the sample did; haemolysis, contamination and mislabelling account for most implausible results.
