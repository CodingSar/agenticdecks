# Functional testing: spirometry, exercise and sleep

## Essence

Imaging shows what an organ looks like; functional testing measures what it can do. Spirometry measures how much air the lungs move and how fast; gas transfer measures how well oxygen crosses into blood; exercise testing loads the heart, lungs and muscles together and records which fails first; sleep studies record breathing, oxygenation and brain state through a night. Each is a controlled physiological experiment on the patient, with a reference value predicted from age, sex, height and ancestry, and each exposes a reserve that resting measurement cannot. The idea that unifies them is that disease is often invisible at rest because compensation hides it, and that the right stress reveals the limit: a narrowed airway at forced expiration, a stenosed coronary at peak demand, an unstable airway when muscle tone falls in sleep.

## Core tenets

1. **Spirometry separates obstruction from restriction with two numbers and a ratio.** Forced vital capacity is the volume exhaled after a full breath; the forced expiratory volume in one second is how much of it comes out in the first second ([lung mechanics](../../06-physiology/04-respiratory-system/ventilation-and-lung-mechanics.md)). A ratio below about 0.7 means airflow is limited (asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease); a reduced capacity with a preserved ratio means the lungs or chest cannot expand (fibrosis, chest wall disease, weakness). Flow depends on airway calibre and lung elastic recoil, so the shape of the flow–volume loop shows where the narrowing is: a scooped expiratory limb in small-airway disease, a plateau in fixed large-airway obstruction.

2. **Reversibility and provocation test the mechanism of obstruction.** Repeating spirometry after an inhaled bronchodilator shows whether the narrowing is smooth-muscle tone that relaxes (asthma) or structural loss of recoil and airway destruction that does not (emphysema), the distinction on which [asthma and COPD](../../12-organ-system-specialties/02-pulmonology/asthma-and-copd.md) rest. Provocation (inhaled methacholine or exercise) detects airway hyper-responsiveness in a patient whose resting spirometry is normal; peak flow diaries record variability over days. Effort-dependence is spirometry's weakness: a poor manoeuvre mimics restriction, so the quality criteria matter as much as the numbers.

3. **Lung volumes and gas transfer measure what spirometry cannot.** Total lung capacity (by body plethysmography or gas dilution) distinguishes true restriction from the air trapping of obstruction, in which residual volume rises while vital capacity falls. The transfer factor for carbon monoxide measures the diffusing capacity of the alveolar–capillary membrane and the capillary blood volume ([gas exchange](../../06-physiology/04-respiratory-system/gas-exchange-and-transport.md)): reduced in fibrosis, emphysema and pulmonary vascular disease, preserved in asthma and chest-wall restriction. A low transfer factor with normal spirometry points to the vessels or the interstitium ([interstitial lung disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/02-pulmonology/interstitial-and-restrictive-lung-disease.md)).

4. **Exercise testing reveals the limit that rest conceals, and the pattern of failure names the system.** Oxygen consumption rises with work until it plateaus at a maximum set by the weakest link in the chain from lungs to mitochondria ([exercise physiology](../../06-physiology/01-integrative-principles/exercise-and-stress-physiology.md)). Cardiopulmonary exercise testing measures ventilation, oxygen uptake and carbon dioxide output breath by breath: a low peak oxygen uptake with an early anaerobic threshold and a heart rate at maximum indicates cardiac limitation; one with ventilation reaching its ceiling and desaturation indicates pulmonary limitation; one with neither indicates deconditioning or poor effort. Peak oxygen uptake predicts operative mortality and is the basis of [perioperative risk](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/perioperative-risk-and-complications.md) assessment and transplant selection.

5. **The exercise ECG is a test of supply against demand.** A coronary stenosis that permits adequate flow at rest cannot meet the demand of exercise, so ischaemia appears as ST depression, symptoms or a fall in blood pressure at a reproducible workload ([ischaemic heart disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/ischemic-heart-disease.md)). Its sensitivity and specificity are modest (roughly 65–70% and 70–75% in standard summaries), so its positive likelihood ratio is only about 2–3 and its worth depends on the pre-test probability; it is most useful at intermediate probability and nearly useless at the extremes ([thresholds](../01-the-clinical-encounter/using-tests-pretest-probability-and-thresholds.md)). Stress imaging (echocardiography or perfusion scintigraphy) adds where the ischaemia is and is more accurate.

6. **Sleep studies catch the physiology that only appears when tone and drive fall.** During sleep pharyngeal muscle tone falls and the ventilatory response to carbon dioxide is blunted ([control of breathing](../../06-physiology/04-respiratory-system/control-of-breathing.md); [sleep physiology](../../06-physiology/01-integrative-principles/sleep-and-circadian-rhythms.md)). Polysomnography records airflow, respiratory effort, oxygen saturation, EEG, eye movement and muscle tone, and counts apnoeas and hypopnoeas per hour. Obstructive sleep apnoea shows effort without flow and cyclic desaturation; central apnoea shows neither effort nor flow; hypoventilation shows sustained hypercapnia. Home oximetry and respiratory polygraphy are simpler, cheaper and sufficient when the pre-test probability is high.


## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Forced vital capacity (FVC) | Total volume exhaled forcibly after a full inspiration |
| Forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) | Volume exhaled in the first second of that manoeuvre |
| FEV1/FVC ratio | Below about 0.7 indicates airflow obstruction |
| Flow–volume loop | Plot of flow against volume whose shape localises the obstruction |
| Bronchodilator reversibility | Improvement in FEV1 after an inhaled bronchodilator, indicating reversible smooth-muscle narrowing |
| Transfer factor (DLCO) | Uptake of carbon monoxide as a measure of alveolar–capillary diffusing capacity |
| Peak oxygen uptake (VO2 max) | Maximum rate of oxygen consumption; the integrated capacity of the oxygen transport chain |
| Apnoea–hypopnoea index | Events per hour of sleep; the severity measure of sleep-disordered breathing |
| Lower limit of normal | The 5th percentile of the predicted distribution, the correct threshold for abnormality |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Below 80% of predicted is abnormal."**: the correct threshold is the lower limit of normal (5th percentile), which varies with age; a fixed 80% misclassifies the old and the young.
- **"A negative exercise ECG rules out coronary disease."**: with a negative likelihood ratio around 0.4–0.5 it lowers probability modestly; at high pre-test probability a negative test leaves the diagnosis likely.
- **"A normal chest X-ray and normal oxygen saturation at rest mean normal lungs."**: fibrosis and emphysema can be present with a normal film, and desaturation may appear only on exertion; function and structure are different measurements.
