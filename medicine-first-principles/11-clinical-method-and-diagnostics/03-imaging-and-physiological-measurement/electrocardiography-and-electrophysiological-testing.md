## Essence

Excitable cells generate currents when they depolarise, and the body is a conductor, so those currents produce voltages at the skin. The Electrocardiogram (ECG) records the millivolt signals of the heart from electrodes on the limbs and chest; the Electroencephalogram (EEG) records the microvolt signals of the cortex from the scalp; nerve conduction studies and electromyography record the signals of peripheral nerve and muscle, often after a stimulus. Each is a direct measurement of physiology in real time, cheap and repeatable, and each is interpreted by the same principle: the recorded waveform is the sum of many cells' activity projected onto the line between two electrodes, so its timing tells you about conduction and its shape and direction tell you where the current came from and where it went.

## Core tenets

1. **An ECG lead records the projection of the heart's net electrical vector onto that lead's axis.** As a wave of depolarisation sweeps through the myocardium, the boundary between depolarised and resting tissue acts as a moving dipole ([excitable membranes](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/electricity-and-excitable-membranes.md)). A lead whose positive electrode lies in the direction of travel records a positive deflection; one lying opposite records a negative one; one at right angles records nothing. Twelve leads look at the same vector from twelve angles, which is why the standard ECG can localise an abnormality (anterior, inferior, lateral) to the territory of a coronary artery.

2. **Each wave is a stage of the cardiac cycle's electrical sequence.** The P wave is atrial depolarisation; the PR interval is conduction through the atrioventricular node, the deliberate delay that lets the atria empty; the QRS complex is ventricular depolarisation, narrow (under 120 ms) because the His–Purkinje system distributes it fast, wide when it spreads cell to cell; the T wave is ventricular repolarisation; the QT interval is the duration of the ventricular action potential ([cardiac electrophysiology](../../06-physiology/03-cardiovascular-system/cardiac-electrophysiology.md)). Every interval is a conduction time and every abnormal interval names a conduction fault.

3. **Rhythm diagnosis is asking where each beat originated and how it was conducted.** A P wave before every QRS with a constant PR interval means the sinus node is in charge. Absent P waves with an irregular baseline and irregular QRS means atrial fibrillation; a QRS without a P wave means a ventricular or junctional origin; progressive lengthening of PR until a beat drops means a failing node. A wide-complex tachycardia is ventricular until proven otherwise because the stakes are asymmetric. These rules follow from anatomy and conduction, which is why [arrhythmias](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/arrhythmias.md) can be diagnosed from a strip, and a cardiac arrest rhythm identified and shocked or not in seconds ([resuscitation](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/resuscitation-and-cardiac-arrest.md)).

4. **Ischaemia and infarction change repolarisation, and the pattern locates the lesion.** Ischaemic myocardium repolarises abnormally and sustains an injury current, which displaces the ST segment: elevation in leads facing a transmural injury (the pattern that mandates emergency reperfusion), depression and T-wave inversion in subendocardial ischaemia. Dead muscle generates no current, so leads over an old infarct see the unopposed vector of the far wall as a Q wave. The ECG is fast and specific for the large transmural infarct and insensitive for the small one, which is why it is paired with troponin ([ischaemic heart disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/ischemic-heart-disease.md)).

5. **The EEG records cortical rhythms and their disruption, and its signal is small.** Summed synaptic potentials of millions of pyramidal cells reach the scalp at tens of microvolts, attenuated and smeared by skull and scalp, so spatial resolution is centimetres and deep sources are invisible. Normal rhythms vary with state (alpha with eyes closed, slow waves in deep sleep); focal slowing marks local dysfunction; spikes and sharp waves mark the hypersynchronous discharge of [epilepsy](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/epilepsy.md), whose diagnosis the EEG supports but, between seizures, cannot exclude (a single routine recording catches abnormalities in perhaps half of patients). Continuous and video recording raise the yield; EEG also grades encephalopathy and confirms brain death protocols in some jurisdictions.

6. **Nerve conduction and electromyography localise the lesion in the motor unit.** Stimulating a nerve and recording the response downstream gives conduction velocity (slowed by demyelination, since saltatory conduction depends on myelin) and amplitude (reduced by axon loss) ([action potential](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/membrane-potential-and-the-action-potential.md)). A needle in muscle records spontaneous activity in denervated fibres and the shape of motor unit potentials, separating neuropathy from myopathy and localising a root, plexus or single-nerve lesion. The same logic (latency equals conduction time, amplitude equals how many units responded) underlies evoked potentials from eye and ear.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Lead | A voltage measured between two points; records the projection of the cardiac vector onto its axis |
| P wave, QRS complex, T wave | Atrial depolarisation, ventricular depolarisation, ventricular repolarisation |
| PR and QT intervals | Atrioventricular conduction time; duration of ventricular activation and recovery |
| Cardiac axis | The mean direction of ventricular depolarisation in the frontal plane |
| ST segment | Interval between depolarisation and repolarisation; displaced by injury currents |
| Q wave | Initial negative deflection; pathological when it marks electrically dead muscle |
| Electroencephalogram (EEG) | Scalp recording of summed cortical synaptic activity |
| Interictal epileptiform discharge | Spike or sharp wave between seizures, supporting a diagnosis of epilepsy |
| Nerve conduction velocity | Speed of impulse propagation; slowed by demyelination |
| Electromyography | Needle recording of muscle electrical activity at rest and on contraction |

- **Above:** [arrhythmias](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/arrhythmias.md) and [ischaemic heart disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/ischemic-heart-disease.md) are diagnosed on the ECG; [epilepsy](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/epilepsy.md) and [neuromuscular disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/demyelinating-and-neuromuscular-disease.md) on EEG and nerve studies; [resuscitation](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/resuscitation-and-cardiac-arrest.md) is directed by the rhythm; [electrolyte disorders](../../12-organ-system-specialties/03-nephrology/electrolyte-and-acid-base-disorders.md) are read from the T wave.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A normal ECG excludes a heart attack."**: the initial ECG is normal or non-diagnostic in a substantial minority of infarctions; it is specific for the transmural pattern and insensitive for the rest, which is why serial ECGs and troponin are paired with it.
- **"A normal EEG excludes epilepsy."**: between seizures a single routine EEG is abnormal in only about half of people with epilepsy; the diagnosis is clinical, and the EEG supports and classifies it.
- **"The ECG shows the heart's pumping."**: it shows electrical activity; electrical activity without mechanical output (pulseless electrical activity) is a cardiac arrest with a normal-looking trace.
