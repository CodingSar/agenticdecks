## Essence

Transplantation and cancer are the two problems in which medicine wants the immune system to make the opposite of its natural decision. A graft is non-self that must be tolerated; a tumour is self, slightly altered, that must be attacked. The same molecules decide both: Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) differences make a graft the most immunogenic tissue a T cell ever meets, while a tumour's few mutated peptides make it barely visible. Transplant medicine is applied suppression of recognition, with infection and cancer as its costs; immuno-oncology is applied release of recognition, with autoimmunity as its cost.

## Core tenets

1. **Allogeneic MHC is the strongest antigen the immune system knows.** Between 1 and 10% of a person's T cells respond to the MHC molecules of an unrelated donor, against roughly one in 10^5 for an ordinary pathogen peptide, because the T-cell repertoire was selected to bind MHC and foreign MHC molecules loaded with thousands of peptides present an enormous number of fits (direct allorecognition). Recipient dendritic cells also present donor MHC fragments as ordinary peptides (indirect allorecognition). Matching donor and recipient Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) types reduces, but never removes, the mismatch, since minor histocompatibility antigens (any polymorphic peptide) remain; only identical twins need no immunosuppression ([antigen presentation and MHC](../03-adaptive-immunity/antigen-presentation-and-mhc.md)).

2. **Rejection comes in tempos that name the mechanism.** *Hyperacute* rejection, within minutes to hours, is preformed antibody (ABO blood-group or anti-HLA from previous transfusion, pregnancy or transplant) fixing complement on graft endothelium and clotting its vessels; cross-matching recipient serum against donor cells prevents it ([blood groups](../../06-physiology/08-blood/blood-groups-and-transfusion-compatibility.md)). *Acute cellular* rejection, in days to weeks, is T cells infiltrating the graft and is usually reversible. *Acute antibody-mediated* rejection is newly made donor-specific antibody. *Chronic* rejection, over months to years, is low-grade immune injury producing fibrosis and vascular narrowing, the main reason grafts fail late, and it responds poorly to treatment.

3. **When the graft is the immune system, it attacks the host.** After haematopoietic stem-cell transplantation, donor T cells recognise the recipient's tissues as foreign: graft-versus-host disease damages skin, gut and liver and is the main cause of death after allogeneic transplant. The same donor cells also kill residual leukaemia (the graft-versus-leukaemia effect), which is much of why the transplant cures.

4. **Immunosuppression blocks the three signals of T-cell activation, and its costs are the jobs of the cells it blocks.** Calcineurin inhibitors (ciclosporin, tacrolimus) block signal 1; the fusion protein belatacept blocks CD28 co-stimulation (signal 2); mycophenolate and mTOR inhibitors block the proliferation that follows signal 3; corticosteroids suppress cytokines broadly; depleting antibodies remove T cells at induction ([T cells](../03-adaptive-immunity/t-cells.md)). The price is opportunistic infection (cytomegalovirus, *Pneumocystis*), virus-driven cancer (skin squamous carcinoma, Epstein-Barr-virus lymphoma) and drug toxicity. True tolerance, a graft accepted without drugs, has been achieved in a few patients by mixing donor and recipient bone marrow, and remains the field's goal ([tolerance](../03-adaptive-immunity/tolerance-and-self-recognition.md)).

5. **Tumours are seen by the immune system, and most are edited by it.** Burnet and Thomas proposed immune surveillance in the 1950s. Immunosuppressed people have far more virus-associated cancers; tumours infiltrated by T cells carry a better prognosis; mice without lymphocytes grow more spontaneous tumours; and tumours that emerge in immunocompetent hosts have measurably lost the antigens T cells would see. Schreiber's three Es describe the course: *elimination* of most transformed cells, *equilibrium* in which the tumour persists but is held, and *escape* when a variant evades control. Tumour antigens include neoantigens from mutations (so melanoma and smoking-related lung cancer, with high mutational burden, are the most immunogenic), viral proteins and ectopic self-proteins.

6. **Escape uses the body's own brakes.** Tumours lose MHC class I, express the checkpoint ligand PD-L1 to exhaust T cells, recruit regulatory T cells and suppressive myeloid cells, secrete transforming growth factor beta, and build a fibrous microenvironment that T cells cannot enter ([tumour microenvironment](../../08-pathology/03-neoplasia/invasion-metastasis-and-the-tumour-microenvironment.md)). Cells that lose MHC class I become targets for Natural Killer (NK) cells, so complete escape needs both routes closed ([NK cells](../02-innate-immunity/cytokines-interferons-and-nk-cells.md)). Evading immune destruction is now listed among the [hallmarks of cancer](../../08-pathology/03-neoplasia/hallmarks-of-cancer.md).

7. **Immunotherapy reverses escape, and its side effects are autoimmunity.** Antibodies blocking CTLA-4 (2011) and PD-1 or PD-L1 (2014 onward) release exhausted T cells and produce durable remissions in a minority of patients with melanoma, lung, kidney, bladder and mismatch-repair-deficient cancers, roughly 20 to 40% responding in melanoma; the Nobel Prize for this went to Allison and Honjo in 2018. Their adverse effects (colitis, thyroiditis, hepatitis, diabetes, pneumonitis) are autoimmunity, the predictable cost of removing checkpoints. Chimeric antigen receptor T cells, engineered to bind CD19, put the majority of children with relapsed B-cell leukaemia into remission (around 80% initially), at the price of cytokine release syndrome and the loss of all normal B cells. Monoclonal antibodies (rituximab, trastuzumab) kill tumour cells through antibody-dependent cytotoxicity, and the papillomavirus and hepatitis B vaccines prevent cancer by preventing the infection that causes it ([systemic anticancer therapy](../../12-organ-system-specialties/07-oncology/systemic-anticancer-therapy.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Allograft | Tissue transplanted between genetically different members of one species |
| Allorecognition (direct and indirect) | T cells binding intact donor MHC, or donor peptides on recipient MHC |
| Cross-match | Test of recipient serum against donor cells for preformed antibody |
| Hyperacute, acute and chronic rejection | Antibody in hours, T cells in weeks, fibrosis over years |
| Graft-versus-host disease | Donor immune cells attacking a stem-cell transplant recipient |
| Immune surveillance | Continuous elimination of transformed cells by the immune system |
| Elimination, equilibrium, escape | The three phases of a tumour's relationship with immunity |
| Neoantigen | Peptide created by a tumour mutation, absent from normal self |
| Checkpoint blockade | Antibodies against CTLA-4, PD-1 or PD-L1 that release T-cell inhibition |
| Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell | Patient's T cell engineered with an antibody-derived receptor for a tumour antigen |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A perfectly matched organ is not rejected."** HLA-identical siblings still differ at minor antigens and still need immunosuppression; only an identical twin's organ is accepted freely.
- **"Cancer grows because the immune system does not see it."** Most tumours are seen and shaped by immunity; the ones that present clinically are the survivors of that selection, which is why they carry the marks of escape.
