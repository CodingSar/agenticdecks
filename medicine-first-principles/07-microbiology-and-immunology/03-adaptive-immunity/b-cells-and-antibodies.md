## Essence

Antibodies are soluble receptors. A B cell makes one receptor by random gene rearrangement, and when antigen selects it the cell secretes that receptor into blood and secretions, where it binds the native surface of a microbe or toxin and marks it for destruction. One end of the molecule (the variable Fab region) decides what is bound; the other (the constant Fc region) decides what happens next: complement activation, phagocytosis, killing by Natural Killer (NK) cells, transport across the placenta or onto a mucosal surface, or arming of a mast cell. Antibody quality improves during a response by mutation and selection within lymph nodes, and the plasma cells produced can secrete for decades.

## Core tenets

1. **An antibody has two jobs, carried by two ends.** Two identical heavy and two identical light chains form a Y. The tips of the arms bind antigen (two sites per molecule, so antibody can cross-link and clump particles). The stem, the Fc region, is constant within a class and is what the rest of the immune system recognises ([protein structure](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/01-macromolecules/proteins-structure-and-folding.md)). Antibodies bind native, three-dimensional shapes on any class of molecule (protein, sugar, lipid, nucleic acid), unlike T cells, which see only processed peptides.

2. **The class (isotype) sets the function and the location.** Immunoglobulin M (IgM) is made first, as a pentamer: low affinity, good at complement activation, confined to blood. Immunoglobulin G (IgG) is the main serum antibody (~10 g/L), has a half-life of ~3 weeks, crosses the placenta, opsonises, neutralises, fixes complement and arms NK cells. Immunoglobulin A (IgA) is secreted as a dimer across mucosal epithelia; several grams a day coat the gut and airway, more than all other classes combined. Immunoglobulin E (IgE) is present in nanogram amounts but sits on mast cells and basophils, where cross-linking triggers degranulation against helminths, and in allergy. A B cell switches class by recombining its heavy-chain constant genes while keeping the same antigen-binding site; the cytokines from helper T cells (interferon-gamma, interleukin-4, transforming growth factor beta) decide which.

3. **Antibody protects mainly by neutralisation and opsonisation.** Bound to a virus's receptor-binding protein or to a toxin's binding subunit, antibody prevents attachment: neutralising antibody titre is the correlate of protection for most vaccines. Bound to a bacterium, the Fc tail engages Fc receptors on phagocytes and recruits [complement](../02-innate-immunity/complement-phagocytes-and-the-inflammatory-cascade.md), so encapsulated bacteria that resist complement alone are eaten once coated. Antibody cannot enter cells, so intracellular pathogens are left to T cells.

4. **Good antibody needs T-cell help; sugars do not get it.** Protein antigens are internalised by the B cell, presented on Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) class II to a follicular helper T cell of the same specificity, which delivers CD40 ligand and cytokines; this T-dependent response gives class switching, affinity maturation and memory. Polysaccharides, such as bacterial capsules, cannot be presented on MHC and only cross-link B-cell receptors directly, yielding IgM without memory, and almost nothing in children under two. Conjugating a capsular sugar to a carrier protein recruits T-cell help for the sugar, which is why conjugate vaccines nearly eliminated invasive *Haemophilus influenzae* type b disease in infants.

5. **The germinal centre is natural selection run inside a lymph node.** Activated B cells proliferate in follicles and switch on activation-induced cytidine deaminase, which mutates the antigen-binding genes at about one base per division. Cells whose mutated receptor binds antigen displayed on follicular dendritic cells more tightly capture more of it, present more to helper T cells and survive; the rest die. Several rounds raise affinity by 10- to 1,000-fold over weeks ([natural selection](../../04-genetics-evolution-and-development/02-evolution/natural-selection-and-population-genetics.md)). The output is long-lived plasma cells, which settle in bone marrow and secrete antibody for years to decades, and memory B cells, which wait for the next encounter.

6. **The kinetics of the response are what serology reads.** A first exposure gives detectable IgM after about a week and IgG a few days later, both at modest titre; a second exposure gives a faster, larger, higher-affinity IgG response with little IgM. So IgM against a pathogen means recent infection, IgG alone means past infection or vaccination, and a rising IgG titre between two samples means active infection ([serological diagnosis](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/02-laboratory-medicine/microbiological-and-serological-diagnosis.md)). Maternal IgG, transferred across the placenta, protects an infant for about six months and then wanes, which is when antibody deficiencies first show.

7. **Antibody is also a drug, and a cause of disease.** Passive transfer of antibody (pooled immunoglobulin, antitoxin, monoclonal antibodies) gives immediate protection that lasts weeks. Köhler and Milstein's hybridoma method (1975) made single-specificity antibodies manufacturable, and humanised monoclonals are now among the most used drugs in medicine ([biologics](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/biologics-and-advanced-therapies.md)). The same molecules misdirected cause disease: autoantibodies against receptors and cells, IgE against harmless proteins, and immune complexes in vessels ([hypersensitivity](../04-immunity-in-disease/hypersensitivity-and-allergy.md)). Absence of antibody (X-linked agammaglobulinaemia) brings recurrent infection with encapsulated bacteria from the second half of infancy.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fab and Fc | Antigen-binding arms and constant stem of an antibody |
| Isotype | Antibody class (IgM, IgG, IgA, IgE, IgD) set by the heavy-chain constant region |
| Class switching | Changing isotype while keeping the same binding site |
| Neutralisation | Blocking a pathogen's or toxin's attachment by binding its surface |
| Opsonisation | Coating a particle so that phagocytes bind it through Fc or complement receptors |
| T-dependent and T-independent antigens | Proteins that recruit helper T cells, and polysaccharides that do not |
| Conjugate vaccine | Capsular sugar linked to a carrier protein to obtain T-cell help |
| Germinal centre | Lymphoid follicle site of somatic hypermutation and affinity maturation |
| Plasma cell | Terminally differentiated B cell secreting antibody |
| Monoclonal antibody | Antibody of a single specificity produced from one B-cell clone |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Antibodies kill microbes."** They mark and block; killing is done by complement, phagocytes and NK cells reading the Fc tail.
- **"A positive antibody test means current infection."** IgG persists for years after infection or vaccination; only IgM, a rising titre or direct detection of the organism indicates present infection.
