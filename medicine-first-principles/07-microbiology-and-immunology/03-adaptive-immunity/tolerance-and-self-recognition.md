# Tolerance and self-recognition

## Essence

A system that makes receptors at random will make receptors against its own body; the price of being able to recognise anything is the risk of recognising yourself. Tolerance is the set of mechanisms that find and disarm self-reactive lymphocytes: deletion in the thymus and bone marrow before they mature, and in the periphery a layered defence of ignorance, anergy, deletion and active suppression by regulatory T cells. It must also cover things that are not self but must be lived with: food, the commensal flora and the fetus. Every layer leaks, so self-reactive cells are present in every healthy person; disease arises when enough layers fail at once.

## Core tenets

1. **Self-reactivity is the default output of a random repertoire.** Roughly half to three-quarters of newly made B cells in human bone marrow bind self-antigens (Wardemann et al., 2003), and a comparable fraction of thymocytes bind self peptide-MHC strongly enough to be dangerous. Tolerance is therefore the main activity of lymphocyte development: most cells ever made are destroyed for recognising self.

2. **Central tolerance deletes or edits the worst offenders before release.** In the thymus, strong binding to self peptide-MHC triggers apoptosis; the transcription factor AIRE makes thymic medullary cells express insulin, thyroid, adrenal and other tissue proteins so that they too can be tested against ([T cells](t-cells.md)). In bone marrow, immature B cells that bind self are deleted, made anergic or given a second chance by rearranging a new light chain (receptor editing). Central tolerance is necessarily incomplete: not every protein is expressed in the thymus, and cells of low affinity slip through.

3. **Peripheral tolerance is layered, and anergy is its first layer.** A mature T cell that meets its antigen on a resting tissue cell, without co-stimulation, is switched off rather than on ([antigen presentation](antigen-presentation-and-mhc.md)); in health self-antigen is presented constantly in exactly this way. Some antigens are simply never seen (immunological ignorance: the lens, the testis, parts of the brain), which is why trauma to one eye can trigger an attack on both (sympathetic ophthalmia). Repeatedly stimulated cells are deleted through the Fas death pathway. Inhibitory receptors, CTLA-4 and PD-1, raise the threshold for activation; antibodies that block them for cancer therapy cause colitis, thyroiditis and diabetes, a direct demonstration of what these receptors normally prevent.

4. **Tolerance is dominant and active: regulatory T cells enforce it.** About 5 to 10% of CD4 T cells express FOXP3 and suppress other lymphocytes by consuming interleukin-2, secreting interleukin-10 and transforming growth factor beta, and stripping co-stimulatory molecules from dendritic cells. Mutation of FOXP3 (the IPEX syndrome) causes fatal multi-organ autoimmunity in infancy, and transferring regulatory cells prevents autoimmunity in animals. Medawar and colleagues (1953) first showed that tolerance is acquired, by making mice accept foreign skin grafts after exposure to the donor's cells before birth.

5. **The mucosa tolerates what it eats and what lives there.** The gut presents the largest antigen load in the body: food proteins and ~10^13 bacteria. It answers with a dedicated programme: dendritic cells conditioned by retinoic acid and transforming growth factor beta induce regulatory T cells rather than effectors, Immunoglobulin A (IgA) coats microbes without inflaming, and the epithelium keeps bacteria at a distance ([the human microbiome](../01-microbes-and-infection/the-human-microbiome.md)). Early oral exposure to peanut reduces later peanut allergy; failure of mucosal tolerance to gluten is coeliac disease and to commensals is part of inflammatory bowel disease.

6. **The fetus is a tolerated allograft.** Half of its genes, and so half of its Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) molecules, are foreign to the mother. Trophoblast cells at the interface express no classical class I or class II molecules (removing the targets), express the non-classical HLA-G (which inhibits Natural Killer cells), and the decidua is rich in regulatory T cells and in enzymes that starve T cells of tryptophan ([pregnancy and the placenta](../../06-physiology/09-reproduction-and-the-life-course/pregnancy-placenta-and-lactation.md)). Where tolerance is incomplete, maternal antibody crosses the placenta and attacks the fetus: haemolytic disease of the newborn from anti-D, neonatal Graves' disease or myasthenia from maternal autoantibodies.

7. **Innate cells also recognise self, by markers rather than by learning.** Complement regulators (factor H, CD55, CD59) mark host surfaces as protected; MHC class I tells NK cells to hold fire; CD47 and sialic acids tell macrophages not to eat. Loss of these markers is read as non-self, and tumours over-express CD47 to escape phagocytosis.

8. **Tolerance breaks when several layers fail together.** Infection supplies co-stimulation to a tissue presenting self-antigen; a microbial peptide may resemble a self-peptide (molecular mimicry: rheumatic fever after streptococcal pharyngitis, Guillain-Barré syndrome after *Campylobacter*); tissue damage releases sequestered antigens and, through inflammation, exposes new epitopes; drugs modify self-proteins into neoantigens; inherited variants in HLA, CTLA4, PTPN22 and other checkpoint genes lower the threshold. One failure is usually survivable, which is why autoimmunity is multifactorial and why the same trigger affects only some people ([autoimmunity](../04-immunity-in-disease/autoimmunity.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Central tolerance | Deletion, editing or anergy of self-reactive lymphocytes during development in thymus or marrow |
| Peripheral tolerance | Mechanisms restraining mature self-reactive cells in the tissues |
| AIRE | Transcription factor driving expression of tissue antigens in the thymus |
| Anergy | Functional unresponsiveness after antigen without co-stimulation |
| Immunological ignorance | Self-antigen in sites lymphocytes do not normally reach |
| Regulatory T cell (Treg) | FOXP3-expressing CD4 cell that suppresses other lymphocytes |
| Oral tolerance | Systemic unresponsiveness induced by feeding an antigen |
| Molecular mimicry | Microbial antigen resembling self and so breaking tolerance |
| Markers of self | Innate signals (complement regulators, MHC class I, CD47) that protect host cells |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Healthy people have no self-reactive lymphocytes."** They have many; they are anergic, suppressed or ignorant rather than absent, and low-titre autoantibodies are common in healthy blood.
- **"Tolerance is the immune system ignoring something."** Much of it is active suppression by regulatory cells; remove them and autoimmunity follows within weeks.
