## Essence

A virus is a genome in a protein coat, sometimes wrapped in a membrane stolen from its last host cell. It has no ribosomes, makes no energy and cannot replicate alone; it is a set of instructions that a living cell is tricked into executing. The receptor it binds decides which cells it enters and so which illness it causes. The only selective drug targets are the few enzymes it brings itself, so antivirals are narrow and few. And because many viruses copy their genomes without proofreading, they evolve faster than anything else we fight.

## Core tenets

1. **A virus is an obligate intracellular parasite with a minimal parts list.** The particle (virion) is 20 to 300 nm across: a Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) or Ribonucleic Acid (RNA) genome, single- or double-stranded, of a few thousand to a few hundred thousand bases, a protein capsid, and in enveloped viruses a lipid bilayer carrying glycoprotein spikes. Outside a cell a virion is inert; inside, it redirects transcription, translation and membrane traffic to make copies of itself.

2. **Genome type dictates the replication strategy and the drug targets.** The Baltimore classification sorts viruses by the route from genome to messenger RNA. Double-stranded DNA viruses (herpesviruses, papillomaviruses) replicate in the nucleus using mostly host enzymes. Positive-sense RNA genomes (coronaviruses, poliovirus, hepatitis C) are themselves messenger RNA and are translated at once. Negative-sense RNA viruses (influenza, measles, Ebola) must carry their own RNA polymerase, because the cell has none that copies RNA from RNA. Retroviruses (Human Immunodeficiency Virus, HIV) reverse-transcribe RNA into DNA and integrate it into the host chromosome, becoming a permanent part of the cell's genome. Every enzyme a virus must bring is a potential selective target.

3. **The receptor decides the disease.** Entry begins when a viral surface protein binds a host molecule that evolved for something else: HIV binds CD4 plus the chemokine receptor CCR5, so it destroys helper T cells; Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) binds Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme 2 (ACE2), which is dense in the nasal and alveolar epithelium; influenza binds sialic acid, whose linkage differs between birds and humans and forms part of the species barrier. People homozygous for a CCR5 deletion are resistant to HIV.

4. **The replication cycle is a sequence of steps, each interruptible.** Attachment, entry, uncoating, genome replication and transcription, protein synthesis, assembly, and release by budding (enveloped) or lysis (non-enveloped). Antivirals map onto these steps: entry, polymerase (nucleoside analogues such as aciclovir, which a herpes enzyme activates only in infected cells), protease, integrase and release inhibitors. Combination therapy for HIV works because escape would need simultaneous mutations at several steps.

5. **Infection has four possible outcomes, and herpesviruses show all of them.** *Lytic* infection kills the cell and releases virions. *Latent* infection parks the genome (in neurons for herpes simplex and varicella zoster, in B cells for Epstein-Barr virus) with almost no protein expression, invisible to T cells, and reactivates when immunity falls (shingles decades after chickenpox). *Chronic* infection produces virus for years (hepatitis B and C, HIV). *Transforming* infection drives the cell toward cancer: papillomavirus E6 and E7 proteins disable p53 and retinoblastoma protein; roughly 10 to 15% of human cancers worldwide are attributable to viruses. Latency means that immunosuppression always carries the risk of reactivation.

6. **RNA viruses evolve fast because they copy without proofreading.** Viral RNA polymerases make roughly one error per 10^4 to 10^5 bases, a thousand to a million times more than cellular DNA replication, so a single infected person carries a cloud of variants (a quasispecies). *Antigenic drift* accumulates point mutations in surface proteins so that last year's antibodies bind less well; *antigenic shift* in influenza swaps whole genome segments between strains co-infecting one animal, producing a virus to which nobody has memory and a pandemic. Measles has one stable serotype because its receptor-binding protein tolerates little change, so one vaccine protects for life.

7. **The host's main antiviral weapons are interferons and killer cells; viruses counter both.** Infected cells sense viral nucleic acid and secrete type I interferons that put neighbours into an antiviral state; Natural Killer (NK) and cytotoxic T cells destroy infected cells before they finish assembling virions; antibodies neutralise free virus between cells ([cytokines, interferons and NK cells](../02-innate-immunity/cytokines-interferons-and-nk-cells.md), [T cells](../03-adaptive-immunity/t-cells.md)). Large DNA viruses carry genes that block interferon signalling, remove Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) class I from the cell surface and mimic cytokine receptors: a viral genome is a catalogue of the defences it has had to evade.

8. **Prions are the limiting case: infection without a genome.** A misfolded prion protein templates the misfolding of its normal counterpart, propagating a shape rather than a sequence. Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease shows that transmissibility needs only a self-replicating pattern, and that an agent with no nucleic acid is immune to every antiviral and most sterilisation.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Virion | The complete infectious particle: genome, capsid and (if present) envelope |
| Envelope | Host-derived lipid bilayer with viral glycoproteins; makes the virus fragile to soap and drying |
| Tropism | The range of cells a virus can infect, set mainly by receptor use |
| Baltimore classification | Grouping of viruses by genome type and the route to messenger RNA |
| Reverse transcriptase | Retroviral enzyme that copies RNA into DNA for integration |
| Latency | Persistence of the viral genome with minimal expression and no virion production |
| Antigenic drift | Gradual mutation of surface antigens that erodes existing immunity |
| Antigenic shift | Reassortment of genome segments producing a novel antigenic combination |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Viruses are tiny bacteria."** They have no metabolism, no ribosomes and no wall; nothing that kills bacteria touches them.
- **"If you recover, the virus is gone."** Herpesviruses, hepatitis B and HIV persist for life; recovery means control, not clearance.
