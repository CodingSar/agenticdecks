## Essence

A human body carries about as many bacterial cells as human ones, most of them in the colon, plus fungi, archaea and a vast population of bacteriophages. This community, the microbiome, is not a contaminant but an organ acquired at birth and shaped by diet, antibiotics and age. It digests what we cannot, makes vitamins and signalling molecules, trains the immune system and, most importantly, occupies every niche so that pathogens cannot. The clinical consequences follow from that last job: the commonest serious infections arise from our own flora when it is displaced into the wrong place or a weakened host, and the commonest cause of a dangerous shift in the community is an antibiotic.

## Core tenets

1. **The microbiome is large, dense and mostly in the colon.** Estimates put the bacterial population at ~4 x 10^13 cells, roughly equal to the number of human cells (Sender, Fuchs and Milo, 2016), with the colon holding ~10^11 cells per gram of contents, almost all strict anaerobes. The collective bacterial gene count exceeds the human genome's ~20,000 protein-coding genes by two orders of magnitude, so most of the metabolic repertoire in a human body is bacterial. Each body site has its own community: anaerobes in the colon, lactobacilli in the vagina, staphylococci and propionibacteria on skin, streptococci in the mouth.

2. **The community is acquired, then shaped by diet, antibiotics and age.** The fetus is close to sterile; colonisation begins at birth (vaginal delivery seeds with maternal gut and vaginal flora, caesarean with skin flora), is moulded by breast milk, whose oligosaccharides feed bifidobacteria rather than the infant, and settles into an adult-like composition by about three years. Diet selects continuously: fibre feeds fermenters, a Western diet thins diversity. A course of broad-spectrum antibiotics collapses diversity within days; recovery takes weeks to months and may be incomplete.

3. **Colonisation resistance is the microbiome's main protective function.** Resident bacteria exclude invaders by occupying adhesion sites, consuming nutrients, keeping the lumen anaerobic and acidic, and secreting bacteriocins. The paradigm is *Clostridioides difficile*: its spores are ingested by many people harmlessly, but after antibiotics strip the competing anaerobes it germinates, produces toxins and causes colitis. Transplanting a healthy person's faecal community cures recurrent infection in roughly 80 to 90% of cases, the clearest proof that the community itself is the defence.

4. **Microbial metabolism feeds the host and modifies its drugs.** Colonic bacteria ferment dietary fibre into short-chain fatty acids: butyrate is the main fuel of colonocytes and a signal that expands regulatory T cells, propionate and acetate reach the liver. They synthesise vitamin K and some B vitamins, convert primary bile acids into secondary ones that regulate lipid and glucose metabolism, and metabolise drugs (gut bacteria inactivate digoxin and activate or deactivate several others), so the same dose can behave differently in different people.

5. **The immune system is educated by, and tolerates, its microbes through containment rather than ignorance.** Germ-free animals have shrunken lymphoid tissue, few Immunoglobulin A (IgA)-producing cells and skewed T-cell responses; specific commensals induce specific T-cell subsets. Tolerance of this enormous antigenic load is achieved by keeping it at arm's length: a mucus layer, antimicrobial peptides, secretory IgA that coats bacteria without inflaming, and epithelial and dendritic cells that read commensal signals as "calm". When the barrier breaks, the same bacteria become targets ([tolerance and self-recognition](../03-adaptive-immunity/tolerance-and-self-recognition.md)).

6. **Most infections are endogenous.** Urinary infection is usually the patient's own gut *Escherichia coli*; peritonitis after a perforated bowel is colonic flora; surgical-site infection is skin *Staphylococcus*; pneumonia in a ventilated patient is aspirated oral flora; bacteraemia in a neutropenic patient is translocated gut flora. Pathogenicity here is a matter of location and host state, not of the organism's species.

7. **Dysbiosis is easy to observe and hard to prove causal.** Altered communities are reported in inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, type 2 diabetes, liver disease and depression. But disease, diet and drugs alter the microbiome, so most of these are associations; causation has been shown convincingly in animals and for a few human conditions (*C. difficile*, some antibiotic-associated effects). The field's lesson is the one from [causal inference](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/02-inference-and-causation/causal-inference.md): a difference in composition is a hypothesis, not a mechanism.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Microbiome | The community of microbes (and their genes) living on and in a host |
| Commensal | An organism that lives on the host without harming it |
| Colonisation resistance | Exclusion of pathogens by the resident community |
| Dysbiosis | A community shifted from its usual composition; a description, not a diagnosis |
| Short-chain fatty acids | Acetate, propionate and butyrate: fermentation products that feed and signal to the host |
| Secretory IgA | Dimeric antibody secreted across mucosa that coats microbes without triggering inflammation |
| Faecal microbiota transplantation | Transfer of a healthy donor's stool community to restore colonisation resistance |
| Germ-free animal | One raised without any microbes; the experimental baseline for what the microbiome does |
| Endogenous infection | Infection by the patient's own flora displaced or unchecked |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Bacteria outnumber human cells ten to one."** The older figure was a rough guess; current estimates put the ratio near 1:1, which is still remarkable.
- **"A probiotic restores the microbiome."** Commercial strains rarely colonise and can delay recovery of the native community after antibiotics; the strongest evidence for restoring a community is faecal transplantation for *C. difficile*.
- **"Dysbiosis causes the diseases it is found in."** In most conditions the direction of causation is unknown; illness, diet and drugs all change the flora.
