# Cytokines, interferons and NK cells

## Essence

Immune cells coordinate by secreting small proteins, cytokines, that act on receptors of nearby cells and tell them what kind of threat has been found and what to do about it. Cytokines are the immune system's language: a few hundred words, each with several meanings depending on the listener, spoken locally and briefly, and amplified into systemic effects (fever, shock) only when the threat is large. Interferons are the cytokines of antiviral defence: an infected cell warns its neighbours to shut down the machinery a virus needs. Natural Killer (NK) cells are lymphocytes that need no prior exposure and kill cells that have stopped displaying the normal marks of self, which catches viruses and tumours that hide from T cells.

## Core tenets

1. **Cytokines are local, potent, pleiotropic and redundant.** They act at picomolar concentrations through high-affinity receptors (most through the JAK-STAT pathway), mostly on the cell that made them or its immediate neighbours, and are cleared within minutes to hours. One cytokine acts differently on different cells (interleukin-6 makes B cells secrete antibody, hepatocytes make C-reactive protein and the hypothalamus raise temperature), and several cytokines can produce the same effect. The family names (interleukins, interferons, tumour necrosis factor, chemokines) are historical, not functional. Redundancy is why blocking one cytokine often has a modest, specific effect rather than switching immunity off ([signalling and information](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/03-systems-and-models/information-signals-and-noise.md)).

2. **The first cytokines made decide the type of response.** Innate cells read the threat and emit a cytokine that commits the response to one of three programmes. Interleukin-12 and interferon-gamma drive *type 1* immunity (macrophage activation, cytotoxic cells) against intracellular bacteria and viruses. Interleukin-4, -5 and -13 drive *type 2* immunity (Immunoglobulin E (IgE), eosinophils, mast cells) against helminths. Interleukin-17 and -23 drive *type 3* immunity (neutrophil recruitment, antimicrobial peptides at epithelia) against extracellular bacteria and fungi. Interleukin-10 and transforming growth factor beta restrain all three. Each programme has a matching helper [T-cell](../03-adaptive-immunity/t-cells.md) subset, and each, misapplied, is a disease: type 2 is allergy, type 3 is psoriasis, type 1 is much organ-specific autoimmunity.

3. **Interferons are an infected cell's warning to its neighbours.** Any cell that senses viral nucleic acid secretes type I interferon (alpha and beta). On neighbouring cells it induces hundreds of interferon-stimulated genes that degrade RNA, halt translation and raise Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) class I expression so that infected cells are easier for T cells to see; it also activates NK cells. The response is in place within hours, long before antibody, and its importance shows in its failures: inborn errors of type I interferon signalling, or autoantibodies that neutralise interferon, were found in roughly 10 to 15% of patients with life-threatening COVID-19 in 2020 studies. Interferon-gamma (type II) is a different molecule from T cells and NK cells that activates macrophages; interferon-lambda (type III) protects epithelial surfaces. Interferons cause the aches of viral illness, which is why interferon therapy feels like influenza.

4. **NK cells recognise missing self and stressed self.** They carry inhibitory receptors for MHC class I and activating receptors for molecules that cells display when stressed, infected or transformed. A healthy cell shows MHC class I and no stress ligands, so inhibition wins. A cell that has lost MHC class I, as many virus-infected and tumour cells do to escape T cells, loses the brake and is killed by perforin and granzymes delivered into its membrane. NK cells thus close the escape route that evading T cells opens. They also carry a receptor for the Fc tail of IgG and so kill antibody-coated cells (antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity), a bridge exploited by therapeutic monoclonal antibodies. NK deficiency presents as overwhelming herpesvirus infection.

5. **Chemokines are positional information.** About 50 small cytokines and 20 receptors tell cells where to go: a gradient of CXCL8 draws neutrophils to a wound, CCL19 and CCL21 draw dendritic and naive T cells into lymph node T-cell zones, CXCL13 draws B cells into follicles. Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) uses the chemokine receptor CCR5 as its co-receptor.

6. **Systemic effects are the local programme at scale.** Interleukin-1, interleukin-6 and tumour necrosis factor reaching the hypothalamus raise the temperature set point through prostaglandin E2 ([thermoregulation](../../06-physiology/01-integrative-principles/thermoregulation-and-energy-balance.md)); fever modestly impairs microbial growth and speeds immune reactions, at a metabolic cost of ~10% per degree. The same cytokines drive the acute-phase response, cause the fatigue and anorexia of illness and, over months, the wasting of chronic infection and cancer (cachexia, for which tumour necrosis factor was first named). Released everywhere at once, they make endothelium leak and vessels dilate throughout the body: septic shock and the cytokine release syndrome after T-cell therapies are cytokine storms.

7. **Blocking a cytokine both treats disease and reveals its natural job.** Antibodies against tumour necrosis factor (rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn's disease), interleukin-6, interleukin-1 (periodic fevers), interleukin-17 and -23 (psoriasis), interleukin-4 and -13 (atopic dermatitis, asthma) and IgE, and small molecules that block JAK kinases, are the [biologics](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/biologics-and-advanced-therapies.md) that transformed inflammatory disease. Each blockade exposes the cytokine's role: anti-tumour necrosis factor reactivates latent tuberculosis because that cytokine holds granulomas together; anti-interleukin-17 causes mucosal candidiasis because interleukin-17 defends epithelia against fungi.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cytokine | Secreted protein that signals between immune and other cells |
| JAK-STAT | Kinase and transcription-factor pathway used by most cytokine receptors |
| Type 1, 2 and 3 immunity | The three effector programmes: intracellular, helminth and extracellular/fungal |
| Type I interferon | Interferon alpha and beta: the antiviral warning made by any infected cell |
| Natural Killer (NK) cell | Innate lymphocyte that kills cells lacking MHC class I or showing stress ligands |
| Missing self | NK recognition principle: absence of MHC class I removes inhibition |
| Antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity | NK killing of a cell coated with IgG, via the Fc receptor CD16 |
| Cytokine storm | Systemic cytokine release causing vasodilation, capillary leak and organ failure |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Fever is harmful and should always be lowered."** It is a regulated rise in set point that aids defence; it is treated for comfort and in those who cannot afford the metabolic cost, not because it is damage.
- **"Blocking an inflammatory cytokine suppresses the immune system wholesale."** Redundancy keeps most defence intact; the specific gaps (tuberculosis with anti-tumour necrosis factor, candidiasis with anti-interleukin-17) map to the cytokine's specific job.
