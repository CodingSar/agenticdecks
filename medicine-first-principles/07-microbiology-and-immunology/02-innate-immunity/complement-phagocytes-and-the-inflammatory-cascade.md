# Complement, phagocytes and the inflammatory cascade

## Essence

Once a microbe is recognised, the innate system must deliver killers to it. Two systems do this. Complement, ~30 plasma proteins activated by cascading proteolysis, coats the microbe for eating, punches holes in it and releases fragments that summon cells. Phagocytes (blood neutrophils, tissue macrophages) engulf what is coated and destroy it with oxidants and enzymes. Getting them from blood to tissue requires the vessel wall to change: it dilates, leaks plasma and becomes sticky, and these vascular changes are the redness, heat, swelling and pain of inflammation. Inflammation is a delivery mechanism, not a disease, and it ends only by an active programme of resolution.

## Core tenets

1. **Complement attacks every surface unless the surface proves it is self.** The alternative pathway starts spontaneously: C3 hydrolyses at a low rate in plasma and deposits C3b on any nearby surface. Host cells carry regulators (factor H, CD55, CD59) that strip it off; microbes do not, so deposition amplifies into a C3 convertase that coats the microbe within minutes. The classical pathway (antibody bound to a surface recruits C1q) and lectin pathway (mannose-binding lectin on microbial sugars) start the same cascade on demand. Red cells that lose CD55 and CD59 (paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria) are lysed in the bloodstream.

2. **Complement has three outputs: opsonisation, recruitment and lysis.** Surface-bound C3b is recognised by complement receptors on phagocytes, so a coated microbe is eaten far faster than a naked one; this is complement's main job. The small fragments C3a and C5a diffuse away as anaphylatoxins: they attract neutrophils and make mast cells release histamine. The terminal components C5b to C9 assemble the membrane attack complex, a pore that lyses thin-walled organisms. Deficiencies reveal which output matters: C3 deficiency gives severe pyogenic infection, terminal-component deficiency only recurrent *Neisseria* infection, and C1q, C2 or C4 deficiency a lupus-like disease because immune complexes and apoptotic debris go uncleared.

3. **Neutrophils are the disposable first responders; macrophages are the resident managers.** The marrow makes ~10^11 neutrophils a day; each lives hours in blood, arrives within hours of injury and dies on the job. Tissue macrophages (Kupffer cells, alveolar macrophages, microglia) are long-lived, sense damage first, release the alarm cytokines, and later clear debris and direct repair. A neutrophil count below 0.5 x 10^9 per litre makes bacterial and fungal infection from the patient's own flora almost inevitable.

4. **Phagocytes kill with oxygen radicals and enzymes inside a sealed compartment.** Receptors for C3b and for the Fc tail of antibody trigger engulfment into a phagosome, which fuses with lysosomes. NADPH oxidase then dumps electrons onto oxygen to make superoxide, hydrogen peroxide and, via myeloperoxidase, hypochlorous acid (bleach); proteases and defensins finish the job at low pH. Chronic granulomatous disease, where the oxidase is missing, causes recurrent abscesses with *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Aspergillus*, showing that the oxidative burst is the decisive weapon.

5. **Recruitment is a stepwise adhesion cascade on activated endothelium.** Tumour necrosis factor and interleukin-1 make endothelial cells express selectins; passing neutrophils roll on them. Chemokines presented on the endothelium then switch the neutrophil's integrins to a high-affinity state, which bind ICAM-1 and arrest the cell. It squeezes between endothelial cells and crawls up gradients of C5a, leukotriene B4 and interleukin-8 to the source. In leukocyte adhesion deficiency, with defective integrins, neutrophils crowd the blood but cannot leave it and pus never forms; antibodies against integrins (used in multiple sclerosis and Crohn's disease) act on the same step.

6. **The cardinal signs are the vascular changes needed for delivery.** Histamine, bradykinin and prostaglandins dilate arterioles (redness, heat) and open gaps between venular endothelial cells so that protein-rich plasma leaks out (swelling), because the oncotic gradient that retains fluid is lost ([microcirculation](../../06-physiology/03-cardiovascular-system/microcirculation-and-capillary-exchange.md)). Bradykinin and prostaglandin E2 sensitise nociceptors (pain). The exudate brings complement, antibody and fibrinogen, which clots to wall off the site. Macrophage tumour necrosis factor and interleukin-1 sit at the top of the hierarchy, switching on the endothelium and the lipid mediators made from membrane arachidonic acid (prostaglandins via cyclo-oxygenase, leukotrienes via lipoxygenase), which is where cyclo-oxygenase inhibitors and corticosteroids act.

7. **Inflammation has systemic signs because cytokines reach the liver, brain and marrow.** Interleukin-6 drives the liver to make C-reactive protein, fibrinogen and hepcidin (which locks iron away: the anaemia of chronic inflammation); interleukin-1 and tumour necrosis factor raise the hypothalamic set point (fever); colony-stimulating factors release immature neutrophils from marrow. C-reactive protein and the neutrophil count are the clinician's readout. The same cytokines in bulk, activating endothelium everywhere at once, are septic shock ([hemorrhage, shock and sepsis](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/hemorrhage-shock-and-sepsis.md)).

8. **Resolution is active, and failure to resolve is chronic inflammation.** Neutrophils undergo apoptosis and are eaten by macrophages; that act switches the macrophage from a killing to a repairing programme (interleukin-10, transforming growth factor beta) and lipid mediators change from prostaglandins to lipoxins and resolvins that stop further recruitment. If the stimulus persists (an unkillable organism, a foreign body, an autoantigen), macrophages and lymphocytes replace neutrophils, granulomas may form, and repair lays down scar ([chronic inflammation and granulomas](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/chronic-inflammation-and-granulomas.md), [tissue repair and fibrosis](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/tissue-repair-regeneration-and-fibrosis.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Complement | Plasma protease cascade that opsonises, recruits and lyses |
| Alternative pathway | Spontaneous, continuous complement activation checked only by host regulators |
| Opsonin | Molecule (C3b, Immunoglobulin G (IgG), mannose-binding lectin) that coats a particle to promote phagocytosis |
| Anaphylatoxin | C3a or C5a: fragments that recruit cells and degranulate mast cells |
| Membrane attack complex | C5b-9 pore that lyses susceptible organisms |
| Respiratory burst | NADPH-oxidase generation of reactive oxygen species inside the phagosome |
| Adhesion cascade | Selectin rolling, chemokine activation, integrin arrest, transmigration |
| Acute-phase response | The liver's cytokine-driven change in plasma proteins (C-reactive protein up, albumin down) |
| Resolution | The active programme that ends inflammation and starts repair |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Inflammation is the disease."** It is the delivery system for defence and repair; disease arises when it is misdirected, excessive or fails to resolve.
- **"Complement's job is to lyse bacteria."** Lysis matters only for *Neisseria*; the main functions are opsonisation and recruitment, which is why C3 deficiency is far worse than C9 deficiency.
- **"Pus means the body is losing."** Pus is dead neutrophils that have done their work, walled off because resolution failed; it needs drainage, not alarm.
