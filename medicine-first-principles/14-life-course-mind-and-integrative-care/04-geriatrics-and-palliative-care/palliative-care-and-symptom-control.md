## Essence

Palliative care is medicine whose target is the person's experience rather than the disease's course: the relief of pain, breathlessness, nausea, delirium and distress in people with life-limiting illness, from diagnosis to death. It began in cancer (the modern hospice movement, Saunders, 1967) and now applies as much to heart failure, lung disease, kidney failure, dementia and frailty, which together kill more people than cancer along slower and less predictable trajectories. Its method is the ordinary method of medicine applied to symptoms: identify the mechanism of this symptom in this patient and act on it, while attending to the psychological, social and spiritual components that Saunders named "total pain". The unifying idea is **symptom as a mechanism to be reversed, not a sign to be interpreted**, together with the finding that doing this early improves quality of life and sometimes length of it.

## Core tenets

1. **Palliative care is not the care of the last days; started early, it improves outcomes.** Worldwide an estimated 57 million people a year need palliative care and about one in seven receive it (World Health Organization, 2020). A randomised trial in metastatic lung cancer (Temel, 2010) found that early palliative care alongside oncology improved quality of life and mood, reduced aggressive end-of-life treatment and was associated with longer survival. The explanation is probably better symptom control, earlier recognition of deterioration and decisions that avoid harmful treatment ([harm and benefit](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#harm-and-benefit)). The same logic applies in heart failure, chronic lung disease and kidney failure, where palliative care runs in parallel with disease treatment, not after it.

2. **Illness follows one of three trajectories, and prognosis is hardest where it matters most.** Cancer typically holds function until a steep decline over weeks to months; organ failure (heart, lung, kidney, liver) follows a saw-tooth of exacerbations and partial recoveries, any of which may be the last; frailty and dementia decline slowly over years with a low plateau ([time-courses](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#time-courses); [etiology, pathogenesis and natural history](../../08-pathology/05-disease-as-process/etiology-pathogenesis-and-natural-history.md)). Clinicians systematically over-estimate survival, and the "surprise question" (would I be surprised if this person died in the next year?) is a crude but useful trigger for planning ([prognosis and shared decision-making](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/01-the-clinical-encounter/prognosis-and-shared-decision-making.md)). Uncertainty is itself communicated: hoping for the best while preparing for the worst is a clinical plan.

3. **Pain is treated by mechanism, and the opioid is the model drug.** Nociceptive pain from tissue damage responds to anti-inflammatory drugs and opioids; neuropathic pain from nerve injury (burning, shooting, allodynia) responds poorly to opioids and better to drugs acting on neuronal excitability (gabapentinoids, tricyclics, serotonin–noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors); bone pain responds to anti-inflammatories and to a single fraction of radiotherapy ([pain](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/pain.md); [radiotherapy and radiation biology](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/04-non-drug-therapeutics/radiotherapy-and-radiation-biology.md)). Opioids act on mu receptors in the dorsal horn, brainstem and limbic system to reduce both the transmission and the affective component of pain; given regularly by the clock to match a half-life rather than on demand, with breakthrough doses, they control most cancer pain ([elimination, half-life and dosing](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/01-pharmacokinetics/elimination-half-life-and-dosing.md)). Their adverse effects follow receptor distribution: constipation (gut mu receptors, to which tolerance does not develop, so a laxative is always co-prescribed), nausea (chemoreceptor trigger zone, which does tolerate), sedation and respiratory depression (which tolerate quickly and are rare when the dose is titrated to pain, since pain itself is a respiratory stimulant). Renal failure accumulates morphine's active metabolite, so a different opioid is chosen ([tolerance, dependence and withdrawal](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/02-pharmacodynamics/tolerance-dependence-and-withdrawal.md)).

4. **Breathlessness is a perception, and it is treated as one when the cause cannot be.** Dyspnoea arises when the brainstem's respiratory drive outstrips the achievable ventilation and this mismatch reaches consciousness ([control of breathing](../../06-physiology/04-respiratory-system/control-of-breathing.md)). Reversible causes (effusion, anaemia, bronchospasm, infection, heart failure) are treated when consistent with goals; the residual symptom responds to low-dose opioids, which blunt the central perception of air hunger without clinically important respiratory depression, to a hand-held fan directed at the face (trigeminal cooling reduces perceived breathlessness), to positioning, pacing and anxiety management. Oxygen helps only if the patient is hypoxaemic; otherwise moving air works as well.

5. **Nausea is routed through identifiable receptors, so the antiemetic is chosen by cause.** The vomiting centre receives input from the chemoreceptor trigger zone (drugs, metabolites such as calcium and urea; dopamine D2 and serotonin 5-HT3 receptors), the vestibular system (movement; histamine H1 and muscarinic receptors), the gut (distension, obstruction, chemotherapy; 5-HT3 and D2), and the cortex (anxiety, raised intracranial pressure) ([motility and its neural control](../../06-physiology/06-gastrointestinal-system/motility-and-its-neural-control.md); [specificity and recognition](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#specificity-and-recognition)). A D2 antagonist for drug- or metabolite-induced nausea, a 5-HT3 antagonist for chemotherapy, an antihistamine or antimuscarinic for vestibular or obstructive causes, a corticosteroid for raised intracranial pressure: the prescription is the mechanism.

6. **Cachexia is not starvation and feeding does not reverse it.** Cancer cachexia and the wasting of end-stage heart, lung and kidney disease are driven by tumour- and host-derived cytokines that raise resting energy expenditure, break down muscle through the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway and suppress appetite centrally ([nutritional disease, obesity and malnutrition](../../08-pathology/04-genetic-metabolic-and-environmental-disease/nutritional-disease-obesity-and-malnutrition.md); [cytokines, interferons and NK cells](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/02-innate-immunity/cytokines-interferons-and-nk-cells.md)). Artificial nutrition does not restore muscle or prolong life in advanced cancer and adds burden; what helps is treating reversible contributors (nausea, mouth problems, depression) and releasing the patient and family from the obligation to eat. The same reasoning governs artificial hydration in the last days.

7. **A few emergencies in advanced disease are reversible if recognised within hours.** Malignant spinal cord compression (back pain, then weakness, sensory level and sphincter disturbance) is treated with corticosteroids and urgent radiotherapy or surgery before paralysis fixes; hypercalcaemia of malignancy (confusion, thirst, constipation, from tumour-derived parathyroid-hormone-related peptide or bone resorption) is reversed by fluids and bisphosphonates ([calcium and bone mineral metabolism](../../06-physiology/07-endocrine-system/calcium-and-bone-mineral-metabolism.md)); superior vena cava obstruction, malignant bowel obstruction and catastrophic haemorrhage each have a plan made in advance. Palliative care is not the absence of acute medicine.

8. **Who else is involved.** Palliative care is embedded in [oncology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/07-oncology/README.md) and shares patients with [cardiology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/README.md) (advanced heart failure, deactivating defibrillators), [pulmonology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/02-pulmonology/README.md) (end-stage lung disease, breathlessness), [nephrology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/03-nephrology/README.md) (conservative kidney management instead of dialysis), [neurology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/README.md) (motor neuron disease, advanced Parkinson disease, dementia), [gastroenterology and hepatology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/04-gastroenterology-and-hepatology/README.md) (end-stage liver disease, malignant obstruction), [geriatrics](frailty-multimorbidity-and-polypharmacy.md), [paediatrics](../01-paediatrics/README.md) (children's palliative care), [anaesthesia](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/README.md) and pain medicine (nerve blocks, intrathecal drugs), [surgery](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/README.md) and interventional radiology (stents, drainage), [psychiatry](../03-psychiatry/README.md) (depression, delirium), and [primary care](../05-primary-care-and-integration/README.md) and community nursing, who deliver most palliative care at home.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Palliative care | Care aimed at quality of life in life-limiting illness, from diagnosis onward, not only at the end |
| Total pain | Saunders' concept: pain as physical, psychological, social and spiritual at once |
| Illness trajectory | The typical shape of functional decline: cancer, organ failure or frailty |
| Surprise question | "Would I be surprised if this patient died within a year?" as a trigger for planning |
| Breakthrough pain | Pain that exceeds background control, treated with an additional dose |
| Opioid rotation | Changing to a different opioid when one produces toxicity or inadequate relief |
| Neuropathic pain | Pain from nerve injury, with burning, shooting or allodynia, poorly opioid-responsive |
| Chemoreceptor trigger zone | A brainstem area outside the blood–brain barrier sensing emetogenic chemicals |
| Cachexia | Cytokine-driven wasting with raised energy expenditure, not reversed by feeding |
| Malignant spinal cord compression | Metastatic compression of the cord, reversible only before fixed deficit |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Opioids given for pain cause addiction and hasten death."** In pain, titrated opioids rarely cause addiction and, because pain stimulates breathing, rarely cause respiratory depression; properly used they do not shorten life.
- **"Palliative care means stopping treatment."** It runs alongside disease-directed treatment, and some of its interventions (radiotherapy, surgery, antibiotics, fluids) are the same tools used to a different end.
- **"A dying patient who does not eat is starving and should be fed."** Cachexia and the reduced intake of dying are cytokine-driven and physiological; artificial nutrition does not reverse them and adds burden.
