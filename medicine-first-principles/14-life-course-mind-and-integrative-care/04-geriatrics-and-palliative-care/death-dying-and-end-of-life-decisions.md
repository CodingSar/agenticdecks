# Death, dying and end-of-life decisions

## Essence

Every patient dies, and in high-income countries most now die slowly, of chronic disease, in old age, in a hospital or care home, after a series of decisions about which treatments to start, continue and stop. Medicine has made death a process to be recognised and managed rather than an event to be witnessed: the dying phase has a physiology and signs, death itself has two legal definitions (circulatory and neurological) that technology forced apart, and the decisions around it (resuscitation, withdrawal of life support, advance directives, assisted dying) are where clinical judgement, the patient's values and the law meet most sharply. The unifying idea is **that the goal of care changes before the body fails**: good dying follows from recognising that the trajectory has turned and shifting from prolonging life to the quality of what remains, and most harm at the end of life comes from recognising it too late.

## Core tenets

1. **Dying has a recognisable physiology.** In the last days of a slow death the patient becomes bed-bound and increasingly drowsy, stops eating and then drinking, swallows poorly (so oral drugs are converted to subcutaneous), breathes irregularly with periods of apnoea (Cheyne–Stokes breathing, from a brainstem whose response to carbon dioxide has become sluggish, [control of breathing](../../06-physiology/04-respiratory-system/control-of-breathing.md)), accumulates upper-airway secretions that rattle, and shows peripheral cyanosis and mottling as perfusion is centralised ([hemorrhage, shock and sepsis](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/hemorrhage-shock-and-sepsis.md)). Terminal delirium is common. These signs distress the family more than the patient; recognising them lets the team stop futile observations and treatments, prescribe in anticipation for pain, breathlessness, secretions and agitation, and bring the family in.

2. **Death is defined twice, because machines separated the heart from the brain.** Circulatory death is the irreversible cessation of circulation, confirmed by absence of pulse, heart sounds and breathing over an observation period. Neurological death is irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness and for breathing from destruction of the brainstem (the United Kingdom's brainstem criterion) or of the whole brain (most other jurisdictions), confirmed by absent brainstem reflexes and apnoea despite a rising carbon dioxide in a patient with a known cause and no reversible contributor (hypothermia, sedatives, metabolic derangement) ([higher functions](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/higher-functions-memory-language-and-consciousness.md); [cell injury, adaptation and death](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/cell-injury-adaptation-and-death.md)). A ventilated body with a dead brainstem has a beating heart and warm skin; the definition exists because that body is dead and because organs can be retrieved from it for transplant ([transplant and tumour immunology](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/04-immunity-in-disease/transplant-and-tumour-immunology.md)).

3. **Resuscitation is a treatment with an indication, and for most dying people it has none.** Cardiopulmonary resuscitation restarts a heart that has stopped suddenly from a reversible cause; in hospital overall about 15–20% of patients survive to discharge, and in those with advanced cancer, multi-organ failure or frailty the figure is close to zero, with rib fractures, hypoxic brain injury and a death in an intensive care unit as the likely outcomes ([resuscitation and cardiac arrest](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/resuscitation-and-cardiac-arrest.md)). A decision not to attempt resuscitation is therefore a clinical judgement that the treatment will not work, discussed with the patient or family but not requiring their consent in most jurisdictions, and it concerns only that one intervention. It is increasingly embedded in broader emergency-care plans that record which treatments the person would and would not want.

4. **Withholding and withdrawing treatment are ethically and (in most legal systems) legally equivalent, and neither is killing.** A treatment that cannot achieve a goal the patient values may be stopped: ventilation, dialysis, artificial nutrition, antibiotics, a defibrillator. The patient then dies of the underlying disease, which is the ethical distinction from active killing ([medical ethics](../../15-medicine-in-society/02-ethics-law-and-the-profession/medical-ethics.md)). Most deaths in intensive care follow such a decision, made with the family when the patient cannot participate ([organ support and ICU syndromes](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/03-critical-care/organ-support-and-icu-syndromes.md)). The difficulty is practical, not conceptual: families often experience withdrawal as the act that causes death, and clinicians delay the conversation because prognosis is uncertain and the conversation is hard.

5. **The patient decides while they can and plans for when they cannot.** A person with capacity may refuse any treatment, including one that will keep them alive. Advance care planning records values, preferred place of care and specific refusals; an advance directive (living will) is a legally binding refusal of specified treatments in specified circumstances; a lasting power of attorney for health appoints someone to decide; and when none exists, decisions are made in the patient's best interests, reconstructing what they would have wanted ([consent, capacity and confidentiality](../../15-medicine-in-society/02-ethics-law-and-the-profession/consent-capacity-and-confidentiality.md)). Capacity is decision-specific and fluctuates in the dying, so the conversations happen early, which is the strongest practical reason for the "surprise question" of [palliative care](palliative-care-and-symptom-control.md).

6. **Relieving suffering does not shorten life, and when it might, intention is what the law weighs.** Opioids and sedatives titrated to symptoms do not hasten death in observational studies; the "double effect" doctrine, that a foreseen but unintended shortening of life is permissible when the intention is relief, is invoked more often than the pharmacology requires ([tolerance, dependence and withdrawal](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/02-pharmacodynamics/tolerance-dependence-and-withdrawal.md)). Palliative sedation, the deliberate lowering of consciousness for symptoms refractory to everything else in the last days, is proportionate and reversible in principle and is distinguished from euthanasia by intention, dose and the fact that the disease causes the death.

7. **Assisted dying is lawful in a growing minority of jurisdictions and draws the sharpest line in medical ethics.** Physician-assisted suicide (the patient takes a prescribed lethal drug) and euthanasia (the clinician administers it) are legal in the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg, Canada, several Australian states, New Zealand, Spain, Austria, Switzerland and some states of the United States, with eligibility ranging from terminal illness with months to live to unbearable suffering from any cause (status as of 2024; legislation is in flux elsewhere). The arguments for are autonomy and relief of suffering that palliative care cannot always achieve; the arguments against are the duty not to kill, the pressure on the vulnerable and the slippery slope that some jurisdictions' broadening criteria are cited as evidence of ([medical ethics](../../15-medicine-in-society/02-ethics-law-and-the-profession/medical-ethics.md)). The empirical questions, whether access to palliative care reduces requests and whether safeguards hold, are studied in the jurisdictions that permit it.

8. **After death there is certification, donation and grief.** The clinician certifies the cause of death as a causal chain, which becomes the mortality statistics on which [epidemiology](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/01-epidemiology/measures-of-frequency-and-association.md) rests; unexplained or unnatural deaths go to medico-legal investigation. Organ donation is raised with every suitable family. Grief is normal and raises the survivor's mortality for a time; prolonged grief disorder is the minority outcome needing treatment ([mood and anxiety disorders](../03-psychiatry/mood-and-anxiety-disorders.md)).

9. **Who else is involved.** [Critical care](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/03-critical-care/README.md) confirms neurological death and conducts most treatment withdrawals; [emergency medicine](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/README.md) decides about resuscitation in the moment; [oncology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/07-oncology/README.md), [cardiology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/README.md), [nephrology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/03-nephrology/README.md) and [neurology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/README.md) make the decision to stop disease-directed treatment; transplant teams act on donation; [psychiatry](../03-psychiatry/README.md) assesses capacity in doubt and treats complicated grief; [geriatrics](frailty-multimorbidity-and-polypharmacy.md) and [primary care](../05-primary-care-and-integration/README.md) hold most advance care planning and most deaths at home and in care homes; and the law, chaplaincy and the coroner stand outside medicine but inside the process.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Dying phase | The last days, recognisable by drowsiness, reduced intake, altered breathing and poor perfusion |
| Anticipatory prescribing | Drugs for pain, breathlessness, secretions and agitation prescribed in advance of need |
| Brainstem death | Irreversible loss of brainstem function with apnoea, legally death in the United Kingdom |
| Do-not-attempt-resuscitation decision | A clinical decision that cardiopulmonary resuscitation would not work or is not wanted; it concerns no other treatment |
| Withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment | Stopping a treatment that cannot achieve a goal the patient values; the disease then causes death |
| Advance directive | A legally binding refusal of specified treatments made while capacitous, for use when capacity is lost |
| Best interests | The standard for decisions on behalf of someone without capacity, reconstructing their wishes and values |
| Double effect | The principle that a foreseen but unintended harm is permissible when the act's intention is good |
| Palliative sedation | Proportionate lowering of consciousness for refractory symptoms in the last days |
| Assisted dying | Physician-assisted suicide or euthanasia, lawful under conditions in some jurisdictions |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A do-not-resuscitate decision means do not treat."** It concerns one intervention; antibiotics, fluids, surgery and intensive care may all still be appropriate, and a separate plan records them.
- **"Withdrawing a ventilator kills the patient."** The disease that made the ventilator necessary kills the patient; the clinician stops a treatment that no longer serves a goal, which is permissible and often obligatory.
- **"Morphine at the end of life hastens death."** Titrated to symptoms it does not; the belief leads to under-treatment of pain and breathlessness in the dying.
