## Essence

Psychiatry has no biopsy, no blood test and no scan that makes a diagnosis; its examination is the mental state, a structured observation of how a person appears, speaks, feels, thinks, perceives and understands, taken alongside a history that reaches back into childhood and outward into relationships and circumstances. Because its disorders are defined by patterns of experience and behaviour rather than by lesions, psychiatry replaces the single-cause diagnosis of much of medicine with a **formulation**: a hypothesis about why this person has developed this problem at this time, built from biological, psychological and social contributions and from what predisposed, precipitated and perpetuates it. The unifying idea is that **the brain's output is the examination**, and that the output is shaped by the brain, the mind's learned patterns and the world around it at once, so an explanation must work at all three levels without collapsing any into another.

## Core tenets

1. **The mental state examination is the neurological examination of the whole brain's output.** Appearance and behaviour, speech (rate, volume, form), mood (what the patient reports) and affect (what the examiner observes), thought form (the logic and flow of ideas) and content (preoccupations, delusions, suicidal ideas), perception (hallucinations), cognition (orientation, attention, memory) and insight are recorded in a fixed order ([higher functions](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/higher-functions-memory-language-and-consciousness.md)). Each domain maps loosely to systems that neuroscience studies, prefrontal executive control, limbic emotion, temporal memory, but the examination is deliberately descriptive: pressured speech with flight of ideas is recorded before it is called mania, so the data survive a change of theory.

2. **Diagnosis is syndromal, and reliability was bought before validity.** The International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) and the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM-5) define disorders by operational criteria (so many symptoms for so long with such impairment), introduced in the 1970s and 1980s so that two clinicians would agree ([measurement error and biological variation](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/01-probability-and-measurement/measurement-error-and-biological-variation.md)). This made research possible but does not guarantee that a category carves nature at a joint: symptoms overlap, comorbidity is the rule, genetic risk crosses diagnostic lines, and the same category contains people with different mechanisms. Dimensional approaches that score severity on continuous axes are gaining ground. A psychiatric diagnosis is therefore best read as a reliable description with a known prognosis and treatment response, not as the name of a lesion.

3. **Organic causes are excluded first, because they are treatable and because they mimic everything.** Delirium presents as psychosis or agitation, hypothyroidism and Cushing syndrome as depression, hyperthyroidism as anxiety, temporal lobe epilepsy and autoimmune encephalitis as psychosis, frontal tumours and dementia as personality change, and intoxication or withdrawal as almost anything ([geriatric syndromes](../04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/geriatric-syndromes.md); [addiction and substance use](addiction-and-substance-use.md); [neurodegenerative disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/neurodegenerative-disease.md)). Clouded consciousness, disorientation, visual hallucinations, a first presentation late in life and focal neurological signs each raise the probability of a physical cause. The cognitive part of the mental state and a physical examination are not optional.

4. **The biopsychosocial formulation is a causal model, not a slogan.** Engel's model (1977) states that disease at the level of the person emerges from interacting biological (genes, neurotransmitters, physical illness), psychological (temperament, learned beliefs, coping style) and social (relationships, work, housing, culture) systems. Operationalised as the four Ps, it asks what *predisposed* the person (family history, early adversity), what *precipitated* this episode (loss, illness, a drug), what *perpetuates* it (unemployment, avoidance, substance use, isolation) and what *protects* them (relationships, skills). The formulation yields the treatment plan: a perpetuating factor is a target, a protective one a resource. The levels are not reducible to each other: knowing the serotonin transporter genotype does not predict the effect of bereavement, and knowing the bereavement does not predict who becomes ill ([multifactorial disease and risk](../../08-pathology/05-disease-as-process/multifactorial-disease-and-risk.md)).

5. **Risk assessment is probabilistic and its base rates are low.** Suicide kills around 720,000 people a year worldwide (2021) and is a leading cause of death in the young; most people who die by suicide had contact with services in the preceding year. Risk factors (previous attempt, male sex, mental illness, substance use, recent discharge, access to means, hopelessness) raise probability, but suicide is rare enough that no score predicts it usefully at the individual level ([risk prediction and stratification](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/03-screening-and-prevention/risk-prediction-and-stratification.md); [probability and uncertainty](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#probability-and-uncertainty)). Assessment therefore aims at modifiable factors and a safety plan rather than at a prediction. Violence to others is similarly rare and more associated with substance use than with mental illness.

6. **Treatment acts on the formulation at every level, and the evidence base is the same as the rest of medicine.** Drugs act on receptors ([central nervous system drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/central-nervous-system-drugs.md)); psychotherapies act on learned patterns through mechanisms such as extinction of conditioned fear, re-appraisal of beliefs and the therapeutic relationship; social interventions act on housing, work and isolation. All are tested in randomised trials, where the placebo response is large because expectation, attention and the passage of time all improve symptoms ([behaviour change and the placebo effect](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/04-non-drug-therapeutics/behaviour-change-and-the-placebo-effect.md); [randomized controlled trials](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/02-evidence-based-medicine/randomized-controlled-trials.md)). Combined treatment usually outperforms either alone, which is itself evidence that the levels interact.

7. **Capacity and compulsion make psychiatry unique in law.** A person whose illness removes the ability to understand, retain, weigh and communicate a decision may be treated without consent, and one who is a danger to self or others may be detained under mental health legislation ([consent, capacity and confidentiality](../../15-medicine-in-society/02-ethics-law-and-the-profession/consent-capacity-and-confidentiality.md)). Capacity is decision-specific and the presumption is in its favour; insight, the patient's own view of whether they are ill, is a component of the mental state because it bears on both. The power to detain is the strongest in medicine and its justification is that the illness itself is what removes the freedom.

8. **Who else is involved.** [Primary care](../05-primary-care-and-integration/README.md) sees most mental illness and treats most of it; [neurology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/README.md) shares the organic boundary; [emergency medicine](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/README.md) receives self-harm and acute disturbance; liaison psychiatry works inside general hospitals where a third or more of inpatients have a comorbid mental disorder; and psychology, nursing, social work and occupational therapy deliver most of the non-drug treatment.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mental state examination | The structured observation of appearance, speech, mood, affect, thought, perception, cognition and insight |
| Affect | The observed, moment-to-moment emotional expression, as distinct from reported mood |
| Delusion | A fixed false belief, held against evidence and out of keeping with the person's culture |
| Hallucination | A perception without an external stimulus, in any modality |
| Insight | The person's recognition that their experiences are symptoms of illness and that treatment may help |
| Operational criteria | Explicit rules (symptom counts, durations, impairment) that define a diagnostic category |
| Formulation | The individualised causal hypothesis combining biological, psychological and social factors over time |
| Four Ps | Predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating and protective factors |
| Organic disorder | A psychiatric presentation caused by identifiable physical disease, drug or toxin |
| Capacity | The decision-specific ability to understand, retain, weigh and communicate a choice |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Psychiatric diagnoses are not real because there is no test."** Reliability, prognostic value and treatment response make them useful; the absence of a biomarker reflects the complexity of the organ, not the absence of disease. The same was true of epilepsy before the electroencephalogram.
- **"Mental illness is a chemical imbalance."** Neurotransmitter changes are part of the mechanism of some disorders and of most drug effects, but no disorder has been reduced to a single transmitter, and psychological and social treatments change the brain too.
- **"Risk assessment predicts suicide."** It identifies modifiable factors; the base rate is too low for any instrument to predict the individual, and false reassurance from a low score is a documented harm.
