## Essence

Depression and anxiety are the commonest mental disorders and, by years lived with disability, among the largest causes of ill health in the world. Both are exaggerations of adaptive states: low mood and withdrawal conserve resources after loss, and fear and vigilance protect against threat. They become disorders when the state is disproportionate, persistent and self-sustaining, when the brain's stress, reward and threat systems settle into a stable abnormal configuration maintained by biology, by learned patterns of thought and avoidance, and by circumstances. Bipolar disorder adds the opposite pole, mania, and a stronger genetic signature. The unifying idea is **a maladaptive stable state**: effective treatments, whether drug, psychological or social, work by perturbing the state enough for the system to settle elsewhere.

## Core tenets

1. **Depression is a syndrome of mood, drive, cognition and body, and it is common.** Persistent low mood or loss of pleasure for at least two weeks, with changes in sleep, appetite, energy, concentration, self-worth and thoughts of death, defines a major depressive episode. Lifetime prevalence is roughly 10–20% in high-income countries, about twice as high in women, and the condition recurs in most people who have had one episode. Somatic symptoms (pain, fatigue, insomnia) are often the presenting complaint in primary care, where most depression is seen and much is missed ([history taking and communication](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/01-the-clinical-encounter/history-taking-and-communication.md)).

2. **Monoamines are part of the mechanism, but the time course shows they are not the whole of it.** Antidepressants raise synaptic serotonin or noradrenaline within hours, yet clinical benefit takes weeks, so the therapeutic event is downstream: altered receptor sensitivity, increased brain-derived neurotrophic factor, restored synaptic density and hippocampal plasticity, and a shift in the processing of emotional information measurable within days ([synaptic transmission and neurotransmitters](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/synaptic-transmission-and-neurotransmitters.md); [central nervous system drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/central-nervous-system-drugs.md)). Ketamine, an N-methyl-D-aspartate antagonist, relieves depression within hours through a glutamatergic route, confirming that monoamines are one entrance to a common pathway. Chronic stress with sustained cortisol ([thyroid, adrenal and stress hormones](../../06-physiology/07-endocrine-system/thyroid-adrenal-and-stress-hormones.md)) and raised inflammatory cytokines are associated with depression and are plausible routes from the social to the biological.

3. **Depression has a cognitive and behavioural architecture that psychotherapy targets.** Negative biases in attention, memory and interpretation, a triad of beliefs about self, world and future, rumination and withdrawal from rewarding activity each maintain the state ([higher functions](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/higher-functions-memory-language-and-consciousness.md)). Cognitive behavioural therapy tests and revises the beliefs and schedules activity; its effect size in mild to moderate depression is similar to that of medication, and combining the two is more effective than either ([randomized controlled trials](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/02-evidence-based-medicine/randomized-controlled-trials.md)). The number needed to treat with an antidepressant for response in moderate to severe depression is roughly 7–9 against placebo, with a placebo response near 40%, which is why antidepressants help clearly in severe illness and marginally in mild.

4. **Electroconvulsive therapy is the most effective treatment for severe depression and the least understood.** A generalised seizure under anaesthesia produces remission in most patients with severe, psychotic or treatment-resistant depression, faster than any drug; its mechanism is probably a broad resetting of network connectivity and neurotrophic signalling. Its main adverse effect is memory loss around the course. Its continued use rests on trial evidence, not tradition ([harm and benefit](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#harm-and-benefit)).

5. **Bipolar disorder is a disorder of mood regulation with a strong genetic basis and a specific drug.** Mania (elevated or irritable mood, reduced need for sleep, pressured speech, grandiosity, disinhibition) alternating with depression affects about 1–2% of people; heritability is around 60–85%, the highest among the common mental disorders ([complex traits and heritability](../../04-genetics-evolution-and-development/01-inheritance/complex-traits-and-heritability.md)). Lithium, acting on inositol signalling and glycogen synthase kinase-3, prevents both poles and reduces suicide, with a narrow therapeutic index that makes blood level monitoring mandatory ([dose-response and therapeutic index](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/02-pharmacodynamics/dose-response-and-therapeutic-index.md)). Antidepressants given alone can precipitate mania, so a history of elevated mood is sought before any depressed patient is treated.

6. **Anxiety disorders are the threat system stuck on, and avoidance is what keeps it on.** The amygdala-centred fear circuit, conditioned by experience and modulated by the prefrontal cortex, generates the physiology of fear: tachycardia, hyperventilation, sweating, muscle tension ([the autonomic nervous system](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/the-autonomic-nervous-system.md)). In panic disorder these sensations are catastrophically misinterpreted (a racing heart as a heart attack), producing a positive feedback loop; in phobias and social anxiety a conditioned cue triggers it; in generalised anxiety the threat is diffuse and worry is the behaviour; in obsessive-compulsive disorder intrusive thoughts and neutralising rituals cycle through cortico-striato-thalamic loops; in post-traumatic stress disorder a memory is stored in a form that replays as if present. In every case avoidance prevents the learning that the feared outcome does not occur, so exposure, which allows extinction, is the common active ingredient of effective psychotherapy. Serotonergic antidepressants help all of these; benzodiazepines relieve symptoms quickly through GABA-A potentiation but produce tolerance and dependence within weeks ([tolerance, dependence and withdrawal](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/02-pharmacodynamics/tolerance-dependence-and-withdrawal.md)).

7. **Mood disorders are physical risk factors.** Depression roughly doubles the risk of coronary heart disease and worsens the outcome of diabetes, stroke and cancer, through inflammation, autonomic imbalance, poor self-care and non-adherence ([ischemic heart disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/ischemic-heart-disease.md); [chronic disease and self-management](../05-primary-care-and-integration/chronic-disease-and-self-management.md)). People with severe mental illness die 10–20 years early, mostly of cardiovascular and respiratory disease. Treating the depression is part of treating the heart.

8. **Who else is involved.** [Primary care](../05-primary-care-and-integration/README.md) diagnoses and treats most mood and anxiety disorders; [endocrinology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/05-endocrinology/README.md) is consulted for thyroid, adrenal and other mimics; [cardiology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/README.md) and [emergency medicine](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/README.md) see panic as chest pain and overdose as its consequence; [obstetrics](../02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/README.md) shares perinatal depression; [neurology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/README.md) shares post-stroke and Parkinson depression and functional disorders; [oncology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/07-oncology/README.md) and [palliative care](../04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/README.md) distinguish depression from appropriate sadness in the dying; and anaesthesia delivers electroconvulsive therapy.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Anhedonia | Loss of the capacity for pleasure; with low mood, a core symptom of depression |
| Neurotrophic hypothesis | The proposal that depression involves loss of synaptic plasticity and that treatments restore it |
| Cognitive triad | Negative beliefs about the self, the world and the future that maintain depression |
| Number needed to treat | Patients treated for one additional good outcome relative to control |
| Mania | A sustained state of elevated mood, energy and disinhibition with impaired judgement |
| Lithium | A mood stabiliser with a narrow therapeutic index that prevents relapse and reduces suicide |
| Panic attack | An abrupt surge of fear with autonomic symptoms, often driven by catastrophic misinterpretation |
| Exposure | Deliberate, graded contact with a feared stimulus so that conditioned fear extinguishes |
| Rumination | Repetitive, passive focus on distress and its causes, a maintaining factor in depression |
| Treatment-resistant depression | Depression that has not responded to adequate trials of two or more treatments |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Antidepressants do not work better than placebo."** In moderate to severe depression they do, modestly and consistently across large meta-analyses; in mild depression the margin is small and psychological treatment is a reasonable first choice.
- **"Anxiety is treated by avoiding the trigger."** Avoidance is the engine of the disorder; treatment is exposure.
- **"Depression is sadness."** Sadness is a feeling; depression is a syndrome of mood, drive, cognition and body that persists without a proportionate cause and impairs function.
