## Essence

Addiction is compulsive use of a substance or pursuit of a behaviour despite harm, with loss of control over it. Tobacco kills about 8 million people a year, alcohol about 2.6 million (2019), and opioid overdose became the leading cause of death in young adults in the United States in the 2010s; together they outweigh most other preventable causes of death. The brain is the organ: drugs of dependence hijack the mesolimbic dopamine system that evolved to mark food, sex and safety as worth repeating, and with repetition the pursuit shifts from a choice the prefrontal cortex can veto to a habit it struggles to. Pharmacology explains tolerance and withdrawal; neuroscience explains why wanting outlasts liking; and the social environment explains who starts, who stops and who dies. The unifying idea is **learning taken over**: the drug teaches the brain to value it above its own interests.

## Core tenets

1. **Every drug of dependence raises dopamine in the nucleus accumbens, by a different route.** Stimulants block or reverse the dopamine transporter; opioids disinhibit dopamine neurons by acting on mu receptors on inhibitory interneurons in the ventral tegmental area; nicotine excites them through alpha4beta2 nicotinic receptors; alcohol acts on GABA-A, glutamate and opioid systems; cannabis through CB1 receptors ([synaptic transmission and neurotransmitters](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/synaptic-transmission-and-neurotransmitters.md)). The dopamine surge is larger and faster than any natural reward produces, and the faster the delivery (smoking, injection) the more addictive the drug: pharmacokinetics decides abuse liability ([absorption and bioavailability](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/01-pharmacokinetics/absorption-and-bioavailability.md)).

2. **Dopamine teaches rather than pleases, so wanting outlasts liking.** Dopamine encodes the difference between expected and received reward and so drives learning about what predicts it ([signalling and information](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#signalling-and-information)). A drug that always produces a dopamine surge never stops being "better than expected", so cues associated with it (places, people, paraphernalia, mood states) acquire ever stronger pull. With repetition control passes from the ventral striatum (goal-directed) to the dorsal striatum (habitual), while the prefrontal regions that weigh consequences become less active. The result is craving triggered by cues, use that continues after pleasure has faded, and relapse years after withdrawal has ended.

3. **Tolerance and withdrawal are the pharmacology of adaptation, and they make the drug a necessity.** Chronic receptor stimulation is met by receptor down-regulation and compensatory changes in the opposing systems ([tolerance, dependence and withdrawal](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/02-pharmacodynamics/tolerance-dependence-and-withdrawal.md); [feedback and homeostasis](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#feedback-and-homeostasis)). Remove the drug and the compensation stands exposed: alcohol withdrawal is unopposed glutamate and deficient GABA (tremor, autonomic overactivity, seizures, delirium tremens, which can kill); opioid withdrawal is noradrenergic rebound from the locus coeruleus (misery, but rarely fatal); benzodiazepine withdrawal resembles alcohol's. Negative reinforcement, using to escape withdrawal and the lowered mood of an adapted reward system, joins positive reinforcement as a driver. Withdrawal is treated by substituting a longer-acting agonist and tapering, which is why a long-acting benzodiazepine such as chlordiazepoxide is used for alcohol withdrawal.

4. **Substitution and antagonism are the two pharmacological strategies, and both have trial evidence.** Methadone (a full mu agonist) and buprenorphine (a high-affinity partial agonist that blocks other opioids and has a ceiling on respiratory depression) stabilise opioid dependence and roughly halve mortality in cohort studies ([receptor theory, agonists and antagonists](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/02-pharmacodynamics/receptor-theory-agonists-and-antagonists.md)). Nicotine replacement and varenicline (a partial agonist at alpha4beta2) substitute for tobacco; naltrexone blocks opioid and, in alcohol, reward; acamprosate dampens glutamatergic hyperactivity; disulfiram makes drinking aversive by blocking acetaldehyde dehydrogenase. Naloxone, a competitive antagonist, reverses opioid overdose within minutes and is distributed to users for that reason. None works without the behavioural and social scaffolding that keeps the person in treatment.

5. **Vulnerability is heritable, developmental and social.** Heritability of dependence is around 50%; variants in alcohol-metabolising enzymes protect (the flushing aldehyde dehydrogenase variant common in East Asia) and variants in nicotinic receptor genes predispose ([pharmacogenomics and individual variation](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/02-pharmacodynamics/pharmacogenomics-and-individual-variation.md)). Use beginning in adolescence, when the prefrontal cortex is immature, is far more likely to become dependence ([adolescent health and safeguarding](../01-paediatrics/adolescent-health-and-safeguarding.md)). Trauma, mental illness, poverty, availability and price all shape who uses: taxation and minimum pricing are among the most effective interventions for alcohol and tobacco at population level, which places much of addiction medicine in public health ([social determinants and health inequity](../../15-medicine-in-society/03-populations-and-the-future/social-determinants-and-health-inequity.md)).

6. **Addiction is a chronic relapsing condition and is managed as one.** Relapse rates after treatment are comparable to the rates of non-adherence and relapse in hypertension, asthma and diabetes, so a return to use is a signal to adjust treatment, not evidence that treatment fails ([chronic disease and self-management](../05-primary-care-and-integration/chronic-disease-and-self-management.md)). Harm reduction (needle exchange, supervised consumption, take-home naloxone, drug checking) accepts continued use and prevents the deaths and infections that would otherwise occur, and the evidence for it is strong. Brief motivational interventions in primary care and emergency departments reduce hazardous drinking ([behaviour change and the placebo effect](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/04-non-drug-therapeutics/behaviour-change-and-the-placebo-effect.md)).

7. **The physical harm is organ-specific and often the presenting complaint.** Alcohol damages the liver ([liver disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/04-gastroenterology-and-hepatology/liver-disease.md)), pancreas, heart, brain (Wernicke–Korsakoff from thiamine deficiency, cerebellar degeneration) and fetus, and is a carcinogen; tobacco causes a third of cancers and most chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and much vascular disease; injected drugs transmit hepatitis C and HIV and cause endocarditis and abscesses ([hiv and chronic viral infections](../../12-organ-system-specialties/08-infectious-diseases/hiv-and-chronic-viral-infections.md)); stimulants cause arrhythmia, hypertension and stroke; opioids kill by respiratory depression ([poisoning and overdose](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/poisoning-and-overdose.md)). Much of addiction is first seen by the hepatologist, the respiratory physician or the surgeon.

8. **Who else is involved.** [Gastroenterology and hepatology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/04-gastroenterology-and-hepatology/README.md), [pulmonology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/02-pulmonology/README.md), [cardiology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/README.md) and [infectious diseases](../../12-organ-system-specialties/08-infectious-diseases/README.md) treat the organ damage; [emergency medicine](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/README.md) and [critical care](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/03-critical-care/README.md) manage overdose and severe withdrawal; [anaesthesia](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/README.md) and pain medicine manage the tolerant patient's analgesia and the iatrogenic dependence that pain prescribing creates; [obstetrics](../02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/README.md) and neonatology manage use in pregnancy and neonatal abstinence; [primary care](../05-primary-care-and-integration/README.md) delivers most brief intervention, smoking cessation and substitution prescribing; and public health sets price and availability.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Dependence | A state of adaptation in which withdrawal follows cessation; necessary but not sufficient for addiction |
| Addiction (substance use disorder) | Compulsive use with loss of control and continued use despite harm |
| Mesolimbic pathway | Dopamine projection from the ventral tegmental area to the nucleus accumbens, the reward-learning circuit |
| Reward prediction error | The dopamine signal encoding the gap between expected and received reward, which drives learning |
| Incentive sensitisation | Progressive strengthening of cue-triggered wanting independent of the pleasure obtained |
| Negative reinforcement | Use driven by relief of withdrawal or dysphoria rather than by pleasure |
| Delirium tremens | Severe alcohol withdrawal with confusion, hallucinations and autonomic storm; potentially fatal |
| Opioid substitution | Replacing a short-acting opioid with a long-acting agonist or partial agonist to stabilise and reduce harm |
| Harm reduction | Interventions that reduce the damage of continued use without requiring abstinence |
| Abuse liability | How readily a drug produces dependence, largely set by speed of onset and route |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Addiction is a failure of willpower."** It is a learned reorganisation of valuation and control systems with a heritable vulnerability; willpower is one of the things it damages.
- **"Substitution treatment just swaps one addiction for another."** A long-acting oral agonist at a stable dose removes intoxication, withdrawal, injecting and crime, and halves mortality; the pharmacology, not the molecule class, is what matters.
- **"Withdrawal is the dangerous part of opioid dependence."** Opioid withdrawal is miserable but rarely fatal; overdose after loss of tolerance (on release from prison or detoxification) is what kills, which is why abrupt detoxification without follow-up raises mortality.
