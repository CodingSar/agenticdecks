## Essence

Three young specialties share one premise: that what a person does, where they work and how they live are exposures with dose–response relationships to disease, and that changing them is a treatment. Sports and exercise medicine studies the body under physical load, from the elite athlete's injury to the prescription of activity for the sedentary. Occupational medicine studies the workplace as an exposure, from asbestos and noise to shift work and stress, and the worker's fitness for the job. Lifestyle medicine addresses the handful of behaviours (tobacco, diet, inactivity, alcohol, sleep) that account for a large fraction of chronic disease. All three stand between the individual consultation and public health, and all three run into the same limit: behaviour is shaped by environment and circumstance more than by advice. The unifying idea is **exposure as a modifiable cause**, whether the exposure is a running shoe, a mine or a diet.

## Core tenets

1. **Physical activity has a steep dose–response curve with the biggest gains at the bottom.** Regular moderate activity (the order of 150 minutes a week, or any movement for the inactive) lowers all-cause mortality by around 20–30% in cohort studies, with the largest marginal benefit going from none to a little and diminishing returns above ([exercise and stress physiology](../../06-physiology/01-integrative-principles/exercise-and-stress-physiology.md); [dose-response](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#dose-response)). The mechanisms are many and measured: improved insulin sensitivity through muscle glucose uptake, lower blood pressure, higher high-density-lipoprotein cholesterol, reduced inflammation, preserved muscle and bone, and effects on mood comparable to antidepressants in mild depression. Physical inactivity is among the leading modifiable risk factors for death worldwide, and cardiorespiratory fitness predicts mortality better than most clinical variables. Exercise is prescribed, in dose and type, like a drug.

2. **Training is adaptation to overload, and injury is overload without adaptation.** Muscle, tendon, bone and the cardiovascular system remodel in response to load that exceeds their current capacity, provided recovery allows the remodelling ([bone remodelling and joints](../../06-physiology/10-musculoskeletal-system-and-skin/bone-remodelling-and-joints.md); [tissue repair, regeneration and fibrosis](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/tissue-repair-regeneration-and-fibrosis.md)). Overuse injuries (tendinopathy, stress fracture, shin pain) arise when load rises faster than tissue adapts, typically with a sudden increase in training; the treatment is load management, not rest, since tendon and bone need load to remodel ([rehabilitation and disability](rehabilitation-and-disability.md)). Acute injuries (ligament rupture, muscle tear) are mechanics exceeding tissue strength ([mechanics and materials](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/mechanics-and-materials.md)). The female athlete or any athlete in sustained energy deficit develops relative energy deficiency: suppressed reproductive hormones, bone loss, stress fracture ([reproductive endocrinology](../../06-physiology/07-endocrine-system/reproductive-endocrinology.md)).

3. **Sudden death in young athletes is structural or electrical heart disease unmasked by exertion.** Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, anomalous coronary arteries and ion-channel disorders cause the rare collapse of an apparently fit young person at an incidence of roughly 1 in 50,000 to 1 in 100,000 athlete-years ([arrhythmias](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/arrhythmias.md); [cardiac electrophysiology](../../06-physiology/03-cardiovascular-system/cardiac-electrophysiology.md)). Whether to screen athletes with an electrocardiogram is a classic screening dilemma: the condition is rare, the athlete's heart adapts in ways that mimic disease (physiological hypertrophy, bradycardia, repolarisation changes), and false positives end careers ([screening principles and biases](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/03-screening-and-prevention/screening-principles-and-biases.md)). Automated external defibrillators at sporting venues act on the event rather than the prediction. Concussion, a functional brain injury with no structural lesion on imaging, is managed by graded return and the emerging evidence on repeated head impacts and later neurodegeneration.

4. **Occupational disease is the cleanest natural experiment in causal inference.** Chimney sweeps' scrotal cancer (Pott, 1775), asbestos and mesothelioma, vinyl chloride and angiosarcoma of the liver, coal dust and pneumoconiosis, benzene and leukaemia: an exposure confined to a definable group with a rare outcome gives relative risks so large that confounding cannot explain them, and the Bradford Hill considerations (strength, consistency, dose–response, temporality, plausibility) were articulated for exactly this setting ([causal inference](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/02-inference-and-causation/causal-inference.md); [causes of cancer](../../08-pathology/03-neoplasia/causes-of-cancer.md); [environmental and toxic injury](../../08-pathology/04-genetic-metabolic-and-environmental-disease/environmental-and-toxic-injury.md)). Latency can be decades (mesothelioma 30–40 years after asbestos exposure), so the occupational history reaches back to the first job. Occupational asthma, contact dermatitis, noise-induced hearing loss ([the ear, hearing and balance](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/05-ophthalmology-and-ent/the-ear-hearing-and-balance.md)), musculoskeletal disorders and work-related stress are the common modern burden.

5. **Control follows a hierarchy, and the individual is the last resort.** Occupational hygiene ranks interventions: eliminate the hazard, substitute a safer one, engineer it away (ventilation, enclosure), control it administratively (rotation, limits), and only then protect the individual (respirators, hearing protection), because the measures at the top do not depend on behaviour. The same hierarchy applies to lifestyle risks: a smoke-free law or a sugar tax acts above the individual, where advice acts at the bottom ([harm and benefit](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#harm-and-benefit)). Fitness-for-work assessment runs the other way: given this worker's impairment, what can the job be adjusted to? Good work is itself good for health, and prolonged worklessness after illness carries a mortality risk comparable to smoking; rehabilitation that returns people to work is therefore treatment.

6. **A few behaviours explain much of chronic disease, and tobacco is the largest.** Tobacco kills about 8 million people a year (2019) and halves the life expectancy of long-term smokers; stopping at any age recovers much of the loss, and cessation support combining a drug (nicotine replacement, varenicline, bupropion) with behavioural support roughly doubles or triples quit rates ([addiction and substance use](../03-psychiatry/addiction-and-substance-use.md)). Diet (high energy density, salt, processed meat, low fruit and vegetables), alcohol, inactivity and short sleep ([sleep and circadian rhythms](../../06-physiology/01-integrative-principles/sleep-and-circadian-rhythms.md)) follow; together these behaviours account for a large share of cardiovascular disease, diabetes and several cancers ([obesity, lipids and metabolic syndrome](../../12-organ-system-specialties/05-endocrinology/obesity-lipids-and-metabolic-syndrome.md); [nutritional disease, obesity and malnutrition](../../08-pathology/04-genetic-metabolic-and-environmental-disease/nutritional-disease-obesity-and-malnutrition.md)). Intensive lifestyle intervention prevents or delays type 2 diabetes in people at high risk by about half over several years in trials.

7. **Behaviour is shaped by environment more than by knowledge, which is where lifestyle medicine meets its limit.** Advice alone changes behaviour little; structured programmes using goal-setting, self-monitoring, feedback and social support change it more; and changes to price, availability, default options and the built environment change it most and most equitably ([behaviour change and the placebo effect](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/04-non-drug-therapeutics/behaviour-change-and-the-placebo-effect.md)). Lifestyle risk is socially patterned: smoking, obesity and inactivity concentrate in deprivation, so framing them as individual choices without addressing the conditions that shape them widens inequality ([social determinants and health inequity](../../15-medicine-in-society/03-populations-and-the-future/social-determinants-and-health-inequity.md); [the host and its environment](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#the-host-and-its-environment)). The prevention paradox applies: shifting a whole population's distribution of risk a little prevents more disease than treating the extreme.

8. **Who else is involved.** Sports medicine shares patients with orthopaedics ([orthopaedics, fractures and joints](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/orthopaedics-fractures-and-joints.md)), [rheumatology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/09-rheumatology-and-clinical-immunology/README.md), [cardiology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/README.md) (the athlete's heart), [neurology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/README.md) (concussion) and physiotherapy; occupational medicine with [pulmonology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/02-pulmonology/README.md) (pneumoconiosis, occupational asthma), [dermatology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/11-dermatology/README.md), [ophthalmology and ENT](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/05-ophthalmology-and-ent/README.md) (hearing), [oncology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/07-oncology/README.md), [psychiatry](../03-psychiatry/README.md) (work stress, fitness for work) and the law of compensation; and lifestyle medicine is mostly delivered by [primary care](family-medicine-and-the-generalist.md), [endocrinology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/05-endocrinology/README.md), cardiac and pulmonary [rehabilitation](rehabilitation-and-disability.md), and public health.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cardiorespiratory fitness | Maximal oxygen uptake; a strong independent predictor of mortality |
| Overload principle | Tissue adapts to loads above its current capacity, given recovery |
| Overuse injury | Tissue failure from load rising faster than adaptation |
| Relative energy deficiency in sport | Hormonal, bone and performance consequences of sustained low energy availability |
| Athlete's heart | Physiological cardiac adaptation to training that can mimic cardiomyopathy |
| Latency | The interval between exposure and disease; decades for some occupational cancers |
| Bradford Hill considerations | Criteria (strength, consistency, dose–response, temporality, plausibility and others) for inferring causation from association |
| Hierarchy of controls | Elimination, substitution, engineering, administrative controls, personal protection, in that order |
| Fitness for work | Assessment of whether a worker's health permits a job, and what adjustments would |
| Prevention paradox | Population-wide small shifts in risk prevent more disease than treating the few at high risk |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Exercise is for the fit."** The largest reduction in mortality comes from moving the inactive to a little activity; the frail, the elderly and people with heart failure benefit most in absolute terms.
- **"Occupational disease belongs to history."** Asbestos deaths are still near their peak in several countries because of latency, and musculoskeletal disorders and work stress are the leading causes of sickness absence today.
- **"Lifestyle disease is a matter of personal choice."** Behaviour is patterned by price, availability, work, housing and stress; interventions on those change behaviour more than education does.
