## Essence

Layers 12, 13 and 14 present the specialties as a tree, but the specialties are a graph: a patient with diabetes is seen by primary care, endocrinology, ophthalmology, nephrology, vascular surgery, cardiology and podiatry, and the quality of their care depends less on any one node than on the edges between them. This leaf describes the real structure of that graph. Specialties are cut along five different axes (organ, process, life stage, setting and technique), which is why they overlap; patients move along a few recurring routes (referral, hand-over, shared care, the multidisciplinary meeting); territory is disputed wherever two axes cross the same patient; and the generalist sits at the hub because they are the only node connected to all the others. The unifying idea is **that medicine's division of labour is historical and administrative, not logical**, so its joints must be engineered deliberately, and most avoidable harm occurs at them.

## Core tenets

1. **Specialties are cut along five axes, and the overlaps follow from that.** *Organ-system* specialties ([layer 12](../../12-organ-system-specialties/README.md): [cardiology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/README.md), [pulmonology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/02-pulmonology/README.md), [nephrology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/03-nephrology/README.md), [gastroenterology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/04-gastroenterology-and-hepatology/README.md), [endocrinology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/05-endocrinology/README.md), [neurology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/README.md), [dermatology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/11-dermatology/README.md)) own a physiology. *Process* specialties cut across organs: [oncology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/07-oncology/README.md) (neoplasia anywhere), [infectious diseases](../../12-organ-system-specialties/08-infectious-diseases/README.md) (infection anywhere), [haematology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/06-haematology/README.md) (blood, which is everywhere), [rheumatology and clinical immunology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/09-rheumatology-and-clinical-immunology/README.md) (autoimmunity anywhere). *Life-stage* specialties ([paediatrics](../01-paediatrics/README.md), [obstetrics and gynaecology](../02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/README.md), [geriatrics](../04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/README.md)) own a body at a time of life. *Setting and time* specialties ([emergency medicine](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/README.md), [critical care](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/03-critical-care/README.md), [anaesthesia](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/README.md), palliative care, [primary care](README.md)) own a place or a phase. *Technique* specialties ([surgery](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/README.md) and its subspecialties, [ophthalmology and ENT](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/05-ophthalmology-and-ent/README.md), interventional radiology, and the diagnostic services of [layer 11](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/README.md)) own a method. [Psychiatry](../03-psychiatry/README.md) owns a domain, the mind, orthogonal to all of these. A lung cancer in a 78-year-old is simultaneously pulmonology (organ), oncology (process), geriatrics (life stage), thoracic surgery and radiotherapy (technique), palliative care (phase) and primary care (setting); no axis has priority, so who leads is a convention.

2. **The generalist–specialist relationship is a prevalence filter run by referral and return.** Primary care sees unselected illness at low prior probability and refers the fraction whose probability of serious or specialist-treatable disease has been raised ([family medicine and the generalist](family-medicine-and-the-generalist.md); [using tests, pre-test probability and thresholds](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/01-the-clinical-encounter/using-tests-pretest-probability-and-thresholds.md)). Referral has four purposes: diagnostic uncertainty, treatment beyond the referrer's scope, a procedure, and reassurance of patient or clinician. The specialist's population is therefore enriched, which is why their tests perform better and their intuitions about base rates fail in primary care, and vice versa. The return path matters as much: a specialist letter that states the diagnosis, the plan, what primary care should monitor and when to re-refer closes the loop; one that does not leaves the patient in orbit. Open access to secondary care without a generalist produces more imaging, more procedures and more fragmentation without better outcomes ([organisation and financing of health systems](../../15-medicine-in-society/01-health-systems/organisation-and-financing-of-health-systems.md)).

3. **Shared patients are the rule, and the paradigm cases show the routes.** The person with type 2 diabetes ([chronic disease and self-management](chronic-disease-and-self-management.md)) is held in primary care with endocrinology for complex glycaemia, [ophthalmology](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/05-ophthalmology-and-ent/the-eye-and-vision-loss.md) for retinal screening and laser, nephrology when albuminuria or falling filtration appear ([chronic kidney disease and dialysis](../../12-organ-system-specialties/03-nephrology/chronic-kidney-disease-and-dialysis.md)), vascular surgery and podiatry for the ischaemic or neuropathic foot, cardiology for the coronary disease, and obstetrics if pregnant. The older person with a hip fracture travels emergency medicine → anaesthesia and orthopaedic surgery → geriatrics (orthogeriatrics) → rehabilitation → primary care and social care, within a fortnight ([geriatric syndromes](../04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/geriatric-syndromes.md)). The patient with cancer travels primary care → imaging and histopathology → surgery → oncology and radiotherapy → palliative care, with primary care throughout ([principles of cancer care](../../12-organ-system-specialties/07-oncology/principles-of-cancer-care.md)). The pregnant woman with a repaired congenital heart defect is held jointly by obstetrics and cardiology with anaesthesia planning the birth and neonatology waiting ([pregnancy and its complications](../02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/pregnancy-and-its-complications.md)).

4. **The multidisciplinary team is where joint decisions are made, and it exists because single-specialty decisions were worse.** Cancer multidisciplinary meetings (surgeon, oncologist, radiologist, pathologist, specialist nurse, palliative care) became mandatory in the United Kingdom after the 1995 Calman–Hine report found that outcomes depended on which door the patient entered; the heart team (cardiologist, cardiac surgeon, imaging) decides between stenting, surgery and transcatheter valves; the stroke, trauma, transplant, cleft, neuromuscular and safeguarding teams follow the same model. The mechanism is that each specialty's bias (the surgeon's toward operating, the physician's toward not) is corrected by the others, and that the plan is made once rather than serially ([clinical reasoning and cognitive bias](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/01-the-clinical-encounter/clinical-reasoning-and-cognitive-bias.md)). The cost is time, and the failure mode is a meeting that discusses the scan without the patient's wishes or fitness in the room.

5. **Territory is disputed wherever two axes cross the same patient, and the disputes are informative.** Neurology versus psychiatry over functional disorders, dementia and epilepsy with psychosis; cardiology versus cardiac surgery over revascularisation and valves (now settled by the heart team); gastroenterology versus surgery over inflammatory bowel disease and gastrointestinal bleeding; interventional radiology versus vascular surgery over aneurysms and arterial disease; dermatology versus plastic surgery over skin cancer; orthopaedics versus rheumatology versus pain medicine over back pain and osteoarthritis; ENT versus respiratory medicine over sleep apnoea; endocrinology versus surgery over thyroid nodules; emergency medicine versus acute medicine versus geriatrics over the older medical admission; oncology versus palliative care over when disease-directed treatment stops; and paediatrics versus adult services over the 16- to 18-year-old. Each dispute marks a condition that needs two kinds of knowledge, and the stable solution is usually a joint clinic or a protocol stating who leads at which stage, not a victory.

6. **Hand-overs between the three specialty layers follow the patient's acuity.** Layer 12 hands to layer 13 when a medical patient needs an operation, an airway or organ support (the gastrointestinal bleed to the endoscopist and surgeon, the pneumonia to intensive care); layer 13 hands back to 12 or on to 14 when the acute phase ends (the intensive care survivor to rehabilitation, the post-operative older patient to geriatrics, everyone to primary care). Layer 14 hands to 12 when a life-stage patient develops an organ-system disease (the child with congenital heart disease becoming the adult congenital cardiology patient, the pregnant woman with lupus nephritis to nephrology) and to 13 in emergencies (obstetric haemorrhage to anaesthesia and critical care, the child's acute abdomen to surgery, the overdose to emergency medicine). Every route ends in primary care, which is the only node with an edge to all the others and the only one that persists across the patient's whole life ([the clinical method](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/README.md) is the common language at every hand-over).

```
                       PRIMARY CARE (hub: first contact, continuity, record)
                        /      |        |        |        \
   life stage (14): paediatrics   obstetrics   psychiatry   geriatrics/palliative
                        \      |        |        |        /
   organ & process (12): cardio  pulm  renal  GI  endo  haem  onc  ID  rheum  neuro  derm
                        \      |        |        |        /
   acute & technique (13): emergency -> anaesthesia/surgery -> critical care ; ophth/ENT
                                      \                 /
                           diagnostics (11): imaging, laboratory, pathology
   edges: referral & return letter | shared care | MDT | emergency transfer | transition
```

7. **The interfaces are where harm concentrates.** Hand-over is the point at which information is lost: a third or more of serious adverse events in hospital involve communication failure at transitions, medicines are changed by one prescriber without the knowledge of another (the main source of polypharmacy, [frailty, multimorbidity and polypharmacy](../04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/frailty-multimorbidity-and-polypharmacy.md)), a specialist's framing becomes diagnostic momentum that the next specialist inherits ([clinical reasoning and cognitive bias](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/01-the-clinical-encounter/clinical-reasoning-and-cognitive-bias.md)), and the patient with a problem that fits no specialty is passed around or dropped. The remedies are structural: a single shared record ([health informatics and artificial intelligence](../../15-medicine-in-society/01-health-systems/health-informatics-and-artificial-intelligence.md)), structured hand-over, medicines reconciliation at every transition, a named coordinator, and the generalist's standing responsibility ([patient safety and quality improvement](../../15-medicine-in-society/01-health-systems/patient-safety-and-quality-improvement.md)).

8. **The graph is shaped by history, technology and money, and it keeps changing.** Specialties arise when a technology creates a skill (endoscopy made gastroenterology procedural; the catheter made interventional cardiology; the ventilator made intensive care), when a body of knowledge grows past one person's grasp, or when a group organises (colleges, boards, payment codes); they are defined differently in different countries (anaesthesia includes intensive care in some, not others; geriatrics barely exists in some systems) ([professionalism, regulation and medical education](../../15-medicine-in-society/02-ethics-law-and-the-profession/professionalism-regulation-and-medical-education.md); [history of medicine](../../15-medicine-in-society/03-populations-and-the-future/history-of-medicine.md)). Sub-specialisation improves outcomes for the specific condition (volume–outcome relationships in surgery are robust) and worsens them for the multimorbid patient who needs someone to see the whole; the generalist, the hospitalist, the geriatrician and the multidisciplinary team are the counter-movement. Payment systems that reward procedures grow the technique specialties; those that reward populations grow primary care ([health economics and priority setting](../../15-medicine-in-society/01-health-systems/health-economics-and-priority-setting.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Axis of specialisation | The principle by which a specialty is cut: organ, process, life stage, setting or technique |
| Referral | Transfer of a question or a patient from one clinician to another, with return of the answer |
| Shared care | Formal division of a patient's management between two services with agreed responsibilities |
| Multidisciplinary team | A standing meeting of the specialties needed for a condition, making one joint plan |
| Transition | The planned move of a patient between services, classically from paediatric to adult care |
| Hand-over | Transfer of responsibility and information at a change of team, shift or setting |
| Medicines reconciliation | Verifying a patient's complete medication list at every transition of care |
| Diagnostic momentum | The tendency for a label, once attached, to persist unexamined through subsequent clinicians |
| Volume–outcome relationship | Better results for a procedure in centres and operators that perform more of it |
| Gatekeeping | The requirement that specialist access pass through primary care |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Specialties partition medicine."** They overlap along five axes; most patients with serious illness belong to several at once, and the organisation of their care is a design problem, not a given.
- **"More specialists means better care."** For a single condition, usually; for a person with several, fragmentation, polypharmacy and hand-over errors grow, and systems with more generalists have better population outcomes.
- **"The multidisciplinary meeting is bureaucracy."** It exists because outcomes depended on which specialist the patient happened to see first; the meeting corrects each specialty's bias with the others'.
