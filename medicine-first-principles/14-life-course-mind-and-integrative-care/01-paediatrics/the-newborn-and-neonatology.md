## Essence

Birth is the most abrupt physiological transition a human ever makes: in a few minutes an organism that obtained oxygen, glucose and warmth from the placenta must breathe, perfuse its own lungs, burn its own fuel and hold its own temperature. Neonatology is the medicine of that transition and of its failure, which is most likely when the infant arrives before its organs are ready (prematurity) or when the transition itself is interrupted (asphyxia, infection, malformation). The single idea is **readiness**: almost every neonatal disease is a normal fetal state that has outlasted its usefulness (fetal circulation, fetal haemoglobin, surfactant-poor lungs, immature liver and immune system) or an adult process arriving in a body with no reserve. Neonatal deaths (the first 28 days) are about 2.3 million a year worldwide (2022), close to half of all deaths under five, and most follow prematurity, intrapartum events and infection.

## Core tenets

1. **The first breath rewires the circulation, and failure to rewire is a disease.** In the fetus the lungs are fluid-filled with high vascular resistance, so blood bypasses them through the foramen ovale and ductus arteriosus ([fetal and neonatal physiology](../../06-physiology/09-reproduction-and-the-life-course/fetal-and-neonatal-physiology.md)). Lung expansion and rising oxygen tension drop pulmonary resistance, left atrial pressure rises and closes the foramen, and loss of placental prostaglandins plus oxygen constrict the ductus. If pulmonary resistance stays high (asphyxia, meconium aspiration, sepsis) blood keeps shunting right-to-left past the lungs: persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn, cyanosis that oxygen alone does not fix. Conversely, a duct-dependent heart lesion stays silent until the duct closes, so an infant who collapses at day 2 to 10 is a cardiology emergency, and a prostaglandin E1 infusion that reopens the duct is life-saving precisely because it reverses the transition.

2. **Surfactant is the rate-limiting molecule of prematurity.** Type II pneumocytes make enough surfactant only from about 34 weeks; without it surface tension collapses alveoli at every expiration ([ventilation and lung mechanics](../../06-physiology/04-respiratory-system/ventilation-and-lung-mechanics.md)), so the preterm infant works harder with each breath, grunts to generate its own end-expiratory pressure and tires. The chest radiograph shows diffuse ground-glass lungs with air bronchograms. Antenatal corticosteroids given to the mother accelerate surfactant synthesis, exogenous surfactant instilled into the trachea restores it, and continuous positive airway pressure splints the alveoli open; each acts on the same physical variable. Prolonged ventilation and oxygen then injure the developing lung (bronchopulmonary dysplasia), a cost of the rescue.

3. **A small body loses heat and runs out of fuel fast.** Surface-to-volume ratio scales inversely with size ([scale and allometry](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/scale-and-allometry.md)), a wet newborn loses heat by evaporation, and the only extra heat source is brown fat, which burns glucose and oxygen the infant may not have. Glycogen stores are small and a preterm or growth-restricted infant, or one whose mother's diabetes left it hyperinsulinaemic, becomes hypoglycaemic within hours. Hypothermia, hypoglycaemia and hypoxia reinforce each other, which is why drying, skin-to-skin contact and early feeding are the first interventions in every resuscitation algorithm.

4. **Neonatal jaundice is normal physiology on a narrow margin.** Fetal red cells are more numerous and shorter-lived, so bilirubin production is high, while hepatic glucuronidation (the enzyme UGT1A1) is immature, so most newborns are visibly jaundiced in the first week ([red cells, haemoglobin and iron](../../06-physiology/08-blood/red-cells-haemoglobin-and-iron.md); [the liver as metabolic hub](../../06-physiology/06-gastrointestinal-system/the-liver-as-metabolic-hub.md)). Unconjugated bilirubin is lipid-soluble and crosses the immature blood–brain barrier; at high concentrations it deposits in the basal ganglia (kernicterus). Jaundice in the first 24 hours is haemolysis until proved otherwise (blood-group incompatibility, [blood groups and transfusion compatibility](../../06-physiology/08-blood/blood-groups-and-transfusion-compatibility.md)); phototherapy works by photo-isomerising bilirubin into water-soluble forms excreted without conjugation. Conjugated jaundice persisting beyond two weeks is a different disease (biliary atresia) and a surgical one.

5. **The newborn immune system is borrowed and incomplete.** Protection comes from maternal Immunoglobulin G (IgG) transferred across the placenta, mostly in the third trimester, so preterm infants have little; the infant's own responses are slow and signs of sepsis are non-specific (temperature instability, poor feeding, apnoea). Group B streptococcus and *Escherichia coli* acquired from the birth canal cause early-onset sepsis, hospital organisms cause late-onset sepsis in intensive care ([infection control and healthcare-associated infection](../../12-organ-system-specialties/08-infectious-diseases/infection-control-and-healthcare-associated-infection.md)). The threshold for cultures and empirical antibiotics is therefore low, and maternal screening or intrapartum antibiotics act on the chain of transmission.

6. **Oxygen deprivation around birth injures the brain in two phases, and the second can be interrupted.** Hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy follows a primary energy failure during the insult and a secondary one hours later as mitochondria fail and excitotoxic glutamate, free radicals and inflammation spread the damage ([cell injury, adaptation and death](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/cell-injury-adaptation-and-death.md)). Cooling the infant to about 33 to 34 °C for 72 hours within six hours of birth slows the secondary cascade and reduces death and disability, one of the few neuroprotective treatments with trial evidence.

7. **Prematurity is a multi-organ disease of unfinished development.** About 1 in 10 births worldwide is preterm (before 37 weeks, 2020). Beyond the lungs, the germinal matrix bleeds (intraventricular haemorrhage), an immature gut becomes ischaemic and inflamed (necrotising enterocolitis), the retina's vessels grow abnormally when oxygen is given (retinopathy of prematurity, driven by vascular endothelial growth factor), and the ductus often stays open. Survival at 24 weeks is now around half in well-resourced units but a large fraction of survivors have disability, which makes the limit of viability an ethical as much as a technical question ([medical ethics](../../15-medicine-in-society/02-ethics-law-and-the-profession/medical-ethics.md)).

8. **Who else is involved.** Neonatology begins before birth: obstetrics decides timing and mode of delivery and gives antenatal steroids ([pregnancy and its complications](../02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/pregnancy-and-its-complications.md)). Paediatric cardiology and paediatric surgery act on duct-dependent lesions and malformations, ophthalmology screens for retinopathy, clinical genetics interprets dysmorphism and the newborn bloodspot screen, and the family's primary care team inherits a graduate of intensive care with feeding, growth and developmental follow-up.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Transitional circulation | The hours-to-days switch from placental to pulmonary gas exchange as the foramen ovale and ductus close |
| Apgar score | A 0–10 bedside score (colour, heart rate, reflexes, tone, breathing) at 1 and 5 minutes describing the state of the transition |
| Respiratory distress syndrome | Surfactant deficiency of prematurity: alveolar collapse, grunting, ground-glass radiograph |
| Persistent pulmonary hypertension | Failure of pulmonary vascular resistance to fall, with right-to-left shunting and refractory cyanosis |
| Duct-dependent lesion | A heart defect whose systemic or pulmonary flow runs through the ductus arteriosus, presenting when it closes |
| Kernicterus | Bilirubin deposition in the basal ganglia; the irreversible end-point of untreated unconjugated jaundice |
| Early-onset sepsis | Infection in the first 72 hours from organisms acquired at birth, mainly group B streptococcus and *E. coli* |
| Therapeutic hypothermia | Cooling after hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy to blunt secondary energy failure |
| Newborn bloodspot screening | Testing days after birth for treatable conditions such as congenital hypothyroidism and phenylketonuria |
| Limit of viability | The gestation (around 22–24 weeks) below which survival outside the womb is rare |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A newborn is a small adult."** Its circulation, haemoglobin, drug metabolism, immune defences and heat balance are all different in kind, not just scale; the same oxygen that saves an adult lung blinds a preterm retina.
- **"Jaundice in a newborn is always benign."** Most is physiological, but jaundice in the first day, a rapidly rising level or conjugated bilirubin each signal a specific and treatable disease.
- **"Cyanosis means a lung problem, so give oxygen."** Cyanosis that does not improve with oxygen points to shunting (persistent pulmonary hypertension or cyanotic heart disease), and in a duct-dependent lesion high oxygen can hasten duct closure.
