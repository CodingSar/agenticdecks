## Essence

Adolescence is the second great developmental transition, from the dependent child to the autonomous adult, driven by puberty in the body and by a brain whose reward and emotional systems mature years before its executive control. Its diseases are therefore less about organs than about behaviour and environment: injury, substance use, sexual health, the first episodes of most mental illness, and the chronic diseases of childhood meeting a patient who now decides for themselves. Safeguarding, the recognition and prevention of harm done to children by the adults responsible for them, belongs here and throughout paediatrics because abuse and neglect are among the strongest determinants of lifelong health and because the clinician is often the only outsider who sees the child. The unifying idea is **autonomy arriving unevenly**: the capacity to consent, to take risks and to be harmed by others each follow their own timetable.

## Core tenets

1. **Puberty is the reawakening of a hormonal axis switched off in infancy.** Hypothalamic kisspeptin neurons reactivate pulsatile gonadotrophin-releasing hormone secretion, pituitary gonadotrophins drive the gonads, and sex steroids produce secondary sexual characteristics, the growth spurt and epiphyseal fusion ([reproductive endocrinology](../../06-physiology/07-endocrine-system/reproductive-endocrinology.md); [growth, puberty and aging](../../04-genetics-evolution-and-development/03-development/growth-puberty-and-aging.md)). Onset outside roughly 8–13 years in girls and 9–14 in boys defines precocious or delayed puberty; the former is usually central and idiopathic in girls but demands a search for a lesion in boys, the latter is usually constitutional but may be hypogonadism, chronic illness or energy deficit. Timing is set by genes, nutrition and body fat (leptin signals sufficiency), which is why the age at menarche fell as nutrition improved.

2. **The adolescent brain is built for exploration before it is built for restraint.** Subcortical reward and limbic circuits mature around puberty while prefrontal control continues to mature into the mid-twenties ([higher functions](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/higher-functions-memory-language-and-consciousness.md)), producing heightened sensation-seeking and peer sensitivity. This is adaptive for a species that must leave home and find mates ([evolutionary medicine](../../04-genetics-evolution-and-development/02-evolution/evolutionary-medicine.md)), and it is why injury, violence and suicide are the leading causes of death in this age group worldwide, and why substance use begun in adolescence is more likely to become dependence ([addiction and substance use](../03-psychiatry/addiction-and-substance-use.md)).

3. **Most mental illness begins here.** Roughly half of lifetime mental disorders start by the mid-teens and three-quarters by the mid-twenties: depression and anxiety, eating disorders, self-harm, and the prodrome of psychosis ([mood and anxiety disorders](../03-psychiatry/mood-and-anxiety-disorders.md); [psychosis](../03-psychiatry/psychosis.md)). The adolescent presents these as somatic complaints, school refusal or behaviour, and the consultation must ask directly about mood, self-harm and safety. Suicide is a leading cause of death at 15–29, and means restriction and early treatment of depression are the interventions with evidence.

4. **The consultation shifts from the parent to the young person, and confidentiality is the price of honesty.** A structured psychosocial history (home, education, activities, drugs, sexuality, suicide and safety) is taken partly alone. A minor who understands the nature and consequences of a decision can consent to treatment without a parent in many jurisdictions (in English law, Gillick competence), and confidentiality is kept unless there is a risk of serious harm ([consent, capacity and confidentiality](../../15-medicine-in-society/02-ethics-law-and-the-profession/consent-capacity-and-confidentiality.md)). Capacity is decision-specific, so a 15-year-old may consent to contraception yet have decisions about refusing life-saving treatment overridden.

5. **Chronic disease meets a patient who may refuse it.** Type 1 diabetes, asthma, epilepsy, cystic fibrosis and transplant immunosuppression all deteriorate in adolescence because adherence falls, insulin resistance of puberty raises requirements, and the move from paediatric to adult services loses patients ([chronic disease and self-management](../05-primary-care-and-integration/chronic-disease-and-self-management.md)). Control of glycaemia in adolescents with type 1 diabetes is typically the worst of any age group. Planned transition, with the young person increasingly seen alone and taught to manage their own condition, is the structural remedy.

6. **Sexual and reproductive health is adolescent medicine.** Sexually transmitted infections peak in the late teens and early twenties because of partner change and immature cervical epithelium; the human papillomavirus vaccine given before sexual debut prevents most cervical cancer ([gynaecological disease](../02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/gynaecological-disease.md)). Adolescent pregnancy carries higher obstetric and social risk; long-acting reversible contraception is the most effective because it removes adherence from the equation ([fertility, contraception and menopause](../02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/fertility-contraception-and-menopause.md)).

7. **Safeguarding: maltreatment leaves patterns that physiology and physics make recognisable.** A fracture in a child who cannot yet walk, metaphyseal or posterior rib fractures, bruises on soft non-bony areas or in an immobile infant, patterned burns, and a history that does not fit the injury or changes with retelling are the signals ([mechanics and materials](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/mechanics-and-materials.md)). Abusive head trauma in infants produces subdural haemorrhage and retinal haemorrhages from shearing. Neglect shows as faltering growth, missed immunisations and appointments, and developmental delay that recovers in care. Emotional abuse and sexual abuse leave fewer physical marks and more behavioural ones. The duty is to recognise, document, share information and refer, not to prove.

8. **Adverse childhood experiences are a dose-dependent exposure.** Abuse, neglect and household dysfunction raise the adult risk of depression, substance dependence, obesity, cardiovascular disease and early death in proportion to the number of exposures, through chronic stress physiology, health behaviour and social trajectory ([exercise and stress physiology](../../06-physiology/01-integrative-principles/exercise-and-stress-physiology.md); [social determinants and health inequity](../../15-medicine-in-society/03-populations-and-the-future/social-determinants-and-health-inequity.md)). Safeguarding is therefore preventive medicine with one of the longest horizons.

9. **Who else is involved.** Child and adolescent psychiatry ([psychiatry](../03-psychiatry/README.md)), sexual health and gynaecology, paediatric endocrinology for pubertal disorders, emergency medicine and trauma surgery for injury ([trauma and haemorrhage control](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/trauma-and-haemorrhage-control.md)), radiology and ophthalmology for the skeletal survey and retinal examination in suspected abuse, forensic medicine, social services, education and the police in safeguarding, and the general practitioner who may know the whole family ([family medicine and the generalist](../05-primary-care-and-integration/family-medicine-and-the-generalist.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Kisspeptin | The hypothalamic peptide whose signalling restarts pulsatile gonadotrophin release at puberty |
| Tanner stages | The five-stage scale of breast, genital and pubic-hair development used to stage puberty |
| Constitutional delay | A familial late but otherwise normal puberty, the commonest cause of delayed puberty in boys |
| Gillick competence | The legal test (England and Wales) that a minor who understands a decision may consent to it |
| HEADSS | A structured adolescent psychosocial history: Home, Education, Activities, Drugs, Sexuality, Suicide and Safety |
| Transition | Planned transfer of a young person with chronic disease to adult care with growing self-management |
| Non-accidental injury | Injury inflicted by a carer, suspected when pattern, age and history do not fit |
| Skeletal survey | A series of radiographs of the whole skeleton to find occult fractures in suspected abuse |
| Adverse childhood experiences | Categories of abuse, neglect and household adversity whose count predicts adult disease |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Adolescent risk-taking is a failure of knowledge."** Adolescents know the risks; their valuation of reward and peer approval is different, so education alone changes little while changing the environment (means restriction, graduated licensing) changes much.
- **"A child with a plausible-sounding injury story is safe."** The question is whether the mechanism described can produce the injury found in a child of that developmental stage, and whether the story stays constant.
- **"Confidentiality for minors undermines parents."** Without it adolescents do not disclose, and the disclosure is what allows the harm to be addressed.
