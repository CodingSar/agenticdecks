## Essence

The body is a structure that must stand, move, pump and stretch for decades without failing, and it is built from materials with peculiar properties: soft, wet, fibrous, self-repairing and alive. Newton's laws govern how forces are transmitted through bones and joints; the mechanics of materials governs how tissues deform and break under those forces. Two ideas carry most of the weight. Stress (force per area) and strain (fractional deformation) are related by stiffness, and tissue stiffness is tuned by composition, collagen for tensile strength, elastin for recoil, mineral for compression. And living tissue remodels in response to load, so bone, tendon, muscle and heart wall are shaped by the forces they bear, strengthening where loaded and wasting where not.

## Core tenets

1. **Forces balance, and levers decide how much muscle it takes.** A body part at rest has zero net force and zero net torque. Most joints are third-class levers with the muscle inserting close to the pivot, so muscle force greatly exceeds the load: holding 10 kg in the hand with the elbow bent demands roughly 70–80 kg of force in the biceps, and lifting a load with a bent back can put several times body weight across the lumbar discs. Joint reaction forces are therefore large, which is why cartilage wears and prostheses loosen.

2. **Stress and strain, not force and length, describe materials.** Stress `σ = F/A` and strain `ε = ΔL/L₀` are independent of the specimen's size; their ratio in the elastic region is the Young's modulus. Bone is about 15–20 GPa, tendon ~1 GPa, cartilage ~1 MPa, soft tissue tens of kilopascals, a million-fold range. A material fails when stress exceeds its strength, and a thinner bone (osteoporosis) or a larger lever arm (a fall onto an outstretched arm) reaches that stress at a smaller load.

3. **Biological tissues are composites with nonlinear, time-dependent behaviour.** Collagen fibres are crimped at rest, so a tendon or artery is soft at low strain and stiffens sharply as the fibres straighten (a J-shaped curve). This protects against overstretch while allowing easy small movements. Tissues are also viscoelastic: they creep under sustained load, relax under sustained stretch and show hysteresis, so a ligament loaded slowly is more compliant than one loaded fast, and intervertebral discs lose height over a day of standing.

4. **Collagen resists tension, elastin stores recoil, mineral resists compression.** Collagen's triple helix gives tensile strength close to steel per unit weight but no resistance to bending or compression. Elastin stretches to twice its length and recovers, giving arteries and lung their recoil. Hydroxyapatite crystals deposited on collagen let bone bear compression and bending; bone is a composite like reinforced concrete, with collagen as the rebar. Diseases of collagen (scurvy), elastin (Marfan syndrome) and mineral (osteomalacia) each fail mechanically in the predicted way.

5. **Cross-sectional shape, not just mass, sets bending strength.** Resistance to bending rises with the fourth power of the radius for a solid beam and is concentrated in the outer fibres, so a hollow tube is nearly as stiff as a solid rod of the same diameter at a fraction of the weight. Long bones are hollow tubes with cortical bone where the stress is; the trabecular struts inside the ends align with principal stress lines (Wolff's law).

6. **Tissue remodels to the load it carries.** Bone cells sense strain and add or remove bone to keep peak strain within a narrow window; astronauts and bed-bound patients lose ~1–2% of bone per month, athletes gain it. Muscle fibres add sarcomeres and myofibrils when loaded and atrophy within days of disuse. The heart wall thickens under pressure load and lengthens under volume load. Adaptation is the rule; what fails is adaptation that outruns supply, or load that exceeds the rate of repair.

7. **Muscle is a force generator whose output depends on length and velocity.** Force rises with the overlap of actin and myosin to an optimal length and falls either side (the length–tension curve); it falls as shortening velocity rises and exceeds isometric force when the muscle is stretched while active (eccentric contraction, the common cause of muscle injury). The heart's Frank–Starling law is the length–tension curve of cardiac muscle: more filling stretches fibres toward their optimum and increases force.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Stress | Force per unit area, Pa (N/m²) |
| Strain | Fractional change in length, dimensionless |
| Young's modulus | Stress per unit strain in the elastic range; stiffness of a material |
| Strength | The stress at which a material fails |
| Viscoelasticity | Deformation that depends on rate and duration of loading; creep, relaxation, hysteresis |
| Torque (moment) | Force × perpendicular distance from the pivot |
| Second moment of area | Geometric measure of a beam's resistance to bending; grows with the fourth power of radius |
| Wolff's law | Bone adapts its structure to the loads it experiences |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Stronger bones are denser bones."** Density matters, but geometry (a wider tube) and collagen quality (toughness) contribute as much; two bones of equal density can differ several-fold in fracture resistance.
- **"Muscles pull harder when they are longer or shorter."** Force peaks at an optimal sarcomere length; an overstretched or over-shortened muscle is weak, and a dilated heart is a muscle past its optimum.
