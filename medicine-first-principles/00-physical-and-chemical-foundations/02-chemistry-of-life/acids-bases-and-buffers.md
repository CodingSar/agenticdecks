## Essence

The hydrogen ion concentration of blood is held at about 40 nanomoles per litre (pH 7.40), a million times lower than that of sodium, within a range of roughly ±25% compatible with life; outside pH 6.8–7.8 enzymes misfold, membranes fail and the heart stops. The body keeps this number steady while producing some 15,000 mmol of carbon dioxide (a volatile acid) and 50–100 mmol of fixed acid every day. It does so with buffers, solutions of a weak acid and its conjugate base that absorb added acid or base with little change in pH, and with two organs that remove acid: the lung within minutes and the kidney within days. One equation, Henderson–Hasselbalch applied to the bicarbonate system, connects what the lung controls (carbon dioxide) to what the kidney controls (bicarbonate) and gives every acid–base disorder its arithmetic.

## Core tenets

1. **pH is a logarithm, so small changes are large.** `pH = −log10[H⁺]`. Plasma at pH 7.40 has [H⁺] = 40 nmol/L; pH 7.10 is 80 nmol/L, double; pH 7.70 is 20 nmol/L, half. A patient "a little acidotic" at pH 7.25 has 40% more hydrogen ions than normal. Protons bind histidine, amine and carboxylate groups on proteins, so a small change in pH changes the charge and shape of every enzyme.

2. **A weak acid and its conjugate base resist pH change near their pKa: Henderson–Hasselbalch.** For `HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻`, `pH = pKa + log10([A⁻]/[HA])`. When the two forms are equal, pH equals pKa and the buffer absorbs acid or base with the smallest change in pH. Buffering is strongest within one pH unit of the pKa; beyond that one form runs out. Blood's effective buffers are bicarbonate (pKa 6.1, but open to the lung), haemoglobin and plasma proteins (histidine, pKa ~6–7) and phosphate (pKa 6.8).

3. **The bicarbonate system is powerful because it is open.** Carbon dioxide, carbonic acid and bicarbonate interconvert: `CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻`, with carbonic anhydrase making the first step fast. Add acid, and the bicarbonate it consumes becomes carbon dioxide that the lung exhales; the product is removed, so the reaction keeps going. Its pKa of 6.1 would make it a poor closed buffer at pH 7.4, but ventilation sets [CO₂] independently. The working form is `pH = 6.1 + log10([HCO₃⁻] / (0.03 × PCO₂))` with PCO₂ in mmHg: at 24 mmol/L and 40 mmHg the ratio is 20:1 and pH is 7.40.

4. **The lung sets the numerator's partner, the kidney sets the numerator; each compensates for the other.** Ventilation controls PCO₂ within minutes; the kidney reabsorbs filtered bicarbonate and excretes fixed acid (as ammonium and titratable phosphate) over hours to days. A metabolic acidosis (low bicarbonate) triggers hyperventilation that lowers PCO₂ and restores the ratio; a respiratory acidosis (high PCO₂) is answered by renal bicarbonate retention. Compensation moves pH toward normal but never overshoots; reading a blood gas means finding the primary change and checking whether the other term has moved as expected.

5. **Buffering is immediate but only borrows; excretion repays.** Buffers neutralise added acid within seconds, but every proton they absorb consumes a base molecule, and the body's bicarbonate (~350 mmol in extracellular fluid, with more inside cells and in bone) is finite. Only excretion of acid, by lung or kidney, or metabolism of the organic anion back to bicarbonate, restores the buffer. Bone is the long-term buffer, which is why chronic acidosis (kidney disease) dissolves it.

6. **Charge state follows pKa, and charge decides whether a molecule crosses a membrane.** A weak acid is mostly uncharged below its pKa and charged above it; a weak base is the reverse. Uncharged forms cross lipid membranes, charged forms are trapped. Aspirin (pKa 3.5) is uncharged in the stomach and absorbed there; once in plasma at pH 7.4 it is charged and stays. Alkalinising the urine traps the ionised form and speeds its excretion.

7. **Acid–base state alters potassium, calcium and oxygen delivery through the same chemistry.** Hydrogen ions entering cells displace potassium outward, so acidosis raises plasma potassium. Albumin binds more calcium when it carries fewer protons, so alkalosis lowers ionised calcium and causes tetany. Protons binding haemoglobin lower its oxygen affinity (the Bohr effect), so acidic, working tissue unloads more oxygen, and alkalosis holds it tighter.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| pH | −log10 of hydrogen ion concentration; plasma 7.35–7.45 |
| pKa | The pH at which an acid is half dissociated; where it buffers best |
| Buffer | A weak acid–conjugate base pair that resists pH change |
| Henderson–Hasselbalch equation | `pH = pKa + log([base]/[acid])` |
| Volatile acid | Carbon dioxide, removed by the lung (~15,000 mmol/day) |
| Fixed acid | Sulfuric, phosphoric and organic acids from metabolism, removed by the kidney (~50–100 mmol/day) |
| Compensation | The other organ's adjustment that moves pH back toward normal |
| Ion trapping | Accumulation of a charged form on the side of a membrane where its pKa makes it ionised |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A normal pH means a normal acid–base state."** Two opposing disorders, or full compensation, give a normal pH; PCO₂ and bicarbonate must be read with it.
- **"Bicarbonate is a weak buffer because its pKa is 6.1."** As a closed system it would be; because the lung removes carbon dioxide it is the dominant extracellular buffer.
