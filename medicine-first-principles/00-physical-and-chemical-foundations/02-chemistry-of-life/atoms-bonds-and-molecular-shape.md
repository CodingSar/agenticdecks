## Essence

Living matter is made of about a dozen elements, dominated by carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen, held together by a small repertoire of bonds whose strengths span three orders of magnitude. Strong covalent bonds (hundreds of kJ/mol) fix the skeleton of a molecule and survive body temperature indefinitely; weak bonds (a few kJ/mol each, hydrogen bonds, ionic attractions, van der Waals forces and the hydrophobic effect) are made and broken constantly at 37 °C, and it is their *sum* across a surface that decides whether two molecules stick. Shape follows from bonds: the angles around each atom and the rotation allowed about each single bond fix the three-dimensional form, and in biology shape is function, because molecules recognise each other by fitting together. Medicine lives at the weak-bond scale: every hormone binding its receptor, every antibody, every drug, is weak bonds summed over a complementary surface.

## Core tenets

1. **Six elements make 99% of the body's mass, and each is chosen by its valence.** Oxygen (~65%, mostly as water), carbon (~18%), hydrogen (~10%), nitrogen (~3%), calcium (~1.5%) and phosphorus (~1%). Carbon forms four stable bonds and chains with itself; nitrogen forms three and carries a lone pair that accepts protons; oxygen forms two and is strongly electronegative; hydrogen caps the rest. The remainder is sodium, potassium, chloride, magnesium, sulfur and trace metals, and the lack of any one is a nameable disease.

2. **Covalent bonds are the stable skeleton; their energy is far above thermal energy.** A carbon–carbon bond holds ~350 kJ/mol and a carbon–hydrogen bond ~410 kJ/mol, against thermal energy `RT` of ~2.6 kJ/mol at 37 °C. Spontaneous breakage is negligible, so molecules keep their identity until an enzyme, a radical or ionising radiation supplies the activation energy. This is why metabolism needs catalysts, and why radiation and radicals, which break strong bonds unasked, are dangerous.

3. **Electronegativity differences make bonds polar, and polarity governs solubility.** Oxygen and nitrogen pull electrons toward themselves, leaving partial charges: the O–H and N–H bonds are polar, the C–H bond is not. A molecule rich in polar groups dissolves in water; one made of C–H bonds does not. Every drug's absorption, distribution and excretion, and every membrane's function as a barrier, follows from where a molecule sits on this polar–nonpolar axis.

4. **Weak bonds are individually trivial and collectively decisive.** A hydrogen bond contributes ~4–20 kJ/mol, an ionic pair in water ~5–10 kJ/mol, a van der Waals contact ~1–4 kJ/mol. Each is comparable to `RT`, so each forms and breaks thousands of times a second. But a drug fitting a receptor pocket makes ten or twenty at once, a Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) double helix makes two or three per base pair over millions of pairs, and a protein's fold is held by hundreds. The strength of a biological interaction is the sum of many weak contacts, and specificity comes from needing them all to line up.

5. **The hydrophobic effect is entropy, not attraction.** Nonpolar groups cluster in water not because they attract each other but because water molecules around them are forced into ordered cages; burying the nonpolar surface frees that water and raises entropy. This drives membrane formation, protein folding (hydrophobic residues inside, polar outside) and the binding of lipid-soluble drugs and hormones to their carriers. Warming often strengthens it, which is unusual for a bond.

6. **Shape is set by bond angles and limited rotation, and shape is function.** Tetrahedral carbon gives ~109.5° angles; a double bond is planar and cannot rotate; single bonds rotate but with preferred staggered positions. These rules fix a molecule's three-dimensional form and its flexibility. A cis double bond kinks a fatty acid, lowering the melting point of a membrane; a trans one does not, which is why trans fats behave like saturated ones. Rings lock conformations, which is why steroids are rigid and bind their receptors precisely.

7. **Chirality means mirror-image molecules are different drugs.** A carbon with four different substituents exists in two non-superimposable forms (enantiomers). Enzymes and receptors are themselves chiral, so they distinguish the two: natural amino acids are all L and sugars D; one enantiomer of a drug may be active and the other inert or harmful. About half of marketed drugs are chiral.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Covalent bond | Shared electron pair; 150–1,000 kJ/mol; stable at body temperature |
| Electronegativity | An atom's pull on shared electrons; O > N > C ≈ H |
| Polar bond | Unequal sharing giving partial charges; O–H and N–H are polar |
| Hydrogen bond | Attraction between an H on O or N and a lone pair on another O or N; ~4–20 kJ/mol |
| Ionic interaction | Attraction between full charges; weakened ~80-fold by water's dielectric |
| Van der Waals force | Short-range attraction from transient dipoles; ~1–4 kJ/mol per contact |
| Hydrophobic effect | Clustering of nonpolar groups driven by water's entropy |
| Conformation | A shape reachable by rotation about single bonds without breaking any |
| Chirality | Handedness; a tetrahedral carbon with four different groups has two mirror forms |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Hydrogen bonds are weak, so they do not matter."** Individually yes; collectively they hold the DNA helix together and give water the properties on which all biology depends.
- **"A drug's mirror image is the same drug."** Enantiomers can differ completely in potency, metabolism and toxicity because the body's receptors and enzymes are chiral.
