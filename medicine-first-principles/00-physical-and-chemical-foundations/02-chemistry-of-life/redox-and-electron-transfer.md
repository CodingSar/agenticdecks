## Essence

The body runs on a slow, controlled combustion. Food is rich in electrons held loosely (in carbon–hydrogen bonds); oxygen wants electrons badly; and the free energy released when electrons fall from food to oxygen, about 2,870 kJ per mole of glucose, is the energy that powers everything else. Oxidation is loss of electrons, reduction is gain, and the two always occur together, so metabolism is a chain of electron hand-offs through carriers whose electron affinity rises step by step until oxygen takes them and becomes water. Each step releases a usable packet of energy, and in the mitochondrion those packets pump protons across a membrane to make Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP). The same chemistry has a dark side: oxygen that accepts electrons one at a time becomes a radical, and radicals attack bonds indiscriminately. Oxygen is both the body's greatest resource and its standing hazard.

## Core tenets

1. **Oxidation and reduction are electron transfer, and they always come in pairs.** When a molecule loses electrons (is oxidised), another gains them (is reduced); in biology, loss of electrons usually appears as loss of hydrogen atoms or gain of oxygen. Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) becomes carbon dioxide while oxygen becomes water; carbon is oxidised, oxygen reduced. Every dehydrogenase, oxidase, reductase and peroxidase in the body catalyses one such pair.

2. **Electron affinity is measured as reduction potential, and electrons flow from low to high.** The standard reduction potential `E°'` says how strongly a couple pulls electrons: NADH/NAD⁺ sits at −0.32 V, oxygen/water at +0.82 V. The free energy released when `n` electrons flow across a potential difference `ΔE` is `ΔG = −nFΔE` (F = 96.5 kJ per volt per mole of electrons). From NADH to oxygen that is about −220 kJ per two electrons, enough for roughly 2.5 ATP. Reduction potentials order the respiratory chain the way height orders a waterfall.

3. **Metabolism moves electrons through carriers so the energy is released in steps.** Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide (NAD⁺/NADH), its phosphate (NADP⁺/NADPH) and Flavin Adenine Dinucleotide (FAD/FADH₂) collect electrons from food; the mitochondrial chain of flavins, iron–sulfur clusters, quinone and cytochromes hands them down in small drops; cytochrome oxidase gives four at once to oxygen. Stepping the energy down lets each complex do work with its share instead of releasing it all as heat.

4. **The proton gradient is the link between electron flow and ATP: chemiosmosis.** The complexes use the energy of electron transfer to pump protons out of the mitochondrial matrix, building a gradient of ~0.2 V and ~1 pH unit across the inner membrane. Protons returning through ATP synthase turn a rotor that makes ATP (Mitchell, 1961). This is why the inner membrane must be intact, why uncouplers that let protons leak (dinitrophenol, and the body's own uncoupling protein in brown fat) make heat instead of ATP, and why cyanide, which blocks the final electron transfer to oxygen, stops ATP synthesis within minutes even in a fully oxygenated patient.

5. **Iron and copper carry electrons because they switch oxidation state easily.** Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ and Cu⁺/Cu²⁺ sit in haem groups and iron–sulfur clusters throughout the chain, and in haemoglobin, where iron must stay Fe²⁺ to bind oxygen. Oxidising it to Fe³⁺ (methaemoglobin, caused by nitrites and some drugs) abolishes oxygen carriage; the red cell spends NADH to keep it reduced. Free iron also catalyses radical formation, which is why the body keeps every atom bound to transferrin or ferritin.

6. **Partial reduction of oxygen makes radicals, and radicals damage bonds without discrimination.** Oxygen prefers to take electrons one at a time, yielding superoxide (O₂•⁻), then hydrogen peroxide, then the hydroxyl radical (•OH), which abstracts hydrogen from any nearby lipid, protein or Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA). A few percent of mitochondrial electrons leak this way; phagocytes make superoxide deliberately to kill bacteria; ionising radiation makes it by splitting water. Lipid peroxidation is a chain reaction, one radical spawning the next, which is how a small initiating event destroys a membrane.

7. **Antioxidant defences are themselves redox couples, and they cost reducing power.** Superoxide dismutase, catalase and glutathione peroxidase convert radicals to water; the glutathione that does so is regenerated by NADPH from the pentose phosphate pathway. Vitamins C and E are sacrificial reducers. A red cell lacking glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase cannot make enough NADPH, so oxidant drugs or fava beans oxidise its haemoglobin and it haemolyses. Paracetamol overdose exhausts hepatic glutathione, and the antidote rebuilds it.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Oxidation / reduction | Loss / gain of electrons (in biology, usually loss / gain of hydrogen) |
| Reduction potential (E°') | A couple's pull on electrons, in volts; higher means stronger oxidant |
| `ΔG = −nFΔE` | Free energy from electrons falling across a potential difference |
| NAD⁺/NADH, NADP⁺/NADPH, FAD/FADH₂ | Soluble electron carriers; NADH feeds the chain, NADPH feeds biosynthesis and antioxidant defence |
| Electron transport chain | Mitochondrial complexes of rising reduction potential ending at oxygen |
| Chemiosmosis | Electron flow pumps protons; proton return makes ATP |
| Uncoupling | Protons bypass ATP synthase; energy becomes heat |
| Reactive oxygen species | Superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radical: partially reduced oxygen |
| Glutathione | The cell's main reducing buffer, regenerated by NADPH |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Oxygen is burned to release energy."** Oxygen is reduced; the energy comes from the carbon and hydrogen of food being oxidised, and oxygen is only the final electron acceptor.
- **"Antioxidant supplements prevent disease by mopping up radicals."** Large trials of vitamin E and beta-carotene showed no benefit and some harm; radicals are also signals and weapons, and the body's defences are enzymatic, not dietary.
