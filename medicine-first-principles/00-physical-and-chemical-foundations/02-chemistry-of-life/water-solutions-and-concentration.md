## Essence

The body is about 60% water by mass, and almost every reaction in it happens in solution. Water is not a passive filler: its polarity dissolves ions and polar molecules, its hydrogen-bonded network gives it a high heat capacity and heat of vaporisation that stabilise body temperature, and its exclusion of nonpolar molecules builds membranes. Because reactions depend on concentrations rather than amounts, medicine measures nearly everything as an amount per litre, and the right unit (molar, osmolar or equivalent) depends on whether the question is chemical, osmotic or electrical. Half of all laboratory medicine is reading concentrations in body water; understanding what a concentration is, and what it is not, is the first skill.

## Core tenets

1. **Water dissolves what it can hydrogen-bond to or surround with charge, and excludes what it cannot.** Each water molecule makes up to four hydrogen bonds. Ions are surrounded by oriented water (hydration shells) that screen their charges, reducing ionic attraction about 80-fold (water's dielectric constant). Polar molecules with –OH, –NH₂ and –C=O groups join the network; hydrocarbons cannot and are squeezed out. Solubility therefore predicts where a molecule goes in the body and whether it can cross a membrane.

2. **Water's hydrogen bonds buffer temperature.** Its heat capacity (~4.2 kJ/kg per °C) means the body's 40 L of water absorb large heat loads with small temperature change, and its heat of vaporisation (~2.4 MJ/kg) makes sweating an efficient cooler. Ice is less dense than water, which is irrelevant to the body but is why frozen tissue is destroyed: expanding crystals rupture cells.

3. **Concentration, not amount, drives reactions and signals.** A reaction rate and a receptor's occupancy depend on moles per litre. Serum sodium of 140 mmol/L reports the ratio of sodium to water, not the body's sodium content; a patient can have a low sodium with excess total sodium (oedema with water excess) or a normal sodium while profoundly salt-depleted. Reading a concentration always requires asking what happened to the denominator.

4. **The right unit depends on the question.** *Molarity* (mol/L) counts molecules and is right for chemistry and drugs. *Osmolarity* counts particles regardless of charge or size and is right for water movement: 1 mmol of sodium chloride gives ~2 mOsm because it dissociates, 1 mmol of glucose gives 1 mOsm. *Equivalents* count charge and are right for electroneutrality: 1 mmol of calcium is 2 mEq. Mass units (mg/dL, g/L) are still used in many countries, so a glucose of 90 mg/dL and 5 mmol/L are the same, and a reader must convert by molar mass (180 g/mol for glucose).

5. **Plasma osmolality can be estimated from its main solutes, and the gap is diagnostic.** Plasma osmolality is ~285–295 mOsm/kg and is approximated by `2 × [Na⁺] + [glucose] + [urea]` in mmol/L. If measured osmolality exceeds this by more than ~10 mOsm/kg, an unmeasured solute is present: ethanol, methanol, ethylene glycol or mannitol. The body regulates this number to within ~1–2% through thirst and antidiuretic hormone, because cell volume depends on it.

6. **Solutions must be electroneutral, so an anion gap means an unmeasured anion.** Total cations equal total anions in every body fluid. Plasma routinely measures sodium, chloride and bicarbonate; the difference, `[Na⁺] − ([Cl⁻] + [HCO₃⁻])`, is normally ~8–12 mmol/L and is filled by albumin, phosphate, sulfate and organic acids. A larger gap means an acid has added its anion (lactate, ketoacids, salicylate, toxic alcohol metabolites) while consuming bicarbonate. Electroneutrality turns a chemistry law into a diagnostic tool.

7. **Water is distributed by osmosis across compartments, and solutes decide which compartment a fluid fills.** Roughly two-thirds of body water is intracellular and one-third extracellular, of which a quarter is plasma. Infused water (as 5% glucose, the glucose being metabolised) distributes across all compartments; infused isotonic saline stays extracellular because sodium is pumped out of cells; infused colloid stays mostly intravascular while the capillary wall retains it. The same litre expands plasma by ~80 mL, ~250 mL or ~800 mL depending on what is dissolved in it. Fluid therapy is applied solution chemistry.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Polarity | Unequal charge distribution; water is strongly polar |
| Dielectric constant | How much a medium weakens electrostatic attraction; water ~80 |
| Hydration shell | Oriented water molecules surrounding an ion or polar group |
| Molarity | Moles of solute per litre of solution |
| Osmolality | Osmoles (particles) per kilogram of water; plasma ~290 |
| Equivalent | Moles of charge; relevant to electroneutrality |
| Osmolar gap | Measured minus calculated osmolality; detects unmeasured solutes |
| Anion gap | Sodium minus (chloride + bicarbonate); detects unmeasured anions |
| Tonicity | Osmolality due to solutes that cannot cross the cell membrane |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Low serum sodium means the patient needs sodium."** It usually means excess water relative to sodium; giving sodium to a water-overloaded patient worsens the oedema.
- **"Saline is just water with salt."** Isotonic saline stays outside cells and expands the extracellular space; pure water would lyse red cells and is never infused.
- **"mmol and mEq are the same."** They coincide only for monovalent ions; for calcium and magnesium the equivalents are double the millimoles.
