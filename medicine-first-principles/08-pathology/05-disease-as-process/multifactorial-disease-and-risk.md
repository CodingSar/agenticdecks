## Essence

Most common diseases have no single cause. Coronary disease, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, asthma, schizophrenia, most cancers and most birth defects arise from many genetic variants, each of small effect, interacting with many exposures over a lifetime. For such diseases the question "what caused it?" has no answer for the individual, only for the population, and the useful concept is **risk**: the probability of disease given a set of factors. The idea that makes this coherent is that liability to a multifactorial disease is a continuous, roughly normally distributed quantity, and disease appears in those who cross a threshold.

## Core tenets

1. **Liability is continuous; disease is a threshold on it.** Many genes and exposures each add or subtract a little, so their sum is approximately normally distributed in the population (the central limit theorem at work). Disease occurs when liability exceeds a threshold, which explains the patterns of multifactorial inheritance: recurrence risk in relatives falls off quickly with genetic distance (about 3–5% in first-degree relatives of a child with cleft lip or neural tube defect, against 50% for a dominant disorder), rises if the index case is severely affected or of the less commonly affected sex, and rises with each affected relative.

2. **Risk factors are statistical associations that become causes only with evidence of mechanism.** A risk factor raises the probability of disease; it may be a cause (Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol), a marker of a cause (low High-Density Lipoprotein (HDL) marks poor efflux and insulin resistance), or a confounded bystander (coffee and lung cancer, through smoking). Bradford Hill's considerations and the methods of [causal inference](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/02-inference-and-causation/causal-inference.md) separate them; Mendelian randomisation, using inherited variants as unconfounded natural experiments, has confirmed LDL and blood pressure as causes and demoted HDL and C-reactive protein to markers. Only causal factors are worth modifying.

3. **Risks multiply, so the same factor matters more in a person who already has others.** Relative risks from independent factors combine roughly multiplicatively on the probability scale. A systolic pressure of 160 mmHg roughly doubles coronary risk whether or not the patient smokes, but the absolute risk it adds is far greater in a smoker with diabetes than in a young non-smoker, because it doubles a larger number. This is why risk scores (Framingham, QRISK, SCORE2) estimate absolute risk from the combination, and why treatment decisions are made on absolute risk: the benefit of lowering any one factor is proportional to the risk the patient started with.

4. **Most cases come from the middle of the distribution, not the tail: the prevention paradox.** Because many people have moderate risk and few have very high risk, more events arise from the moderate group. Rose showed that shifting the whole population's blood pressure or cholesterol down slightly prevents more disease than treating only those above a threshold, though it offers each individual little. The two strategies complement each other: the high-risk approach (identify and treat the few, large individual benefit) and the population approach (change diet, salt, tobacco and air for everyone, large aggregate benefit).

5. **Gene–environment interaction is the rule, and heritability is not fixed.** A variant's effect depends on exposure: phenylketonuria is harmless without phenylalanine, aldehyde dehydrogenase deficiency without alcohol, obesity-risk alleles have twice the effect in sedentary as in active people. Heritability is the proportion of variance in liability explained by genes in one population at one time; make the environment uniform and genes explain more of what remains. A heritable disease is therefore not an unpreventable one, and the rise of obesity and diabetes since 1975 in unchanged genomes proves the point.

6. **Polygenic scores quantify the inherited share, and they add to, not replace, the environment.** Genome-wide association studies have found thousands of common variants for coronary disease, diabetes, breast cancer and schizophrenia, each shifting risk by a few percent. Summed into a polygenic risk score, they place a person in a liability distribution; the top few percent carry risks comparable to a monogenic mutation for coronary disease or breast cancer. Scores transfer poorly across ancestries, a limit with equity consequences. They identify who gains most from lowering a modifiable factor, which is their clinical use.

7. **Risk is distributed by society as much as by biology.** Income, education, occupation, housing, discrimination and place shape exposure to tobacco, diet, pollution, stress, infection and health care, and so distribute multifactorial disease along a social gradient: in most high-income countries, life expectancy differs by a decade between the poorest and richest areas. These "causes of the causes" act through the same mechanisms as every other risk factor; the multifactorial model has no boundary at the skin.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Multifactorial (complex) disease | Disease from many genetic variants and exposures of individually small effect |
| Liability–threshold model | Continuous, normally distributed liability with disease above a cut-off |
| Risk factor | A characteristic associated with increased probability of disease; causal or not |
| Relative and absolute risk | Ratio of risks between exposed and unexposed; the actual probability over a period |
| Heritability | Proportion of variance in a trait explained by genetic variation, in one population and environment |
| Gene–environment interaction | A genetic effect whose size depends on exposure, or vice versa |
| Mendelian randomisation | Use of inherited variants as natural experiments to test whether a risk factor is causal |
| Polygenic risk score | Weighted sum of common variants estimating inherited liability |
| Prevention paradox | A measure that offers little to each individual may prevent most cases in a population |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A heritability of 60% means 60% of the disease is genetic."** Heritability describes variance in a population, not causation in a person, and it changes when the environment changes.
- **"Risk factors are causes."** Many are markers or confounded; only mechanism and experiment, including Mendelian randomisation, establish cause, and only causes repay intervention.
- **"Treat the high-risk and the problem is solved."** Most events occur among the many at moderate risk; population measures prevent more disease than any clinic can.
