## Essence

Acute inflammation is the vascular and cellular response that delivers plasma proteins and leucocytes from the blood to a site of injury or infection within minutes to hours. Injured cells and microbes release signals, small vessels dilate and leak, neutrophils crawl out and digest what they find, and the response switches off once the trigger is cleared. The four classical signs (redness, heat, swelling, pain) are the visible face of three vascular events, and the chemistry that kills bacteria also damages host tissue. Every organ's "-itis" is this process in a particular place.

## Core tenets

1. **Inflammation is triggered by recognition of damage or danger, not by the injury itself.** Resident sentinel cells (macrophages, mast cells, dendritic cells) carry pattern-recognition receptors that bind conserved microbial molecules and molecules released from necrotic cells (damage-associated molecular patterns: Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP), uric acid, nuclear proteins). Binding activates the inflammasome and cytokine transcription, so sterile necrosis (a burn, an infarct, urate crystals) provokes the same cascade as a bacterium; a myocardial infarct causes fever and a raised white-cell count without any infection.

2. **Three vascular changes produce the cardinal signs.** First, brief arteriolar constriction then sustained vasodilatation (histamine, nitric oxide, prostaglandins) raises blood flow: redness and heat. Second, endothelial cells contract and retract (histamine, bradykinin, leukotrienes), opening gaps in postcapillary venules; combined with raised hydrostatic pressure this drives a protein-rich **exudate** into the tissue: swelling. Third, fluid loss concentrates red cells and slows flow (stasis), so leucocytes fall out of the axial stream and touch the wall. Pain comes from bradykinin and prostaglandins sensitising nociceptors.

3. **The exudate is the point: it brings antibodies, complement and fibrin to a place the blood does not normally reach.** The leaky venule delivers opsonins (complement C3b, antibody) that mark targets for phagocytes, coagulation factors that lay down a fibrin mesh to wall off infection, and kinins that amplify the signal. An exudate is protein-rich; a transudate (the [oedema](../02-hemodynamic-disorders/edema-and-fluid-imbalance.md) of heart failure) is protein-poor because the wall is intact. Measuring protein in a pleural or peritoneal fluid therefore tells a clinician whether it came from inflammation or from hydrostatic forces.

4. **Leucocyte recruitment is a multistep adhesion cascade controlled by the endothelium.** Cytokines (tumour necrosis factor, interleukin-1) make endothelium display selectins, which tether passing neutrophils and make them roll; chemokines then activate neutrophil integrins to bind endothelial ligands and arrest the cell; the neutrophil squeezes between endothelial cells and follows a chemokine, C5a and leukotriene gradient to the target. Leucocyte adhesion deficiency (defective integrins) produces recurrent infections without pus because neutrophils cannot leave the blood.

5. **Neutrophils kill by phagocytosis and the oxidative burst, and the same chemistry damages the host.** The neutrophil engulfs an opsonised particle, fuses the phagosome with granules of proteases and antimicrobial peptides, and assembles NADPH oxidase, which pumps superoxide into the vacuole; myeloperoxidase makes hypochlorous acid from hydrogen peroxide and chloride. Chronic granulomatous disease (defective NADPH oxidase) shows how essential this is. But enzymes and oxidants leak, and dying neutrophils spill their contents: an abscess is a cavity dug by the host's own enzymes, and acute lung injury is the alveolar wall destroyed by the neutrophils sent to defend it.

6. **The mediators are redundant, short-lived and local, which is what makes the response controllable.** Histamine (mast cells) acts in seconds; arachidonic acid metabolites follow in minutes, prostaglandins via cyclo-oxygenase (vasodilatation, pain, fever) and leukotrienes via lipoxygenase (chemotaxis, permeability); cytokines act over hours. Plasma cascades (complement, kinins, coagulation) are activated at the site and inactivated within seconds by circulating inhibitors, so inflammation stays where the injury is unless the stimulus is overwhelming. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs block cyclo-oxygenase; glucocorticoids block transcription of many mediators at once.

7. **Systemic effects are the same mediators acting at a distance.** Interleukin-1, interleukin-6 and tumour necrosis factor reaching the hypothalamus raise the thermoregulatory set point (fever, via prostaglandin E2); reaching the liver they switch synthesis to acute-phase proteins (C-reactive protein, fibrinogen); reaching the marrow they release neutrophils. When the stimulus is large enough for these mediators to act on every vessel at once, local inflammation becomes the systemic vasodilatation and leak of [septic shock](../02-hemodynamic-disorders/hemorrhage-shock-and-sepsis.md).

8. **Resolution is an active programme, and its outcome depends on the tissue and the stimulus.** Neutrophils live a day or two in tissue and die by apoptosis; macrophages that ingest them switch to anti-inflammatory signals (interleukin-10, transforming growth factor beta, resolvins) and clear the exudate via lymphatics. If the agent is removed and the framework intact, the organ returns to normal. If pus forms, an abscess is walled off. If the stimulus persists, macrophages and lymphocytes replace neutrophils: [chronic inflammation](chronic-inflammation-and-granulomas.md). If the framework is destroyed, [repair by fibrosis](tissue-repair-regeneration-and-fibrosis.md) follows.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Exudate | Protein-rich inflammatory fluid that has crossed a leaky vessel wall; may contain fibrin and cells |
| Transudate | Protein-poor fluid formed by altered hydrostatic or oncotic pressure across an intact wall |
| Damage-associated molecular pattern | Host molecule released by necrotic cells that triggers pattern-recognition receptors |
| Inflammasome | Cytosolic multiprotein complex that activates caspase-1 to release interleukin-1 beta |
| Chemotaxis | Directed migration along a gradient of chemokines, C5a or leukotriene B4 |
| Opsonin | Molecule (C3b, antibody) that coats a particle and marks it for phagocytosis |
| Oxidative burst | NADPH-oxidase-driven production of superoxide and derived oxidants inside the phagosome |
| Acute-phase response | Liver-driven change in plasma proteins (C-reactive protein up, albumin down) induced by interleukin-6 |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Inflammation means infection."** Any necrosis, foreign body, crystal or immune reaction triggers it; a sterile infarct raises temperature and white-cell count just as a bacterium does.
- **"Inflammation is the damage."** The vascular and cellular response is the defence; the damage to host tissue is a side-effect of neutrophil chemistry, severe only when the response is large, prolonged or misdirected.
- **"Suppressing inflammation is always helpful."** It eases symptoms but can let infection spread or a wound fail; the mediators that hurt also recruit the cells that clear the cause.
