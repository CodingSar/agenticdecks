## Essence

After injury the body must refill the space that dead cells left. It has two ways: **regeneration**, replacing lost cells with the same kind from surviving cells or stem cells, and **repair by connective tissue**, filling the gap with a scar of collagen laid down by fibroblasts. Which happens depends on whether the tissue's cells can divide and whether the extracellular scaffold survived to guide them. A scar restores continuity but not function, so the long-term cost of any disease is largely the amount of working tissue replaced by collagen. Fibrosis is repair that will not stop.

## Core tenets

1. **Tissues differ in regenerative capacity, and that difference decides the outcome of every injury.** Labile tissues (epidermis, gut lining, marrow) renew continuously from stem cells and regenerate completely if the stem-cell compartment survives. Stable tissues (liver, kidney tubules, endothelium) are quiescent but re-enter the cell cycle after injury: a liver regains full mass within weeks of losing two-thirds of itself. Permanent tissues (cardiac myocytes, neurons) have negligible proliferative capacity, so a myocardial infarct or a stroke always heals as scar or glial tissue and the function is lost for good. This one fact explains why heart failure follows a large infarct and why a skin cut heals perfectly.

2. **Regeneration needs an intact scaffold; destroy the matrix and even a regenerating tissue scars.** Hepatocytes regenerate after viral hepatitis if the reticulin framework survives, but after repeated injury with collapse of the framework they regrow as disorganised nodules walled by fibrous septa: cirrhosis. Renal tubular cells regenerate along an intact basement membrane; scarred glomeruli do not. The matrix is not inert packing: it stores growth factors, orients migrating cells and tells them when to stop.

3. **Repair proceeds through a fixed sequence driven by growth factors, mostly from macrophages.** Within a day a fibrin clot fills the defect and macrophages arrive to clear debris and release platelet-derived growth factor, transforming growth factor beta and vascular endothelial growth factor. By day 3–5 new capillaries sprout (angiogenesis) and fibroblasts migrate in, forming **granulation tissue**: pink, soft, bleeding, the visible sign of healing. Fibroblasts deposit type III collagen, replaced over weeks by stronger type I; vessels regress and the tissue pales to scar. Myofibroblasts contract the wound.

4. **Scar strength is gained slowly and never fully.** A sutured skin wound has about 10% of normal tensile strength at one week, 70–80% at three months, and never exceeds roughly 80% of unwounded skin. Strength comes from collagen cross-linking and remodelling by matrix metalloproteinases over a year or more. This time course dictates why abdominal incisions dehisce in the first week or two, and why vitamin C deficiency (collagen hydroxylation needs ascorbate) and glucocorticoids (which suppress collagen synthesis) make wounds fail.

5. **Healing by first intention and second intention are the same process at different scales.** A clean incision with apposed edges (first intention) needs little granulation tissue, re-epithelialises within 48 hours and leaves a thin scar. A large open defect (second intention) must fill with granulation tissue, contracts more, takes weeks to months and leaves a broad scar that can distort a joint or an eyelid. A keloid, collagen outgrowing the wound, shows that the stop signal matters as much as the start.

6. **Local and systemic factors that impair any step impair healing.** Infection prolongs inflammation and raises protease activity; ischaemia (arterial disease, diabetic microangiopathy, venous hypertension) starves the wound of the oxygen that collagen synthesis and neutrophil killing need; a foreign body or necrotic tissue perpetuates inflammation; malnutrition, diabetes and glucocorticoids weaken fibroblasts and leucocytes. A chronic leg ulcer is a wound stuck in the inflammatory phase because one of these was never corrected.

7. **Fibrosis is repair that cannot stop, and transforming growth factor beta is its master switch.** When injury persists (viral hepatitis, alcohol, inhaled silica, autoimmunity, hypertension), macrophages and injured epithelium release transforming growth factor beta continuously. It converts fibroblasts and stellate cells to myofibroblasts, drives collagen synthesis and suppresses the metalloproteinases that would remove it. Fibrosis is the common end stage of chronic disease in liver (cirrhosis), lung, kidney and heart: different causes, one final pathway, and the reason organ failure in these diseases is hard to reverse.

8. **Scar changes mechanics and electrics, not just cell count.** Fibrous tissue does not contract, conduct, exchange gas or filter plasma. A fibrotic ventricle fills poorly, and the border between scar and muscle is where re-entrant arrhythmias arise. A fibrotic lung is stiff and its thickened alveolar walls slow diffusion. A cirrhotic liver resists flow, raising portal pressure. Each syndrome follows from what collagen cannot do.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Regeneration | Replacement of lost cells by proliferation of the same cell type, restoring normal structure |
| Repair (scarring) | Replacement of lost tissue by collagen-rich connective tissue |
| Labile, stable and permanent tissues | Continuously dividing, quiescent-but-capable, and non-dividing cell populations |
| Granulation tissue | New capillaries, fibroblasts and loose matrix that fill a healing defect |
| Myofibroblast | Contractile fibroblast that shrinks wounds and deposits collagen |
| Matrix metalloproteinases | Zinc-dependent enzymes that degrade and remodel the extracellular matrix |
| Transforming growth factor beta | The dominant pro-fibrotic cytokine; also anti-inflammatory |
| Cirrhosis | End-stage liver fibrosis with regenerative nodules and distorted architecture |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A healed wound is as good as new."** Scar never exceeds about 80% of the original tensile strength and never regains the function of the tissue it replaced; only true regeneration restores function.
- **"Fibrosis is a passive accumulation of collagen."** It is an active, cytokine-driven programme, and in its early stages it is partly reversible when the injury stops (as liver fibrosis can regress after viral hepatitis is cured).
- **"Inflammation and repair are separate phases."** Macrophages recruited by inflammation supply the growth factors for repair; a wound that cannot mount inflammation (neutropenia, glucocorticoids) heals poorly, and one that cannot stop inflaming heals as fibrosis.
