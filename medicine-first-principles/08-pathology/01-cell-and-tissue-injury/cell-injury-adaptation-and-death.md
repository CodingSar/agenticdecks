## Essence

Every disease begins as a cell that cannot keep up with what is asked of it. A stressed cell has three outcomes: it **adapts** (changes size, number or type), it is **reversibly injured** (swells and loses function but recovers if the stress stops) or it **dies**. The outcome depends on the insult's type, severity and duration, and on the cell's reserve. The organising idea is energy: nearly every pathway of injury runs through loss of Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP), the ion gradients ATP maintains, and the mitochondria that make it.

## Core tenets

1. **Adaptation is physiology stretched; it precedes injury and is reversible.** A cell asked to do more grows (hypertrophy, in cells that cannot divide, such as cardiac muscle under high afterload) or multiplies (hyperplasia, in cells that can, such as endometrium under oestrogen). A cell asked to do less shrinks (atrophy, by autophagy and proteasomal digestion of its own parts). A cell facing a new irritant may switch type (metaplasia: bronchial ciliated epithelium becoming squamous in a smoker). Each reverses when the signal stops, but each has a limit: the hypertrophied heart outgrows its capillary supply, and metaplastic epithelium is a step towards dysplasia and [cancer](../03-neoplasia/hallmarks-of-cancer.md).

2. **Hypoxia and ischaemia injure by stopping ATP synthesis, and everything else follows from that.** Oxidative phosphorylation needs oxygen as the final electron acceptor; without it the cell falls back on glycolysis, which yields about one-fifteenth as much ATP per glucose and produces lactic acid. ATP shortage stops the sodium–potassium pump, so sodium and water enter and the cell swells; it stops calcium pumps, so cytosolic calcium rises and activates phospholipases, proteases and endonucleases. All of this is reversible if oxygen returns in time: roughly 20–40 minutes for cardiac myocytes, only a few minutes for neurons.

3. **The point of no return is mitochondrial and membrane failure.** Irreversible injury is marked by the mitochondrial permeability transition, which collapses the proton gradient so ATP can never be restored, and by loss of plasma membrane integrity, which lets enzymes leak out and calcium flood in. The leaked proteins are what clinical chemistry measures: troponin after myocardial infarction, transaminases after hepatocyte injury. A blood test for organ damage is a measure of membrane rupture.

4. **Reperfusion can add to the injury it was meant to stop.** Returning oxygen to ischaemia-primed tissue produces a burst of reactive oxygen species from damaged mitochondria, a surge of calcium entry, complement activation and neutrophil recruitment. This is why the final infarct after reopening a coronary artery is larger than what was dead when flow returned.

5. **Reactive oxygen species are a shared final pathway of many insults.** Superoxide, hydrogen peroxide and the hydroxyl radical come from leaky mitochondria, neutrophil enzymes, radiation splitting water, and the metabolism of toxins such as paracetamol. They peroxidise membrane lipids in a chain reaction, oxidise protein thiols and break DNA strands. Cells hold antioxidant defences (superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione), so injury means production exceeding capacity; the glutathione depletion of paracetamol overdose is the textbook case.

6. **Necrosis and apoptosis are different deaths with different consequences for the tissue.** Necrosis follows catastrophic injury: the cell swells, membranes rupture, contents spill and provoke [acute inflammation](acute-inflammation.md). Apoptosis is an ordered, ATP-requiring programme triggered by internal damage (the mitochondrial pathway, gated by Bcl-2 family proteins and executed by caspases) or by death receptors; the cell shrinks, its DNA is cut between nucleosomes, and phagocytes clear the fragments without inflammation. Tissue turnover, hormone withdrawal and the removal of virus-infected or DNA-damaged cells all use apoptosis; its failure lets cells with damaged genomes survive to become tumours. Necroptosis and pyroptosis are regulated necrosis, used when a pathogen blocks apoptosis and inflammation is wanted.

7. **The pattern of necrosis is read from the tissue and tells you the cause.** Coagulative necrosis (ghost outlines preserved, enzymes denatured by acidosis) is the signature of ischaemia in solid organs. Liquefactive necrosis (tissue digested to fluid) follows bacterial infection and, uniquely, ischaemia in the brain. Caseous necrosis (cheese-like, with granulomas) means tuberculosis. Fat necrosis means pancreatic lipase has escaped. Fibrinoid necrosis in vessel walls means immune complexes or malignant hypertension.

8. **Intracellular accumulations and calcification are the residue of disordered metabolism.** Fat in hepatocytes (steatosis) means triglyceride supply outruns export, as in alcohol excess and obesity. Misfolded protein accumulates when quality control is overwhelmed, as in alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency and amyloidosis. Dystrophic calcification deposits calcium in damaged tissue at normal serum calcium (atherosclerotic plaque, old valves); metastatic calcification deposits it in normal tissue because serum calcium is high.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Hypertrophy | Increase in cell size (and organ size) in response to increased load or trophic signals |
| Hyperplasia | Increase in cell number in a tissue whose cells can divide |
| Atrophy | Decrease in cell size and function from loss of load, blood supply, innervation, nutrition or hormonal stimulation |
| Metaplasia | Reversible replacement of one differentiated cell type by another better suited to a chronic irritant |
| Ischaemia | Loss of blood flow, removing oxygen, substrate and waste removal together |
| Mitochondrial permeability transition | Opening of inner-membrane pores that collapses the proton gradient and commits the cell to death |
| Reactive oxygen species | Partially reduced oxygen derivatives (superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radical) that damage lipids, proteins and DNA |
| Necrosis | Uncontrolled cell death with membrane rupture, leakage of contents and inflammation |
| Apoptosis | Programmed, caspase-mediated cell death with membrane preservation and phagocytic clearance, without inflammation |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Hypoxia and ischaemia are the same thing."** Ischaemia also removes glucose and lets lactate, hydrogen ions and potassium accumulate, so it injures faster and deeper than hypoxia with preserved flow.
- **"Apoptosis is always pathological."** Most apoptosis is physiological: embryonic sculpting, immune-cell selection, turnover of gut epithelium. Disease arises from too little (cancer, autoimmunity) as well as too much (neurodegeneration).
- **"Once blood flow is restored the damage stops."** Reperfusion adds oxidative and inflammatory injury; the final infarct is larger than the territory already dead at reperfusion.
