## Essence

A neoplasm is a clone of cells that grows without regard to the signals that govern its neighbours. Cancer is the malignant form: the clone also invades and spreads. Hundreds of cancer types, each with its own mutations, converge on a short list of acquired capabilities, the "hallmarks" that Hanahan and Weinberg distilled in 2000 and extended since: sustained proliferative signalling, evasion of growth suppressors, resistance to cell death, replicative immortality, angiogenesis, invasion and metastasis, reprogrammed metabolism and escape from immune destruction, enabled by genomic instability and tumour-promoting inflammation. The idea that makes the list coherent is Darwinian: cancer is evolution by natural selection among the body's own cells, and each hallmark is a trait that selection within the tissue rewards.

## Core tenets

1. **Cancer is clonal evolution inside the body.** Every division risks mutation; most mutations are harmless or lethal to the cell, but one that lets a cell outgrow its neighbours is selected. Over years that clone acquires further mutations, and sub-clones compete. A tumour at diagnosis is a population, genetically diverse, that has already passed through several selective bottlenecks (hypoxia, immune attack, later treatment). This is why cancers take decades, why incidence rises steeply with age, why a single tumour contains resistant cells before any drug is given, and why relapse after therapy is usually a pre-existing sub-clone, not a new disease.

2. **Sustained proliferative signalling: the growth-factor pathway is switched on from inside.** Normal cells divide only when growth factors bind receptor tyrosine kinases and the signal passes through RAS, the mitogen-activated protein kinase cascade and phosphoinositide 3-kinase to the cell-cycle machinery. Cancers make their own growth factor, overexpress or mutate the receptor (HER2 in breast cancer, EGFR in lung cancer), or lock a downstream switch on (RAS mutated in about a quarter of cancers, BRAF in melanoma). Each is a drug target precisely because the cell depends on it: "oncogene addiction".

3. **Evading growth suppressors and resisting death: the brakes are cut.** The retinoblastoma protein gates entry to the cell cycle and p53 halts it or triggers apoptosis when Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) is damaged; TP53 is mutated in about half of all cancers. With the brakes gone, a cell carrying damaged DNA divides rather than dying. Cancers also overexpress anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 (follicular lymphoma) or lose pro-apoptotic signals, so cells that should have been culled by stress, detachment or oncogene activation survive. The [cell-death](../../03-cell-biology/02-the-cell-life-cycle/cell-death-apoptosis-necrosis-and-autophagy.md) programme that protects the organism is the programme cancer must disable.

4. **Replicative immortality: telomerase removes the division counter.** Normal somatic cells lose telomere length with each division and after some 40–60 doublings enter senescence or crisis. About 90% of cancers reactivate telomerase, and the rest lengthen telomeres by recombination, so the clone can divide indefinitely. Cells that pass through crisis without telomerase suffer chromosome fusions and breakage, a source of the genomic chaos that supplies further mutations.

5. **Angiogenesis and reprogrammed metabolism feed a mass that would otherwise starve.** A tumour cannot grow beyond 1–2 mm without a blood supply, because oxygen diffuses only that far. Hypoxic tumour cells stabilise hypoxia-inducible factor and release vascular endothelial growth factor, recruiting leaky, disorganised vessels. Even with oxygen, most cancers run glycolysis and secrete lactate (the Warburg effect), which supplies carbon for biosynthesis, acidifies the surroundings and is the basis of fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography imaging.

6. **Invasion, metastasis and immune evasion are what make a neoplasm lethal.** A benign tumour has every hallmark except these and kills only by pressure or hormone secretion. Malignant cells lose E-cadherin adhesion, degrade basement membrane and migrate; a minority survive in blood and seed distant organs, which is the subject of the [invasion and metastasis](invasion-metastasis-and-the-tumour-microenvironment.md) leaf. Along the way they must evade T cells: by losing antigen presentation, by expressing ligands such as Programmed Death-Ligand 1 (PD-L1) that switch T cells off, or by recruiting suppressive cells. Checkpoint-blocking antibodies work by reversing that last hallmark, and they work because an immune response to the tumour already existed.

7. **Genomic instability and inflammation are the enabling conditions.** The hallmarks need many mutations, and a normal mutation rate would not supply them in a lifetime; defects in DNA repair (mismatch repair in Lynch syndrome, BRCA in breast and ovarian cancer) and chromosomal instability raise the rate. Inflammatory cells supply growth factors, survival signals, angiogenic factors and mutagenic oxidants, so a tumour's stroma resembles a wound that never heals. Together they explain why cancers arise where tissue divides fast and is chronically inflamed, and why the hallmarks are acquired in no fixed order.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Neoplasm | An abnormal clone of cells growing autonomously and persisting after the stimulus stops |
| Benign versus malignant | Malignant neoplasms invade and metastasise; benign ones remain local and encapsulated |
| Clonal evolution | Successive mutation and selection of sub-clones within a tumour |
| Oncogene addiction | A cancer's dependence on one activated pathway, exploited by targeted drugs |
| Senescence | Permanent cell-cycle arrest after telomere shortening or oncogenic stress |
| Telomerase | Enzyme that rebuilds telomeres, re-expressed in most cancers |
| Angiogenic switch | The point at which a tumour recruits its own blood supply |
| Warburg effect | Preference for glycolysis even in the presence of oxygen |
| Immune checkpoint | Inhibitory receptor–ligand pair (PD-1 with PD-L1, CTLA-4) that switches off T cells |
| Tumour heterogeneity | Genetic and phenotypic diversity among cells of one tumour |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Cancer is uncontrolled cell division."** Division is necessary but not sufficient; a benign tumour divides too. Lethality comes from invasion, metastasis and the failure to die.
- **"A cancer is one kind of cell."** It is an evolving population of sub-clones; the cells that survive treatment were usually present before it began.
- **"Cancer cells are weak because they are abnormal."** They are the winners of years of selection within the tissue; each hallmark is a trait that won.
