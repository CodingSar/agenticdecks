## Essence

Cancer is a disease of the genome. The genes whose mutation drives it fall into two classes with opposite logic: **oncogenes**, normal growth-promoting genes that a mutation turns permanently on, where one hit is enough; and **tumour suppressor genes**, brakes and repair systems that mutation turns off, where both copies must usually be lost. A third class, the caretakers of genome integrity, does not drive growth directly but raises the mutation rate so that driver mutations accumulate fast enough to produce cancer in a lifetime. A tumour typically carries a handful of driver mutations among thousands of passengers, and identifying the drivers is now how cancers are classified and how targeted drugs are chosen.

## Core tenets

1. **Oncogenes are accelerators stuck on, and one allele suffices.** A proto-oncogene encodes a normal component of a growth pathway: a growth factor, a receptor tyrosine kinase, a signal transducer, a transcription factor or a cell-cycle protein. It becomes an oncogene by a point mutation that locks the protein active (RAS unable to hydrolyse Guanosine Triphosphate (GTP); BRAF V600E), by amplification that multiplies its dose (HER2, MYC), or by a chromosomal rearrangement that fuses it to an active promoter or a partner that forces dimerisation (BCR-ABL in chronic myeloid leukaemia, which is why the Philadelphia chromosome is diagnostic and imatinib curative). Because the product is active regardless of the normal allele, the mutation acts dominantly at the cell level.

2. **Tumour suppressors are brakes released, and both copies must usually fail.** Knudson's two-hit hypothesis (1971), from retinoblastoma, explains inherited cancer syndromes: a child who inherits one defective copy of RB1 needs only one somatic hit in any retinal cell to lose the brake, so tumours are early, multiple and bilateral; a child with two normal copies needs two hits in the same cell, so sporadic retinoblastoma is late and single. The same arithmetic governs BRCA1 and BRCA2 (breast and ovarian cancer), APC (familial adenomatous polyposis) and the mismatch repair genes of Lynch syndrome. Loss of heterozygosity, the deletion of the remaining normal allele, is the commonest second hit.

3. **Two suppressors stand at the centre: RB and p53.** The retinoblastoma protein holds the E2F transcription factors that drive S-phase genes until cyclin-dependent kinases phosphorylate it; nearly every cancer disables this gate directly (RB1 loss), by overexpressing cyclin D or CDK4, or by losing the CDK inhibitor p16. Human papillomavirus E7 binds RB, and E6 degrades p53, which is how the virus causes cervical cancer. p53 is the "guardian of the genome": Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) damage, hypoxia or oncogene activation stabilise it, and it then arrests the cycle, induces repair, senescence or apoptosis. Lose it and damaged cells divide; TP53 is mutated in about half of all cancers and inherited mutation (Li-Fraumeni syndrome) produces cancers of many organs early in life.

4. **Growth-inhibitory and adhesion pathways are suppressors too.** Transforming growth factor beta signalling restrains epithelial proliferation and is lost in pancreatic and colon cancers. APC degrades beta-catenin; without it the Wnt pathway stays on and colonic crypts form adenomas, the first step of the adenoma–carcinoma sequence. PTEN switches off phosphoinositide 3-kinase signalling; E-cadherin keeps epithelial cells attached and its loss is a step towards invasion. Each pathway loss removes a signal that told the cell "enough" or "stay".

5. **Genomic instability is the engine that supplies drivers.** Cancer needs several drivers, and the spontaneous mutation rate would seldom provide them in a lifetime. Defective mismatch repair makes microsatellites unstable and raises point mutations about a hundred-fold (Lynch syndrome; also sporadic colon and endometrial cancers). Defective homologous recombination (BRCA1 and BRCA2) leaves double-strand breaks to be repaired by error-prone pathways and creates a dependence on the alternative enzyme Poly(ADP-ribose) Polymerase (PARP), which PARP inhibitors exploit (synthetic lethality). Defective nucleotide excision repair (xeroderma pigmentosum) gives skin cancers from ultraviolet damage. Chromosomal instability (errors in mitotic checkpoints, telomere crisis) produces aneuploidy and the translocations that create fusion oncogenes.

6. **Drivers and passengers: most mutations in a tumour do nothing.** A typical solid tumour carries thousands of somatic mutations; the number of drivers is usually 2–8. Passengers are the record of the mutational processes the tumour has lived through (ultraviolet light, tobacco, APOBEC enzymes, aging), and their "signatures" reveal the cause, as the [causes of cancer](causes-of-cancer.md) leaf describes. The genomics of a cancer is therefore read twice: for the drivers that can be targeted and for the signatures that tell the history.

7. **Epigenetic silencing and non-coding changes are mutations in effect.** A tumour suppressor can be switched off by promoter methylation without any change in sequence (MLH1 in sporadic microsatellite-unstable colon cancer; CDKN2A in many tumours), and mutations in chromatin-modifying genes (IDH, DNMT3A, TET2 in leukaemias) reshape expression broadly. MicroRNAs that suppress oncogenes can be lost. The genome's regulatory state is heritable through cell division, so an epigenetic hit is selected like a genetic one and can be inherited by every daughter cell in the clone.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Proto-oncogene | A normal gene promoting growth whose activation by mutation drives cancer |
| Oncogene | The activated, gain-of-function version; dominant at the cell level |
| Tumour suppressor gene | A gene restraining growth or preserving the genome; loss of both copies promotes cancer |
| Two-hit hypothesis | Knudson's model: inherited plus somatic, or two somatic, hits inactivate a suppressor |
| Loss of heterozygosity | Somatic loss of the remaining normal allele at a suppressor locus |
| Driver and passenger mutations | Mutations that confer selective advantage versus those carried along |
| Fusion gene | A hybrid gene from a chromosomal translocation, such as BCR-ABL |
| Microsatellite instability | Length changes in repetitive DNA marking defective mismatch repair |
| Synthetic lethality | Death of a cell lacking two pathways where loss of either alone is tolerated; basis of PARP inhibitors |
| Promoter hypermethylation | Epigenetic silencing of a gene without sequence change |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Inherited cancer syndromes mean cancer is inherited."** What is inherited is one hit; the tumour still needs somatic events, which is why penetrance is incomplete and onset varies. Only 5–10% of cancers involve a high-penetrance inherited mutation.
- **"More mutations means a worse cancer."** Mutation count reflects exposure and repair capacity, not aggressiveness; highly mutated tumours often respond better to immunotherapy because they present more foreign peptides.
- **"Oncogenes are abnormal genes."** They are normal genes in an abnormal state; the same RAS that drives a tumour drives every cell's response to growth factors.
