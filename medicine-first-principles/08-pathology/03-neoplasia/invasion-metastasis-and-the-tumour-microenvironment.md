## Essence

Metastasis, the growth of a tumour at a site distant from its origin, causes about 90% of cancer deaths. It is a multistep journey: cells detach from their neighbours, digest through the basement membrane, move through stroma, enter a blood or lymph vessel, survive transit, lodge, leave the vessel and grow in a foreign tissue. Each step is inefficient, so of millions of cells shed daily only a few found a metastasis, and which organs they colonise depends partly on where the blood flows and partly on which "soil" suits the "seed". None of this happens in isolation: a tumour is an organ with its own stroma of fibroblasts, vessels, immune cells and matrix, which the cancer recruits and which in turn shapes its behaviour.

## Core tenets

1. **Invasion begins with loss of adhesion and ends with proteolysis.** Epithelial cells are held by E-cadherin at adherens junctions and anchored to basement membrane by integrins. Carcinoma cells lose or mutate E-cadherin, change integrin repertoire and undergo a partial epithelial–mesenchymal transition, taking on a migratory, fibroblast-like shape driven by transcription factors (SNAIL, TWIST) and by signals such as transforming growth factor beta from the stroma. They then secrete, or induce stromal cells to secrete, matrix metalloproteinases and cathepsins that cut collagen IV and laminin in the basement membrane. The breach of the basement membrane is the histological definition of invasion and the line between carcinoma in situ and cancer.

2. **Migration uses the ordinary machinery of cell movement, steered by the stroma.** Invading cells extend actin-rich protrusions, bind matrix with integrins, pull themselves forward and release behind, following gradients of growth factors and matrix fragments released by their own proteolysis. Cancer-associated fibroblasts align collagen into tracks the cells travel along; macrophages at the invasive front hand cells to vessels. Invasion is not a new programme but wound-healing migration without the signal to stop.

3. **Spread follows anatomy, and the route is the first clue to the primary.** Carcinomas tend to spread first through lymphatics to regional nodes, which is why lymph node status is the strongest prognostic factor in most solid tumours and why sentinel node biopsy works; sarcomas spread mainly by blood. Venous drainage decides the first filter: gut tumours reach the liver through the portal vein, most others reach the lung through the caval system, and vertebral venous plexuses carry prostate and breast cancer to the spine. Direct seeding across a body cavity (ovarian cancer across the peritoneum) is the third route.

4. **Transit is the bottleneck: most circulating tumour cells die.** Cells in the blood face shear, loss of matrix attachment (which normally triggers anoikis, a form of apoptosis) and natural killer cells. Survivors travel in clusters or coated with platelets and fibrin, which shields them and helps them arrest in capillaries. Fewer than 0.01% of cells that enter the circulation form a metastasis. This inefficiency explains why metastasis is late, why the number of shed cells matters less than their traits, and why detecting circulating tumour cells or Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) is possible without every patient developing metastases.

5. **"Seed and soil": colonisation depends on the match between cancer cell and tissue.** Paget observed in 1889 that breast cancer prefers bone, liver, lung and brain, far more than blood flow explains, while skeletal muscle and spleen are almost never colonised. A disseminated cell must find a niche with the right growth factors, matrix and permissive immune cells; bone's stromal cytokines suit breast and prostate cells, and the tumour can prepare a "pre-metastatic niche" by sending exosomes and cytokines ahead to recruit marrow-derived cells. Cells that land in unsuitable soil die or lie dormant for years, which is why breast cancer can recur a decade after apparent cure.

6. **The tumour microenvironment is recruited normal tissue working for the tumour.** Cancer cells secrete platelet-derived growth factor and transforming growth factor beta to convert fibroblasts into cancer-associated fibroblasts that lay down stiff matrix and release growth factors; they release vascular endothelial growth factor to build leaky vessels; they recruit tumour-associated macrophages that suppress T cells, promote angiogenesis and help invasion; regulatory T cells and myeloid suppressor cells follow. The stroma can be half the tumour's mass. Hypoxia, acidity and high interstitial pressure in this environment select for aggressive cells and impede drug delivery, which is why targeting the stroma and its vessels is part of therapy.

7. **Immune escape is a property of the ecosystem, not only the cell.** Tumours that have been edited by immunity lose the antigens or the antigen-presenting molecules that T cells recognise, express Programmed Death-Ligand 1 (PD-L1) to exhaust the T cells that arrive, and build a stroma that excludes them. "Hot" tumours with infiltrating T cells respond to checkpoint blockade; "cold" ones, with no infiltrate or an excluded one, do not. The therapeutic lesson is that the microenvironment, as much as the genome, decides outcome, and that the immune system can control metastasis when the brakes are released.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Carcinoma in situ | A malignant epithelial clone that has not yet breached the basement membrane |
| Epithelial–mesenchymal transition | Loss of epithelial adhesion and polarity with gain of motility, often partial in carcinoma |
| E-cadherin | The adhesion molecule of adherens junctions whose loss permits invasion |
| Matrix metalloproteinases | Zinc proteases that degrade basement membrane and stromal collagen |
| Anoikis | Apoptosis triggered by loss of matrix attachment, a barrier to cells in transit |
| Sentinel lymph node | The first node draining a tumour, whose status predicts the rest |
| Seed and soil | Paget's principle that metastasis needs a compatible host tissue |
| Pre-metastatic niche | A distant site prepared by tumour-derived factors before cells arrive |
| Tumour dormancy | Disseminated cells surviving for years without growing |
| Cancer-associated fibroblast | Stromal fibroblast reprogrammed to support tumour growth and invasion |
| Tumour-associated macrophage | Macrophage polarised to a repair phenotype that aids angiogenesis and immune suppression |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Metastasis is a late event that happens when the tumour gets big."** Dissemination can occur early, and metastatic cells may lie dormant for years; size correlates with risk but does not cause it.
- **"Cancer cells in the blood mean metastatic disease."** Almost all die in transit; colonisation, not circulation, is the rate-limiting step.
- **"The stroma is bystander tissue."** It is recruited, reprogrammed and essential; its vessels, fibroblasts and macrophages are legitimate therapeutic targets.
