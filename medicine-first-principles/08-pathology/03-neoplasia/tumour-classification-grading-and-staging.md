## Essence

To treat a tumour one must answer three questions: what is it (classification), how abnormal does it look (grade), and how far has it spread (stage). Classification names the tissue of origin and whether the neoplasm is benign or malignant, because that decides its behaviour and the drugs it will answer to. Grade describes differentiation and proliferation under the microscope, a proxy for how aggressive the clone is. Stage records the anatomical extent, tumour size, nodes and metastases, and is in most cancers the strongest predictor of survival and the main determinant of whether local treatment can cure. Together they turn a mass into a prognosis and a plan, and they are the language in which oncology, surgery and pathology speak to each other.

## Core tenets

1. **Nomenclature encodes the cell of origin and behaviour.** Benign tumours take the suffix "-oma" on the tissue name: adenoma (glandular epithelium), lipoma, leiomyoma, chondroma. Malignant epithelial tumours are carcinomas (adenocarcinoma from glands, squamous cell carcinoma from squamous epithelium); malignant mesenchymal tumours are sarcomas (osteosarcoma, liposarcoma); malignant tumours of blood-forming cells are leukaemias and lymphomas. The exceptions are historical (melanoma, lymphoma, seminoma are malignant despite the suffix). The system works because a tumour usually keeps enough of its parent tissue's structure, proteins and gene expression to be recognised, and because tumours from the same tissue share behaviour, spread and drug sensitivity.

2. **Benign and malignant are distinguished by four features, and only one is decisive.** Benign tumours are well differentiated, grow slowly, are circumscribed or encapsulated, and neither invade nor metastasise. Malignant tumours show anaplasia (variable nuclear size and shape, high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, prominent nucleoli, abnormal mitoses, loss of polarity), grow erratically, infiltrate without a capsule, and can metastasise. Only invasion and metastasis are decisive: a well-differentiated carcinoma may look almost benign yet spread, and some benign tumours have worrying mitotic counts. Hence the pathologist's attention to margins and the basement membrane.

3. **Dysplasia and carcinoma in situ are the stages before invasion, and the basis of screening.** Many epithelial cancers pass through years of disordered growth confined to the epithelium: cells crowd, lose polarity and acquire anaplastic nuclei without crossing the basement membrane. Low-grade dysplasia may regress; high-grade dysplasia and carcinoma in situ are clonal and often progress. Because they are detectable (cervical cytology, colonoscopic polyps, Barrett's biopsies) and curable by local removal, they are what cervical and colorectal screening look for. The adenoma–carcinoma sequence of the colon, with its ordered acquisition of APC, KRAS and TP53 mutations, is the model of this progression.

4. **Grade measures how much the tumour resembles its parent and how fast it divides.** Pathologists score differentiation (gland formation, keratinisation), nuclear pleomorphism and mitotic count, usually into three or four grades. The Gleason score in prostate cancer and the Nottingham grade in breast cancer are examples. High grade predicts rapid growth, early metastasis and responsiveness to drugs that target dividing cells; low grade predicts indolence. Grade is partly subjective, varies within a tumour, and matters more in some cancers (sarcomas, prostate, brain gliomas, where it dominates prognosis) than others (where stage dominates).

5. **Stage measures anatomical extent, and it predicts survival better than almost anything else.** The Tumour, Node, Metastasis (TNM) system scores the primary tumour (T: size and local invasion), regional lymph nodes (N: number and location involved) and distant metastasis (M: present or absent), then groups combinations into stages I–IV. Five-year survival for colorectal cancer falls from above 90% at stage I to around 10–15% at stage IV; similar gradients hold for most carcinomas. Stage decides whether surgery or radiotherapy can remove all disease (local, curable in principle) or whether systemic therapy must be the backbone (disseminated). Clinical stage from imaging is revised to pathological stage after resection, which is more accurate because the nodes have been examined.

6. **Molecular classification is becoming part of the name.** Tumours that look identical may carry different drivers and respond to different drugs: lung adenocarcinoma with an EGFR mutation, an ALK fusion or neither; breast cancer by oestrogen receptor, HER2 and proliferation status; glioma by IDH mutation and 1p/19q co-deletion, which the World Health Organization classification now requires; leukaemias and lymphomas by translocation and immunophenotype. Immunohistochemistry, fluorescence in situ hybridisation and sequencing have moved from research to the routine report, and some agents (checkpoint inhibitors for mismatch-repair-deficient tumours) are approved by molecular feature regardless of organ.

7. **Classification, grade and stage are probability statements, and they interact with how the tumour was found.** Each category is a population average: a stage II patient has a distribution of outcomes, not a fate. Screening finds slow-growing tumours disproportionately (length bias) and finds them earlier (lead-time bias), so survival from diagnosis improves even when death is not postponed, and apparent stage shifts need careful reading; overdiagnosis of indolent tumours (some prostate, thyroid and breast lesions) is the cost. Prognostic tools combine stage, grade and molecular markers into risk estimates, which is where [prognosis and shared decision-making](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/01-the-clinical-encounter/prognosis-and-shared-decision-making.md) takes over.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Carcinoma | Malignant tumour of epithelium |
| Sarcoma | Malignant tumour of mesenchymal (connective) tissue |
| Adenoma | Benign glandular epithelial tumour; the precursor of most colorectal carcinomas |
| Anaplasia | Loss of differentiation: pleomorphism, high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, abnormal mitoses |
| Dysplasia | Disordered, pre-malignant epithelial growth confined to the epithelium |
| Carcinoma in situ | Full-thickness malignant change without invasion through the basement membrane |
| Grade | Histological measure of differentiation and proliferation |
| TNM stage | Tumour size and invasion, nodal involvement, distant metastasis |
| Surgical margin | Whether tumour reaches the edge of the resected specimen |
| Immunohistochemistry | Staining of tissue proteins with antibodies to establish origin and markers |
| Lead-time and length bias | Distortions of survival statistics introduced by finding tumours earlier or finding slower ones |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Benign means harmless."** A benign tumour can kill by compressing the brain, obstructing a duct or secreting hormone; it simply does not invade or spread.
- **"Grade and stage are the same thing."** Grade is what the cells look like; stage is where they are. A low-grade tumour can be stage IV and a high-grade one stage I.
- **"Better survival after screening proves screening saves lives."** Lead-time and length bias improve survival statistics without postponing death; only reduced mortality in a randomised trial proves benefit.
