## Essence

Cancer is caused by mutations, and the causes of cancer are the things that make mutations or let mutated cells grow: chemicals, radiation, infectious agents, chronic inflammation, hormones, inherited susceptibility and, underlying all of them, the copying errors of normal cell division accumulating with age. The question "what caused this cancer?" has two layers: the mutational process, which the genome records as a signature, and the exposure that drove it, which epidemiology reveals. Roughly 30–50% of cancers are attributable to known modifiable exposures (tobacco, infection, alcohol, diet and obesity, ultraviolet light, occupational agents), which is why prevention and vaccination remove more cancer than any treatment.

## Core tenets

1. **Most mutations come from normal life; carcinogens add to a baseline.** Every cell division copies three billion base pairs and makes a few errors despite repair; oxidative damage and spontaneous deamination of cytosine add more. Tissues whose stem cells divide most (colon, skin, blood) have the most cancers, and incidence of most carcinomas rises roughly with the fourth to sixth power of age, as expected if several independent events must accumulate. A carcinogen does not create a new process; it raises the rate of one, or adds a signature of its own. This is why cancer occurs without any identifiable exposure and why risk rises with age even in the absence of exposure.

2. **Chemical carcinogens are electrophiles, often made so by the body's own enzymes.** Most carcinogens are not reactive as inhaled or eaten. Cytochrome P450 enzymes in liver, lung and gut convert polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (tobacco smoke, charred food), aromatic amines (dyes, cooked meat) and aflatoxin (mouldy grain) into electrophiles that bind guanine and other bases, forming adducts that cause mispairing at the next replication. Direct-acting agents (alkylating drugs used in chemotherapy) need no activation and cause the second cancers of survivors. Dose matters: tobacco causes about 20% of cancer deaths worldwide and the risk tracks cumulative pack-years, falling slowly after cessation as damaged clones are outcompeted.

3. **Initiation and promotion are separable steps.** A mutagen (initiator) leaves a permanent change; a promoter (hormones, chronic irritation, obesity-related growth factors, alcohol) drives the initiated cell to divide so the change is fixed and expanded, but is not itself mutagenic and is reversible if withdrawn. Oestrogen promotes breast and endometrial cancer in this way, and the rise in endometrial cancer with unopposed oestrogen and its fall when progestogen is added are the natural experiment. Promotion explains why exposure order matters and why removing a non-mutagenic exposure (obesity, hormone therapy) still lowers risk.

4. **Radiation causes cancer in proportion to the energy it deposits in DNA.** Ultraviolet B light makes pyrimidine dimers, repaired by nucleotide excision; the signature C→T mutations fill the genomes of melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancer, and patients who cannot repair them (xeroderma pigmentosum) develop skin cancer in childhood. Ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays, radon, nuclear fallout) breaks both DNA strands; the Japanese atomic-bomb survivors and children exposed to Chernobyl iodine-131 showed leukaemia within years and solid tumours (thyroid, breast) decades later. Risk is roughly linear with dose down to low doses with no proven threshold, which is why medical imaging balances benefit against a small, cumulative cost.

5. **Infections cause about 13% of cancers worldwide, by insertion, by inflammation or by immunosuppression.** Human papillomavirus integrates and expresses E6 and E7, which degrade p53 and bind RB: cervical, anal and oropharyngeal cancer. Hepatitis B and C cause hepatocellular carcinoma mostly through decades of inflammation and regeneration. *Helicobacter pylori* causes gastric carcinoma and lymphoma by chronic gastritis; Epstein–Barr virus drives lymphomas and nasopharyngeal carcinoma; human T-lymphotropic virus 1 causes adult T-cell leukaemia; HIV causes cancer by removing immune control of other viruses (Kaposi sarcoma herpesvirus). Vaccination against hepatitis B and papillomavirus has already measurably reduced the cancers they cause, the clearest proof that an infectious cause was correct.

6. **Chronic inflammation is a carcinogenic field.** Repeated cycles of injury and regeneration supply the cell divisions that fix mutations; neutrophils and macrophages supply oxidants and nitrogen radicals that make them; cytokines and growth factors supply the promoters. Ulcerative colitis and colorectal cancer, Barrett's oesophagus and adenocarcinoma, asbestos and mesothelioma, cirrhosis of any cause and liver cancer are of this type. The [chronic inflammation](../01-cell-and-tissue-injury/chronic-inflammation-and-granulomas.md) leaf supplies the mechanism; the lesson here is that any state of persistent injury is a cancer risk.

7. **Inherited susceptibility ranges from one rare gene to many common variants.** High-penetrance mutations in tumour suppressors (BRCA1 and BRCA2, APC, mismatch repair genes, TP53, RB1) give lifetime risks of 50–90% but account for 5–10% of cancers. Far commoner are moderate- and low-penetrance variants, each shifting risk slightly, whose sum underlies much of the familial clustering of common cancers. Susceptibility also includes variation in carcinogen metabolism and DNA repair, so the same exposure gives different risks in different people. Causation is therefore an interaction of exposure, time and host, the pattern [multifactorial disease](../05-disease-as-process/multifactorial-disease-and-risk.md) describes in general.

8. **The genome records its causes as mutational signatures.** Each process leaves a characteristic pattern of base changes in their sequence context: ultraviolet light C→T at dipyrimidines, tobacco C→A transversions, defective mismatch repair small insertions and deletions, APOBEC enzymes C→T and C→G in TCW motifs, aging C→T at methylated CpG. A tumour's genome can therefore be read as a history of its exposures, and in some cases a signature reveals a cause (aristolochic acid in Balkan and Taiwanese urothelial cancer) before epidemiology does.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Carcinogen | Any agent that increases cancer incidence: chemical, physical or biological |
| Initiator and promoter | A mutagen that leaves a permanent change, and a non-mutagenic agent that expands the clone |
| Metabolic activation | Conversion of an inert procarcinogen to a reactive electrophile by cytochrome P450 |
| DNA adduct | A carcinogen covalently bound to a base, causing mispairing at replication |
| Pyrimidine dimer | The ultraviolet lesion linking adjacent thymines or cytosines |
| Oncogenic virus | A virus that causes cancer by viral oncoproteins, insertion or chronic inflammation |
| Attributable fraction | The proportion of cases that would not occur without a given exposure |
| Penetrance | The probability that a carrier of a susceptibility mutation develops the disease |
| Mutational signature | The characteristic pattern of base changes left by one mutagenic process |
| Latency | The interval, often decades, between exposure and clinical cancer |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Most cancer is bad luck or genes; lifestyle barely matters."** Known modifiable exposures account for roughly a third to a half of cancers in high-income countries, and vaccination and smoking cessation have already shifted national incidence.
- **"A carcinogen causes cancer on exposure."** Latency is usually decades; most exposed people never develop cancer; risk is probabilistic and dose-dependent.
- **"If it damages DNA it causes cancer, and if it does not, it cannot."** Promoters (hormones, obesity, alcohol in part) raise risk without mutating DNA, and many mutagens are repaired without consequence.
