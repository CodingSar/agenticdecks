## Essence

Cancer is caused by mutations, and the causes of cancer are the things that make mutations or let mutated cells grow: chemicals, radiation, infectious agents, chronic inflammation, hormones, inherited susceptibility and, underlying all of them, the copying errors of normal cell division accumulating with age. The question "what caused this cancer?" has two layers: the mutational process, which the genome records as a signature, and the exposure that drove it, which epidemiology reveals. Roughly 30–50% of cancers are attributable to known modifiable exposures (tobacco, infection, alcohol, diet and obesity, ultraviolet light, occupational agents), which is why prevention and vaccination remove more cancer than any treatment.

## Core tenets

1. **Most mutations come from normal life; carcinogens add to a baseline.** Every cell division copies three billion base pairs and makes a few errors despite repair; oxidative damage and spontaneous deamination of cytosine add more. Tissues whose stem cells divide most (colon, skin, blood) have the most cancers, and incidence of most carcinomas rises roughly with the fourth to sixth power of age, as expected if several independent events must accumulate. A carcinogen does not create a new process; it raises the rate of one, or adds a signature of its own.

2. **Chemical carcinogens are electrophiles, often made so by the body's own enzymes.** Cytochrome P450 enzymes convert polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (tobacco smoke, charred food), aromatic amines (dyes) and aflatoxin (mouldy grain) into electrophiles that bind guanine and other bases, forming adducts that mispair at the next replication. Direct-acting agents (alkylating chemotherapy drugs) need no activation and cause the second cancers of survivors. Tobacco causes about 20% of cancer deaths worldwide and the risk tracks cumulative pack-years, falling slowly after cessation.

3. **Initiation and promotion are separable steps.** A mutagen (initiator) leaves a permanent change; a promoter (hormones, chronic irritation, obesity-related growth factors, alcohol) drives the initiated cell to divide so the change is fixed and expanded, but is not itself mutagenic and is reversible if withdrawn. Oestrogen promotes breast and endometrial cancer in this way, and the rise in endometrial cancer with unopposed oestrogen and its fall when progestogen is added are the natural experiment.

4. **Radiation causes cancer in proportion to the energy it deposits in DNA.** Ultraviolet B light makes pyrimidine dimers, repaired by nucleotide excision; the signature C→T mutations fill the genomes of melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancer, and patients who cannot repair them (xeroderma pigmentosum) develop skin cancer in childhood. Ionising radiation breaks both DNA strands; atomic-bomb survivors and children exposed to Chernobyl iodine-131 showed leukaemia within years and solid tumours (thyroid, breast) decades later. Risk is roughly linear with dose with no proven threshold, which is why medical imaging balances benefit against a small, cumulative cost.

5. **Infections cause about 13% of cancers worldwide, by insertion, by inflammation or by immunosuppression.** Human papillomavirus integrates and expresses E6 and E7, which degrade p53 and bind RB: cervical, anal and oropharyngeal cancer. Hepatitis B and C cause hepatocellular carcinoma mostly through decades of inflammation and regeneration. *Helicobacter pylori* causes gastric carcinoma and lymphoma by chronic gastritis; Epstein–Barr virus drives lymphomas and nasopharyngeal carcinoma; human T-lymphotropic virus 1 causes adult T-cell leukaemia; HIV causes cancer by removing immune control of other viruses (Kaposi sarcoma herpesvirus). Vaccination against hepatitis B and papillomavirus has already measurably reduced the cancers they cause.

6. **Chronic inflammation is a carcinogenic field.** Repeated injury and regeneration supply the divisions that fix mutations; neutrophils and macrophages supply the oxidants that make them; cytokines supply the promoters. Ulcerative colitis and colorectal cancer, Barrett's oesophagus and adenocarcinoma, asbestos and mesothelioma, cirrhosis and liver cancer are of this type; the mechanism is in [chronic inflammation](../01-cell-and-tissue-injury/chronic-inflammation-and-granulomas.md).

7. **Inherited susceptibility ranges from one rare gene to many common variants.** High-penetrance mutations in tumour suppressors (BRCA1 and BRCA2, APC, mismatch repair genes, TP53, RB1) give lifetime risks of 50–90% but account for 5–10% of cancers. Far commoner are moderate- and low-penetrance variants, each shifting risk slightly, whose sum underlies much of the familial clustering of common cancers. Causation is therefore an interaction of exposure, time and host, the pattern [multifactorial disease](../05-disease-as-process/multifactorial-disease-and-risk.md) describes in general.

8. **The genome records its causes as mutational signatures.** Each process leaves a characteristic pattern of base changes: ultraviolet light C→T at dipyrimidines, tobacco C→A, defective mismatch repair small insertions and deletions, aging C→T at methylated CpG. A tumour's genome can be read as a history of its exposures, and a signature can reveal a cause (aristolochic acid in urothelial cancer) before epidemiology does.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Carcinogen | Any agent that increases cancer incidence: chemical, physical or biological |
| Initiator and promoter | A mutagen that leaves a permanent change, and a non-mutagenic agent that expands the clone |
| Metabolic activation | Conversion of an inert procarcinogen to a reactive electrophile by cytochrome P450 |
| DNA adduct | A carcinogen covalently bound to a base, causing mispairing at replication |
| Oncogenic virus | A virus that causes cancer by viral oncoproteins, insertion or chronic inflammation |
| Attributable fraction | The proportion of cases that would not occur without a given exposure |
| Penetrance | The probability that a carrier of a susceptibility mutation develops the disease |
| Mutational signature | The characteristic pattern of base changes left by one mutagenic process |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Most cancer is bad luck or genes; lifestyle barely matters."** Known modifiable exposures account for roughly a third to a half of cancers in high-income countries, and vaccination and smoking cessation have already shifted national incidence.
- **"A carcinogen causes cancer on exposure."** Latency is usually decades; most exposed people never develop cancer; risk is probabilistic and dose-dependent.
- **"If it damages DNA it causes cancer, and if it does not, it cannot."** Promoters (hormones, obesity, alcohol in part) raise risk without mutating DNA, and many mutagens are repaired without consequence.
