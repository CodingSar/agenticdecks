## Essence

Oedema is excess fluid in the interstitial space or a body cavity. It forms whenever the forces that push fluid out of capillaries exceed the forces that pull it back, or the lymphatics that drain the surplus fail. The whole subject rests on one balance, Starling's: filtration is driven by the hydrostatic pressure gradient and opposed by the oncotic pull of plasma proteins across a semipermeable wall. Heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome and inflammation each move one term of that balance, and the kidney, retaining sodium when it senses underfilling, turns a local leak into whole-body overload. Where oedema appears tells you which term moved; what it does depends on the organ it fills.

## Core tenets

1. **Oedema is Starling's balance tipped towards filtration.** Net filtration across a capillary wall is proportional to (capillary hydrostatic pressure − interstitial hydrostatic pressure) − σ(plasma oncotic pressure − interstitial oncotic pressure), where σ measures how well the wall excludes protein. Capillary hydrostatic pressure falls from about 35 mmHg at the arteriolar end to about 15 mmHg at the venous end while plasma oncotic pressure stays near 25 mmHg, so the small net outflow (2–4 litres a day for the whole body) is returned by lymphatics. Raise capillary pressure, lower plasma protein, make the wall leaky (σ falls) or block the lymphatics, and interstitial fluid accumulates.

   ```
   out = Kf [ (Pc - Pi) - sigma (pi_c - pi_i) ]
   heart failure: Pc up    nephrotic/cirrhosis: pi_c down
   inflammation:  sigma down, Kf up    lymphatic block: drainage down
   ```

2. **Raised venous pressure produces oedema where gravity and the failing chamber put it.** Capillary pressure follows venous pressure. Left ventricular failure raises pulmonary venous pressure and floods the alveoli; right ventricular failure or venous thrombosis raise systemic venous pressure, and fluid pools in the ankles of a standing patient or the sacrum of a lying one. Pulmonary oedema usually needs a pulmonary capillary pressure above about 25 mmHg, because lymphatic reserve and a tight alveolar wall protect the lung up to that point.

3. **Low plasma albumin removes the force that pulls fluid back, so oedema is generalised.** Albumin supplies most plasma oncotic pressure; loss through a leaky glomerulus (nephrotic syndrome), failed synthesis (cirrhosis) or severe malnutrition lowers it. Because every capillary is affected, the oedema is widespread, often periorbital, and less dependent on posture than venous oedema.

4. **Inflammatory oedema is a leaky wall, and its fluid is an exudate.** Histamine, bradykinin and leukotrienes open gaps between endothelial cells, so protein escapes and σ collapses; the oncotic gradient disappears and fluid follows. The fluid is protein-rich (exudate), unlike the transudate of hydrostatic and oncotic oedema, which is why measuring protein in an effusion separates inflammation and malignancy from heart or liver failure. Burns, sepsis and acute lung injury are this mechanism at its extreme: plasma leaks into tissue faster than the circulation can be refilled.

5. **Lymphatic obstruction causes localised, protein-rich, non-pitting oedema.** Lymphatics return filtered fluid and escaped protein. Block them (tumour, axillary surgery or radiotherapy, filarial worms) and both accumulate in the drained territory; the protein stimulates fibrosis, so the swelling becomes firm and the skin thickens.

6. **The kidney converts a local disturbance into whole-body sodium and water excess.** Oedema forms from plasma, so plasma volume should fall, yet heart failure and cirrhosis patients are overloaded. Arterial underfilling (low cardiac output, or splanchnic vasodilatation in cirrhosis) activates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, sympathetic nerves and vasopressin, and the kidney retains sodium and water. The retained fluid raises venous pressure or dilutes albumin further, forming more oedema: a positive feedback loop broken only by removing sodium (diuretics) or fixing the underfilling. This is why oedema is treated through the kidney whatever its origin.

7. **The harm of oedema depends on where the fluid is.** Ankle oedema is uncomfortable. Pulmonary oedema widens the diffusion distance for oxygen and stiffens the lung: breathlessness, hypoxaemia and, if alveoli flood, respiratory failure. Cerebral oedema raises pressure inside a rigid skull and can herniate the brain; laryngeal oedema in anaphylaxis closes the airway. Hyponatraemia swells cells everywhere, but the brain, which cannot expand, is where it kills.

8. **Fluid imbalance has two axes, volume and tonicity, and they are regulated separately.** Extracellular volume is sodium content, sensed by baroreceptors and corrected by the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system; tonicity is the sodium concentration, sensed by osmoreceptors and corrected by thirst and vasopressin. Dehydration (water loss, high sodium) and volume depletion (salt and water loss) are different states needing different fluid; hyponatraemia is nearly always excess water from vasopressin acting when it should not. Confusing the axes is the commonest error in fluid prescribing.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Starling forces | Hydrostatic and oncotic pressures that determine net fluid movement across a capillary wall |
| Oncotic (colloid osmotic) pressure | Osmotic pull of plasma proteins, mainly albumin (~25 mmHg) |
| Reflection coefficient (σ) | How completely a wall excludes protein; 1 for impermeable, 0 for freely leaky |
| Transudate | Protein-poor oedema fluid from hydrostatic or oncotic imbalance across an intact wall |
| Exudate | Protein-rich fluid from an inflamed, leaky wall |
| Lymphoedema | Protein-rich, non-pitting oedema from lymphatic obstruction |
| Effusion | Oedema fluid in a serous cavity: pleural, pericardial, peritoneal (ascites) |
| Arterial underfilling | Low effective arterial volume that triggers renal sodium retention despite total body excess |
| Tonicity | Effective osmolality; set by the sodium concentration, regulated by vasopressin and thirst |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Oedema means too much fluid, so give a diuretic."** The fluid is in the wrong compartment; in sepsis or nephrotic syndrome the circulation may be underfilled while the tissues are flooded, and the first question is which Starling term moved.
- **"Low sodium means sodium deficit."** Hyponatraemia is nearly always water excess relative to sodium, usually from inappropriate vasopressin; giving salt treats the wrong axis.
- **"Pulmonary oedema always comes from the heart."** Acute lung injury produces the same flooded alveoli with normal cardiac pressures, because the wall leaks rather than the pressure rising.
