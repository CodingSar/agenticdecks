## Essence

Shock is the failure of the circulation to deliver enough oxygen to the tissues, whatever the cause. Perfusion needs a pump, a volume and a vascular tree with tone; shock follows when the heart fails (cardiogenic), the volume is lost (hypovolaemic, as in haemorrhage), the vessels dilate and leak (distributive, chiefly septic) or flow is blocked (obstructive). The body compensates with sympathetic drive and fluid shifts until it cannot; then organs turn anaerobic, and a point arrives after which restoring blood pressure no longer restores life. Sepsis is this process started by infection and driven by the host's own mediators rather than by the microbe.

## Core tenets

1. **Shock is inadequate perfusion, not low blood pressure.** Oxygen delivery is cardiac output times arterial oxygen content; cardiac output is set by preload, contractility, afterload and rate. Any state in which delivery falls below demand is shock, even with a normal blood pressure held up by vasoconstriction. The biochemical sign is lactate: cells denied oxygen run glycolysis without mitochondria, and a lactate above about 2 mmol/L with a cause for low flow means tissues are already hypoxic.

2. **The four types of shock differ in which term of the delivery equation fails, and the body's response differs with them.** Hypovolaemic and cardiogenic shock have low output and high resistance: the patient is cold, pale and clammy as vasoconstriction diverts blood to brain and heart. Distributive shock (sepsis, anaphylaxis, spinal cord injury) has low resistance, often with high output: the patient may be warm and flushed while tissues starve, because flow is shunted past a disorganised microcirculation. Obstructive shock has a mechanical block to filling or ejection and is cured by removing it.

   | Type | Failing term | Resistance | Classic cause |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Hypovolaemic | Preload (volume) | High | Haemorrhage |
   | Cardiogenic | Contractility | High | Myocardial infarction |
   | Distributive | Vascular tone | Low | Sepsis, anaphylaxis |
   | Obstructive | Filling or outflow | High | Pulmonary embolism, tamponade |

3. **Compensation hides haemorrhage until a third of the blood is gone.** Baroreceptors drive tachycardia and vasoconstriction; fluid moves from interstitium into plasma; the kidney retains sodium and water. A healthy adult losing 15% of blood volume (about 750 mL) shows little; at 30–40% blood pressure finally falls. Pressure is therefore a late sign; heart rate, skin perfusion, urine output and mental state fall earlier. The young compensate best and collapse most suddenly; age and beta-blockers blunt the response.

4. **Shock progresses through reversible, progressive and irreversible stages, and the transition is cellular.** At first vasoconstriction holds vital-organ perfusion. As hypoxia continues, lactic acidosis dilates arterioles, blood pools in capillaries, the endothelium becomes leaky and prothrombotic, and ischaemic gut lets endotoxin into the portal blood. Finally lysosomal enzymes leak, mitochondria fail, the myocardium is depressed and microthrombi consume clotting factors (disseminated intravascular coagulation). After this point restoring volume and pressure does not restore function: the organs have passed from ischaemia to [necrosis](../01-cell-and-tissue-injury/cell-injury-adaptation-and-death.md).

5. **Sepsis is a dysregulated host response to infection; the microbe starts it but the mediators drive it.** Pattern-recognition receptors on macrophages and endothelium bind microbial products (endotoxin of Gram-negative bacteria, lipoteichoic acid of Gram-positive) and release tumour necrosis factor, interleukin-1 and interleukin-6 into the circulation. Acting on every vessel at once they cause nitric-oxide-mediated vasodilatation, capillary leak and tissue-factor-driven coagulation: the local programme of [acute inflammation](../01-cell-and-tissue-injury/acute-inflammation.md) applied to the whole body. Massive sterile injury (pancreatitis, burns) produces the same picture, which is why the response rather than the organism defines the syndrome.

6. **Organ failure in sepsis is partly a failure of cells to use oxygen, not only to receive it.** Microthrombosis, shunting and oedema reduce delivery, but mitochondria exposed to nitric oxide and cytokines also stop using the oxygen that arrives, so venous oxygen saturation can be high while lactate climbs. Failing organs show little necrosis at autopsy, suggesting a reversible metabolic shutdown, which is why survivors can recover full kidney and liver function. Lung, kidney, brain and heart fail in sequence or together, and mortality rises with the number of organs involved.

7. **The immune response to sepsis overshoots and then collapses.** The early hyperinflammatory phase gives way within days to immunosuppression: lymphocyte apoptosis, exhausted T cells, monocytes that no longer respond to endotoxin. Many late deaths are secondary infections in this state. Blocking single cytokines has mostly failed in trials because the syndrome is neither one mediator nor one direction; treatment remains source control, antimicrobials and support of perfusion.

8. **Shock injures organs in proportion to their dependence on flow and their reserve.** The kidney loses filtration first and develops acute tubular injury; the gut mucosa sloughs; the liver necroses around its least-oxygenated central veins; the brain's watershed zones between arterial territories infarct; the heart suffers subendocardial necrosis. These patterns are the autopsy evidence of what perfusion failed to reach.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Oxygen delivery | Cardiac output × arterial oxygen content; the quantity shock reduces below demand |
| Lactate | Product of anaerobic glycolysis; the biochemical marker of tissue hypoperfusion |
| Distributive shock | Shock from loss of vascular tone with maldistributed flow; septic, anaphylactic, neurogenic |
| Endotoxin | Lipopolysaccharide of the Gram-negative outer membrane, a potent trigger of the septic cascade |
| Sepsis | Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection |
| Cytopathic hypoxia | Failure of mitochondria to use delivered oxygen, seen in sepsis |
| Disseminated intravascular coagulation | Systemic activation of clotting with microthrombi, consumption of factors and bleeding |
| Multi-organ failure | Sequential or simultaneous failure of two or more organ systems after shock or sepsis |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Normal blood pressure rules out shock."** Vasoconstriction holds pressure until a third of the volume is lost; lactate, urine output and skin perfusion fall first.
- **"Sepsis is a severe infection."** It is a dysregulated host response; the same cascade follows sterile injury, and killing the microbe does not by itself switch it off.
- **"Organ failure in sepsis means the organs are dead."** Autopsies show surprisingly little necrosis; much of the dysfunction is metabolic and reversible, which is why support rather than replacement is the strategy.
