## Essence

Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease of large and medium arteries in which cholesterol-carrying lipoprotein enters the wall, is modified, and provokes a macrophage-rich lesion (the atheroma or plaque) that grows for decades. The plaque harms in two ways: slowly, by narrowing the lumen until demand outruns supply (angina, claudication), and suddenly, by rupturing and triggering a thrombus that blocks the artery within minutes (myocardial infarction, most strokes). It is the largest single cause of death worldwide (ischaemic heart disease and stroke together, roughly a quarter of deaths in the 2020s). The one idea: a lipid that should stay in the blood gets into the wall, and the body's attempt to clear it becomes the disease.

## Core tenets

1. **Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) entering the arterial intima is the necessary cause.** LDL crosses a stressed endothelium in proportion to its plasma concentration, is trapped by proteoglycans and is oxidised. People with lifelong low LDL (below about 1.5–2 mmol/L, as in familial hypobetalipoproteinaemia) rarely develop significant atherosclerosis whatever their other risks, and every intervention that lowers LDL (statins, ezetimibe, Proprotein Convertase Subtilisin/Kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors, ileal bypass) lowers events in proportion to the fall. Risk is LDL concentration integrated over time: cumulative exposure, not today's value.

2. **The lesion is chronic inflammation around oxidised lipid.** Oxidised LDL makes endothelium display adhesion molecules and chemokines; monocytes enter, become macrophages and ingest the modified LDL through scavenger receptors that do not switch off when the cell is full. The lipid-laden foam cell is the hallmark of the fatty streak, present in most aortas by the twenties. Foam cells recruit T cells and smooth-muscle cells; dying foam cells leave a pool of extracellular cholesterol, the necrotic core. The lesion is a granuloma-like response to something the macrophage cannot digest, which is why it sits next to [chronic inflammation](../01-cell-and-tissue-injury/chronic-inflammation-and-granulomas.md) and why C-reactive protein predicts events.

3. **Haemodynamics decides where plaques form.** Plaques cluster at branch points, bifurcations and the inner curve of bends (carotid bulb, coronary ostia, aortic bifurcation), where flow is disturbed and shear is low or oscillating. Steady laminar shear induces anti-inflammatory, antithrombotic endothelial genes (nitric oxide synthase); low or oscillating shear induces adhesion molecules and makes the endothelium permeable to LDL. Straight segments are spared, and so are the low-pressure pulmonary arteries unless pulmonary hypertension develops.

4. **The other risk factors act on the endothelium or the lipid.** Hypertension raises wall stress and endothelial permeability. Smoking generates oxidants that modify LDL and activate platelets and endothelium. Diabetes glycates lipoproteins, raises triglyceride-rich particles and keeps the endothelium inflamed. High-density lipoprotein (HDL) carries cholesterol out of macrophages, so low HDL marks poor efflux, yet raising HDL by drugs has not reduced events: function, not concentration, matters. Risk factors multiply rather than add, the basis of [risk scores](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/03-screening-and-prevention/risk-prediction-and-stratification.md).

5. **The plaque's fibrous cap is the body's repair, and its thickness decides the patient's fate.** Smooth-muscle cells migrate from the media, proliferate and lay collagen over the lipid core, separating thrombogenic core from blood. A thick-capped, small-core plaque is stable: it may cause stable angina but rarely kills. A thin-capped plaque with a large core and many macrophages is vulnerable: macrophage metalloproteinases digest the cap, and when it tears, blood meets tissue factor and a thrombus forms within minutes. Most fatal plaques caused less than 50% stenosis before rupturing, which is why a normal stress test does not exclude infarction and why lipid lowering, which shrinks the core and thickens the cap, prevents events out of proportion to any change in stenosis.

6. **Clinical disease is the sum of slow narrowing and sudden thrombosis, and the artery remodels to delay it.** As a plaque grows the artery first expands outward (positive remodelling), preserving the lumen until the plaque occupies around 40% of the wall area. Flow-limiting stenosis (roughly 70% of diameter) causes symptoms when demand rises: angina on exertion, claudication on walking. Rupture or erosion with thrombosis causes the acute syndromes: myocardial infarction, unstable angina, ischaemic stroke, acute limb ischaemia. The slow and the sudden are the same lesion at different moments.

7. **The same process in different arteries produces the diseases of the specialties.** Coronary arteries: angina and infarction. Carotid and cerebral arteries: stroke. Aorta: aneurysm, as inflammation and metalloproteinases destroy the media and the wall dilates under pressure. Renal arteries: renovascular hypertension. Limb arteries: claudication and gangrene. The lesion is one; the organ at risk changes, and a patient with plaque in one bed almost always has it in the others.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Atheroma (plaque) | Intimal lesion of lipid core, inflammatory cells and a fibrous cap, in a large or medium artery |
| Low-density lipoprotein | The cholesterol-carrying particle whose entry into the intima initiates the lesion |
| Oxidised LDL | Modified LDL that endothelium and macrophages recognise as damage |
| Foam cell | Macrophage (or smooth-muscle cell) engorged with lipid via scavenger receptors |
| Necrotic core | Pool of extracellular cholesterol and dead cells beneath the cap |
| Fibrous cap | Collagen and smooth-muscle layer separating the core from the blood |
| Vulnerable plaque | Thin cap, large core, many macrophages: prone to rupture and thrombosis |
| Shear stress | Frictional force of flowing blood on endothelium; low or oscillating shear favours plaque |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Atherosclerosis is a plumbing problem of narrowing pipes."** Most infarcts arise from rupture of plaques that were not flow-limiting; the disease is inflammatory and thrombotic, not merely obstructive.
- **"Cholesterol is one of several equally important risk factors."** LDL is the necessary cause; the others modify how much LDL enters the wall and how the wall responds. Without LDL in the wall there is no plaque.
- **"It is a disease of old age."** Fatty streaks are present in childhood and plaques by the third decade; age measures cumulative exposure, which is why early LDL lowering prevents more than late.
