# Thrombosis, embolism and infarction

## Essence

Haemostasis is a plug formed at the right place and time; thrombosis is the same plug formed in the wrong place, inside an intact vessel, where it obstructs flow. Virchow's triad names the three conditions that produce it: an injured or activated endothelium, abnormal blood flow, and blood that clots too readily. A thrombus harms by blocking the vessel where it forms or by breaking off and lodging downstream (embolism). When a tissue's supply falls below its need, it dies in a wedge shaped by its vascular anatomy (infarction). These three steps are the mechanism of myocardial infarction, stroke and pulmonary embolism.

## Core tenets

1. **The endothelium is the switch between flowing blood and clot.** Healthy endothelium hides collagen and tissue factor beneath it, displays heparan sulphate and thrombomodulin that inactivate thrombin, and releases nitric oxide, prostacyclin and tissue plasminogen activator. Injured or inflamed endothelium (ruptured plaque, cytokines, toxins) exposes collagen and tissue factor, expresses adhesion molecules and releases less of everything protective. Most arterial thrombosis begins with an endothelial lesion; thrombosis in sepsis begins with endothelium activated by cytokines.

2. **Flow matters because laminar flow keeps platelets away from the wall and dilutes clotting factors.** In laminar flow cells travel in the axial stream, and activated factors are swept away and cleared by the liver. Turbulence (a bifurcation, a stenosis, an aneurysm) and stasis (atrial fibrillation, an immobile leg, a dilated ventricle) bring platelets to the wall, let activated factors accumulate and deprive the endothelium of the shear that keeps it antithrombotic. Stasis dominates venous thrombosis, turbulence arterial.

3. **Hypercoagulability is a shift in the balance between clotting and its inhibitors.** Inherited causes are loss of an anticoagulant brake (antithrombin, protein C or S deficiency) or a factor resistant to that brake (factor V Leiden, carried by roughly 2–7% of Europeans, with a several-fold rise in venous thrombosis risk). Acquired causes are commoner: cancer (tissue factor from tumour cells), surgery and trauma, pregnancy and oestrogen, immobility, antiphospholipid antibodies. Risks multiply: an oral contraceptive in a factor V Leiden carrier raises risk far more than either alone.

4. **Arterial and venous thrombi are different in composition, cause and treatment.** An arterial thrombus forms under high shear on a ruptured plaque and is platelet-rich and pale ("white"); the drugs that prevent it are antiplatelet agents. A venous thrombus forms in slow flow, grows by coagulation around trapped red cells and is fibrin-rich and dark ("red"); the drugs that prevent it are anticoagulants. Thrombi in the heart (on an infarcted wall, in a fibrillating atrium) behave like venous thrombi but embolise to the arterial tree. A thrombus may dissolve, be organised into the wall and recanalised, propagate, or break off.

5. **An embolus goes where the flow takes it, so its origin predicts its destination.** Most emboli are fragments of thrombus. From the leg veins they lodge in the pulmonary arteries; a large one across the bifurcation stops right ventricular output and kills suddenly, smaller ones cause breathlessness, pleuritic pain or a pulmonary infarct. From the left heart or a carotid plaque they lodge in the brain (most stroke is thrombotic or embolic), kidney, spleen or limbs. Fat (fractured long bones), air, amniotic fluid and tumour fragments embolise by the same rule. A venous embolus reaches the arterial tree only through a hole between the circulations (paradoxical embolism through a patent foramen ovale).

6. **Infarction is ischaemic necrosis of a territory, and its shape, colour and timing follow the vascular anatomy.** An end-artery makes a wedge of coagulative necrosis with its apex at the occlusion, pale in solid organs (heart, kidney, spleen). In tissues with a dual or loose supply (lung, bowel) or after venous occlusion (testicular torsion), blood seeps into the dead tissue and the infarct is red. Infarcts become visible on histology after 4–12 hours, are inflamed at the rim by day 2–3, and are replaced by granulation tissue and scar over weeks; in the brain the tissue liquefies and leaves a cyst. Troponin in the blood and the evolving electrocardiogram are the clinical readout.

7. **Whether occlusion causes infarction depends on collaterals, tempo, tissue and oxygen supply.** A slowly closing coronary artery recruits collaterals so its final occlusion may cause little damage; sudden occlusion of a normal vessel infarcts everything downstream. Neurons die after minutes, myocardium after 20–40 minutes, fibroblasts after hours. Anaemia, hypoxaemia and low cardiac output lower the threshold, so an occlusion a healthy heart tolerates infarcts an anaemic or hypotensive one. The same lesion produces different outcomes in different patients.

8. **Thrombosis is the final common mechanism of most vascular death, which makes it the chief target of prevention.** Myocardial infarction and ischaemic stroke are thrombosis on plaque; pulmonary embolism is thrombosis in a leg vein. Because the triad can be read for any patient, prophylaxis is possible: mobilisation and anticoagulants after surgery, antiplatelet agents after plaque rupture, anticoagulation in atrial fibrillation. Each trades thrombosis against bleeding, the mirror failure of the same system.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Thrombus | A solid mass of platelets and fibrin formed from flowing blood inside the circulation during life |
| Virchow's triad | Endothelial injury, abnormal flow, hypercoagulability: the three causes of thrombosis |
| Stasis and turbulence | The two abnormal flows; stasis favours venous, turbulence arterial thrombosis |
| Factor V Leiden | Mutation making factor V resistant to activated protein C; the commonest inherited thrombophilia |
| Embolus | A detached mass (thrombus, fat, air, tumour, amniotic fluid) carried by blood to a distant site |
| Paradoxical embolism | A venous embolus reaching the arterial tree through a right-to-left shunt |
| Infarct | An area of ischaemic necrosis caused by occlusion of its arterial supply or venous drainage |
| End-artery | A vessel whose territory has no collateral supply, so occlusion always infarcts |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A clot is a clot."** Arterial thrombi are platelet-driven and prevented by antiplatelet drugs; venous thrombi are coagulation-driven and prevented by anticoagulants. The wrong drug for the wrong vessel fails.
- **"Occlusion of an artery always causes an infarct."** Collateral supply, the speed of occlusion and the tissue's tolerance decide; the liver and lung often survive arterial occlusion because they have a second supply.
- **"Pulmonary embolism is a lung disease."** It is a leg-vein disease whose embolus happened to lodge in the lung; prevention and the search for its cause are directed at the veins and the triad.
