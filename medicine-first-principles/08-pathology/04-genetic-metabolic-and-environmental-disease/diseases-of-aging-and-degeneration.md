## Essence

Aging is the progressive loss of the capacity to maintain and repair, and it is the largest risk factor for most of the diseases in this knowledge base: cardiovascular disease, cancer, dementia, osteoporosis, osteoarthritis and type 2 diabetes all rise steeply after 60. The underlying biology is a set of interlinked processes (genomic damage, telomere attrition, epigenetic drift, loss of proteostasis, mitochondrial decline, cellular senescence, stem-cell exhaustion and chronic low-grade inflammation) that selection never tuned because they act after reproduction. Degeneration is the organ-level result: tissue lost faster than it is replaced, often with a misfolded protein as the visible lesion. Aging sets the baseline on which every other disease mechanism acts, and reserve, not age itself, decides how the old patient fares.

## Core tenets

1. **Aging exists because selection weakens with age.** Mutations that harm late in life are barely selected against, and alleles that help early reproduction at a late cost (antagonistic pleiotropy) spread. Growth and repair pathways that benefit the young (insulin and insulin-like growth factor signalling, mechanistic target of rapamycin) accelerate aging when they remain active, and damping them (caloric restriction, rapamycin, mutations in the pathway) extends lifespan in every organism tested. There is no programme for death; there is an absence of maintenance where it no longer pays. This is why aging is universal, why its rate varies between species in proportion to their extrinsic mortality, and why it is plausibly modifiable.

2. **Damage accumulates in the genome, the epigenome and the proteome.** Somatic mutations accumulate linearly with age in every tissue, so that by 60 each blood stem cell carries hundreds and a few clones have expanded (clonal haematopoiesis, which raises risk of leukaemia and, through inflammatory mutant monocytes, of atherosclerosis). Methylation patterns drift, and "epigenetic clocks" built from them predict mortality better than chronological age. Proteins misfold and are no longer cleared: the proteasome, chaperones and autophagy all decline, and aggregates of amyloid-beta, tau, alpha-synuclein, transthyretin and others accumulate in brain, heart and vessels. Each neurodegenerative disease is named by the protein that aggregates in it.

3. **Senescent cells accumulate and poison their surroundings.** Cells with critically short telomeres, damaged DNA or oncogenic stress arrest permanently, which protects against cancer, but they do not die: they secrete inflammatory cytokines, proteases and growth factors (the senescence-associated secretory phenotype) that disrupt tissue structure, drive the low-grade inflammation of age ("inflammaging"), and promote the growth of neighbouring pre-malignant cells. Clearing senescent cells in mice delays several age-related diseases at once. The tumour-suppressive brake of youth becomes a source of damage in age, a trade-off running through the whole biology of aging.

4. **Reserve declines in every organ, and failure appears when reserve meets a load.** Renal filtration falls about 1 mL/min per year after 40, so an 80-year-old has half the clearance for drugs; cardiac output at rest is maintained but maximal rate and compliance fall; vital capacity, bone mass, muscle mass (sarcopenia), immune repertoire and the number of neurons and nephrons all decline. None of these alone causes disease. A young person tolerates an infection, an operation or a new drug because reserve absorbs it; the old person, with several reserves near the edge, decompensates: delirium from a urinary infection, acute kidney injury from a diuretic, a fall from a sedative. This is the mechanism of frailty, and why geriatric medicine treats the loss of reserve rather than any one disease.

5. **Degeneration is tissue lost faster than it is replaced, in tissues that cannot regenerate.** Osteoarthritis is cartilage worn and remodelled by chondrocytes that cannot keep up, with subchondral bone and synovium responding; osteoporosis is bone resorbed faster than it is formed as oestrogen falls and osteoblasts age, until a vertebra or hip breaks under ordinary load. Lens proteins cross-link into cataract; the macula's pigment epithelium fails under a lifetime of oxidative load; hair cells and retinal cells are lost and not replaced; arterial elastin fragments and collagen cross-links, so vessels stiffen and systolic pressure rises. The pattern is the [repair](../01-cell-and-tissue-injury/tissue-repair-regeneration-and-fibrosis.md) leaf's limitation, permanent tissue without regenerative capacity, played out over decades.

6. **Neurodegeneration is protein aggregation spreading through a network.** Alzheimer disease shows extracellular amyloid-beta plaques and intracellular tau tangles; Parkinson disease, alpha-synuclein in Lewy bodies; frontotemporal dementia and motor neuron disease, tau, TDP-43 or others; prion disease, a misfolded form of the prion protein that templates normal copies. In each, a misfolded protein seeds further misfolding, spreads along anatomical connections, and kills neurons in a predictable order that maps onto the clinical progression (memory first in Alzheimer, movement first in Parkinson). Rare inherited forms with mutations in the aggregating protein or its processing (amyloid precursor protein, presenilins, SNCA) prove the protein is causal; the common sporadic forms add age, vascular disease and risk alleles such as APOE ε4. Dementia affects about 55 million people worldwide (2020s) and its prevalence doubles roughly every five years after 65.

7. **Aging is modifiable, and the evidence is behavioural before it is pharmacological.** The known hallmarks are coupled: exercise improves mitochondrial function, insulin sensitivity, muscle mass and brain perfusion together; not smoking, blood-pressure control and education reduce dementia incidence, which has fallen in several high-income countries per age group even as absolute numbers rise. Cellular interventions (senolytics, metformin, rapamycin analogues, partial epigenetic reprogramming) extend health in animals and are in human trials. The target is healthspan, the years lived without disability; the measure of success is compression of morbidity, not extension of life.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Antagonistic pleiotropy | An allele beneficial early in life and harmful late, favoured by selection |
| Hallmarks of aging | Interlinked processes: genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alteration, loss of proteostasis, mitochondrial dysfunction, senescence, stem-cell exhaustion, altered signalling, inflammation |
| Cellular senescence | Permanent cell-cycle arrest with a pro-inflammatory secretory phenotype |
| Inflammaging | Chronic low-grade inflammation of age, driven by senescent cells, visceral fat and clonal haematopoiesis |
| Clonal haematopoiesis | Expansion of mutant blood stem-cell clones with age, raising leukaemia and vascular risk |
| Proteostasis | Maintenance of correctly folded proteins by chaperones, proteasome and autophagy |
| Sarcopenia | Age-related loss of muscle mass and strength |
| Physiological reserve | Capacity beyond resting need that absorbs stress; its loss defines frailty |
| Proteinopathy | A degenerative disease defined by its aggregating protein (amyloid, tau, synuclein, prion) |
| Healthspan | Years lived free of major disease and disability |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Aging is a disease."** It is the substrate on which diseases act; a 90-year-old with no disease still has reduced reserve, and treating aging processes would delay many diseases at once rather than cure one.
- **"Degeneration is wear and tear."** Tissues are actively maintained; degeneration is failure of maintenance and clearance (of misfolded protein, senescent cells, damaged matrix), not passive erosion.
- **"Dementia is normal aging."** Most old people are not demented; dementia is specific proteinopathy and vascular disease, and its age-specific incidence can fall.
