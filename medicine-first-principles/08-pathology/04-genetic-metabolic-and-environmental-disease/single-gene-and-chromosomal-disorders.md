## Essence

Genetic disease spans a scale from one changed base to a whole extra chromosome. At the small end, a mutation in a single gene alters one protein and the phenotype follows from what that protein did: an enzyme missing (phenylketonuria), a structural protein weakened (Marfan syndrome), a receptor absent (familial hypercholesterolaemia), a channel broken (cystic fibrosis). At the large end, a missing or extra chromosome changes the dose of hundreds of genes at once and produces a syndrome of many organs. The link is dosage: whether a mutation hurts depends on how much working protein remains and how sensitive the body is to that amount, which explains dominance, recessiveness, variable severity and why most aneuploidies are lethal before birth.

## Core tenets

1. **The inheritance pattern follows from how much protein is enough.** Recessive disorders are mostly enzyme deficiencies: half the normal enzyme activity suffices, so heterozygotes are well, and homozygotes with little or none are affected (phenylketonuria, sickle cell disease, cystic fibrosis, most inborn errors). Dominant disorders are mostly structural proteins, receptors and regulators, where half is not enough (haploinsufficiency: familial hypercholesterolaemia, with one working LDL-receptor allele giving double the cholesterol) or where the mutant protein poisons the normal one in a multimer (dominant negative: a mutant collagen chain ruining the triple helix in osteogenesis imperfecta). X-linked recessive disorders (Duchenne muscular dystrophy, haemophilia A) affect males, who have one X; carrier females are protected by random X inactivation.

2. **The type of mutation predicts the severity.** A missense change may leave partial function; a nonsense or frameshift mutation, a splice-site change or a large deletion usually abolishes it. The same gene therefore produces a spectrum: in the dystrophin gene, frame-shifting deletions give Duchenne muscular dystrophy with no protein and death in early adulthood, while in-frame deletions give a shortened, partly working protein and the milder Becker form. Cystic fibrosis runs from early lung disease to male infertility alone, according to how much chloride channel reaches the membrane.

3. **Expanding repeats, imprinting and mitochondria break Mendel's rules in predictable ways.** Trinucleotide repeat disorders (Huntington disease, fragile X syndrome, myotonic dystrophy) arise when a repeat tract lengthens during transmission; longer tracts cause earlier, severer disease, so the condition worsens through generations (anticipation). Imprinted regions are expressed from one parental allele only, so deleting the same segment of chromosome 15 gives Prader–Willi syndrome if paternal and Angelman syndrome if maternal. Mitochondrial Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) is inherited only from the mother and distributed unevenly at division, so mitochondrial diseases are maternally transmitted, variable between tissues, and hit energy-hungry muscle, brain and retina.

4. **Penetrance and expressivity are not noise; they are the rest of the genome and the environment.** Incomplete penetrance (a carrier with no disease) and variable expressivity (carriers with different severity) arise from modifier genes, somatic second hits, X-inactivation skew, and exposures. Most homozygotes for the common haemochromatosis mutation never develop iron overload. Knowing the mutation gives a probability, not a prognosis.

5. **Chromosomal disorders are diseases of gene dosage, and most are lethal.** About half of first-trimester miscarriages are chromosomally abnormal. Monosomy of any autosome and trisomy of most are incompatible with life; the viable autosomal trisomies (21, and the rarer 18 and 13) involve the chromosomes with the fewest genes. Trisomy 21 (Down syndrome, about 1 in 700 births, rising steeply with maternal age because non-disjunction in the aging oocyte is the usual cause) illustrates the pattern: a 50% increase in dose of some 200 genes yields intellectual disability, heart defects, early Alzheimer disease (the amyloid precursor protein gene is on chromosome 21) and leukaemia risk. Sex chromosome aneuploidies (Turner 45,X; Klinefelter 47,XXY) are milder because X inactivation and the gene-poor Y buffer the dose.

6. **Structural rearrangements are read by their effect on dose and position.** Deletions and duplications remove or add gene copies; microdeletions too small to see on a karyotype (22q11.2 deletion syndrome, with heart, palate, thymus and parathyroid defects) are detected by array techniques and account for much previously unexplained intellectual disability. Balanced translocations move DNA without changing dose, so the carrier is normal but may have unbalanced gametes, giving recurrent miscarriage or an affected child. A translocation can also break or fuse a gene, which is how [oncogenes](../03-neoplasia/oncogenes-tumour-suppressors-and-genomic-instability.md) such as BCR-ABL are made in somatic cells.

7. **Genetic disease is common in aggregate and increasingly treatable by mechanism.** Individually rare, single-gene disorders together affect about 1% of births, and chromosomal disorders a further 0.5%; carriers of recessive alleles are everywhere (roughly 1 in 25 Northern Europeans carries a cystic fibrosis mutation). Treatment follows the mechanism: supply the missing product (enzyme replacement, factor VIII), restrict the substrate (phenylalanine-free diet), correct the protein's folding (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator modulators), silence a toxic allele (antisense oligonucleotides), or replace the gene (gene therapy for spinal muscular atrophy and some haemoglobinopathies).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Haploinsufficiency | Disease because one working copy produces too little protein; a mechanism of dominance |
| Dominant negative | A mutant protein that disables the normal protein it assembles with |
| Penetrance and expressivity | Probability a genotype produces the phenotype, and how severely |
| Anticipation | Earlier, severer disease in successive generations, from repeat expansion |
| Imprinting | Parent-of-origin-specific gene expression, so the same deletion gives different syndromes |
| Heteroplasmy | Mixture of normal and mutant mitochondrial DNA within a cell |
| Aneuploidy | Abnormal chromosome number; trisomy 21 is the commonest viable autosomal form |
| Non-disjunction | Failure of chromosomes to separate in meiosis, the usual cause of aneuploidy |
| Microdeletion | Loss of DNA too small for a karyotype, detected by array or sequencing |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Dominant means severe, recessive means mild."** Dominance is about dosage sensitivity, not severity; many recessive enzyme deficiencies are lethal in infancy and some dominant conditions are trivial.
- **"Genetic disease is present at birth."** Huntington disease, many cancer syndromes and hereditary haemochromatosis present in adult life; conversely most congenital disease is not single-gene.
- **"Knowing the mutation tells you the outcome."** Penetrance and expressivity make it a probability; two siblings with the same mutation can differ widely.
