## Essence

Nutrition fails in two directions. Too little energy or protein produces the wasting and stunting of undernutrition, still a cause of nearly half of deaths in children under five in low-income settings; too little of a single micronutrient produces a specific deficiency disease because one enzyme or structural process stops. Too much energy, stored as fat, produces obesity, which in the 2020s affects over a billion people and drives type 2 diabetes, atherosclerosis, fatty liver, several cancers and osteoarthritis. The unifying idea is that the body evolved to defend against famine, not plenty: energy balance is regulated asymmetrically, fat tissue in excess becomes an inflamed endocrine organ, and the diseases of affluence are the metabolic machinery of survival running in an environment it was not built for.

## Core tenets

1. **Energy balance is defended by a feedback system biased towards storing.** Fat mass signals the hypothalamus through leptin; the gut signals meals through ghrelin, glucagon-like peptide 1 and peptide YY; the hypothalamus adjusts hunger and, less, expenditure. The system resists weight loss strongly (leptin falls, hunger rises, metabolic rate drops below what the new weight predicts) but resists weight gain weakly, and in obesity leptin is high yet ineffective (leptin resistance). This asymmetry, adaptive when famine was the threat, is why weight lost by dieting is usually regained and why drugs that mimic gut satiety hormones (glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists) produce sustained loss where willpower does not.

2. **Obesity is heritable in its susceptibility and environmental in its expression.** Twin studies give body mass index a heritability of 40–70%, acting through hundreds of common variants mostly expressed in the brain's appetite circuits, and rare single-gene forms (leptin, leptin receptor, MC4R mutations) prove the pathway. Yet prevalence has tripled since 1975 while genes have not changed: the environment of cheap energy-dense food, large portions and little required movement has exposed a susceptibility that was once harmless. Obesity is thus a [multifactorial disease](../05-disease-as-process/multifactorial-disease-and-risk.md) in the strict sense, and social gradients in its prevalence reflect gradients in that environment.

3. **Excess fat harms through where it is stored and what it secretes.** Subcutaneous fat is a relatively safe store; visceral and ectopic fat (in liver, muscle, pancreas, around the heart) is not. Overfilled adipocytes become hypoxic, die and attract macrophages, which release tumour necrosis factor and interleukin-6; adipokine balance shifts (adiponectin falls); free fatty acids spill into liver and muscle, where they impair insulin signalling. The result is insulin resistance, dyslipidaemia (high triglyceride, low HDL, small dense LDL), hypertension and a prothrombotic state: the metabolic syndrome, and the path to type 2 diabetes, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease and accelerated [atherosclerosis](../02-hemodynamic-disorders/atherosclerosis.md). Low-grade inflammation from fat is a major reason chronic inflammation now sits among the causes of common disease.

4. **Obesity's other harms are mechanical, hormonal and oncological.** Weight loads joints (osteoarthritis of knee and hip), narrows the pharynx in sleep (obstructive sleep apnoea), raises intra-abdominal pressure (reflux, hernias) and restricts the chest. Adipose aromatase converts androgens to oestrogen, which with hyperinsulinaemia promotes endometrial and post-menopausal breast cancer; insulin and insulin-like growth factor 1 act as growth factors in colon, pancreas, kidney and liver. Obesity is attributed around 4–8% of cancers in high-income countries, second to tobacco among modifiable causes.

5. **Protein-energy undernutrition presents as wasting or as oedema, and the two differ in mechanism.** Marasmus is adaptation to deficit: fat and muscle are consumed, growth stops, the child is thin but alert and the serum albumin is relatively preserved. Kwashiorkor is failed adaptation, usually with protein deficiency and infection: albumin falls, oedema and a fatty liver appear, skin and hair change, and apathy and immune failure follow. Both impair immunity, so measles, diarrhoea and pneumonia kill the malnourished child; stunting in the first two years leaves permanent deficits in height and cognition. In hospitals, undernutrition in the ill and elderly impairs wound healing and immune function and lengthens stays, and is routinely missed.

6. **Refeeding the starved is dangerous because the body has switched fuels.** Starvation lowers insulin, depletes intracellular phosphate, potassium and magnesium while serum values stay normal, and shrinks the heart. Sudden carbohydrate raises insulin, drives these ions into cells, and demands phosphate for ATP and thiamine for glycolysis: the refeeding syndrome of hypophosphataemia, arrhythmia, heart failure and Wernicke encephalopathy. Nutrition therapy therefore begins slowly with electrolyte and thiamine replacement, a direct application of the fed–fasted biochemistry.

7. **Micronutrient deficiencies are inborn errors of metabolism acquired from the diet.** Each vitamin or mineral is a cofactor or structural component, and its deficiency stops a specific process: vitamin C, collagen hydroxylation (scurvy: bleeding gums, poor healing); thiamine, pyruvate dehydrogenase and the pentose pathway (beriberi and Wernicke encephalopathy, classically in alcohol dependence); vitamin D, calcium absorption (rickets, osteomalacia); vitamin A, retinal pigment and epithelial differentiation (night blindness, xerophthalmia, susceptibility to measles); iron, haemoglobin (the commonest deficiency in the world, about 1.2 billion people anaemic); iodine, thyroid hormone (goitre and congenital hypothyroidism, prevented by salt iodisation); folate, DNA synthesis (megaloblastic anaemia, neural tube defects); vitamin B12, methylation and myelin (anaemia with neuropathy). Deficiency arises from poor intake, malabsorption, increased need (pregnancy, growth) or loss, and fortification (iodine, folate, vitamin D) is among the cheapest interventions in public health.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Body mass index | Weight divided by height squared (kg/m²); obesity conventionally ≥30, with population-specific limits |
| Leptin | Adipocyte hormone signalling fat mass to the hypothalamus; high but ineffective in obesity |
| Visceral and ectopic fat | Fat around abdominal organs and inside liver, muscle and pancreas; the metabolically harmful depots |
| Adipokines | Hormones secreted by fat (leptin, adiponectin, resistin) and the cytokines of inflamed fat |
| Insulin resistance | Reduced tissue response to insulin, from lipid overload and inflammation |
| Metabolic syndrome | Cluster of central obesity, insulin resistance, dyslipidaemia and hypertension |
| Marasmus and kwashiorkor | Wasting from energy deficit versus oedematous malnutrition with protein deficiency and infection |
| Stunting | Low height for age from chronic early undernutrition; largely irreversible after age two |
| Refeeding syndrome | Electrolyte and thiamine crisis when the starved are fed too fast |
| Fortification | Adding a micronutrient to a staple food for a whole population |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Obesity is a failure of willpower."** Appetite is regulated by a hypothalamic system with strong genetic variation and a bias to defend fat mass; the environment, not the character, changed between 1975 and today.
- **"Fat is inert storage."** Adipose tissue is an endocrine and immune organ; where it is and how inflamed it is determine harm more than how much there is.
- **"A starving patient needs feeding fast."** Rapid refeeding kills by hypophosphataemia and thiamine deficiency; the first days are for electrolytes and slow calories.
