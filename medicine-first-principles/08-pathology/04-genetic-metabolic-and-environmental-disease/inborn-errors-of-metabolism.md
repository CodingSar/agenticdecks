## Essence

An inborn error of metabolism is an inherited deficiency of one enzyme or transporter in a metabolic pathway. Garrod coined the term in 1908 from alkaptonuria, the first disease traced to a single chemical step. Each of the several hundred known errors is rare, but together they affect perhaps 1 in 2,000 births, and they are the clearest demonstration of mechanism in medicine: block one step and the consequences follow from chemistry. Substrate accumulates upstream and may be toxic; product is missing downstream; the substrate may be shunted into side pathways whose products do harm; or an undegradable molecule piles up inside the organelle that should have digested it. Reading the pathway predicts the symptoms, the test and the treatment.

## Core tenets

1. **Four consequences of a block explain every presentation.** Consider pathway A → B → C with enzyme B→C missing. **Accumulation of B** is toxic in phenylketonuria (phenylalanine damages the developing brain) and in the urea cycle disorders (ammonia). **Deficiency of C** matters when C is essential and cannot be obtained from food: cortisol in congenital adrenal hyperplasia, glucose from glycogen in glycogen storage disease type I. **Diversion of B** into alternative products causes harm in the organic acidaemias and in the tyrosine-derived pigment of alkaptonuria. **Storage** occurs when the missing enzyme is lysosomal and its substrate is a macromolecule: it fills the lysosome, swells the cell and destroys the organ (Gaucher, Tay–Sachs, the mucopolysaccharidoses). Most disorders combine two of these.

2. **The pattern of inheritance is recessive because enzymes have reserve.** Half-normal enzyme activity is almost always enough to keep flux through a pathway near normal, because enzymes are rarely rate-limiting at their usual substrate concentration. Heterozygotes are healthy carriers and the disorders appear in homozygotes, often in consanguineous families or founder populations. X-linked exceptions (ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency, Fabry disease, Hunter syndrome) and the mitochondrially inherited respiratory chain defects follow their own rules.

3. **Timing of presentation follows the physiology of the substrate.** Disorders of small-molecule catabolism (amino acids, organic acids, ammonia) present within days of birth, because the placenta cleared the metabolite before delivery and the newborn's protein intake begins the load; the picture is poor feeding, vomiting, lethargy and encephalopathy, easily mistaken for sepsis. Disorders of energy production (fatty-acid oxidation, glycogen storage, some mitochondrial disease) present when fasting or illness demands the blocked pathway: hypoglycaemia and collapse during an intercurrent infection. Lysosomal storage disorders present over months to years as the substrate accumulates: regression of milestones, enlarged liver and spleen, coarse features, bone disease. The tempo is the first diagnostic clue.

4. **The laboratory sees the chemistry directly, which is why newborn screening works.** An accumulated metabolite in blood or urine is a specific marker: phenylalanine for phenylketonuria, acylcarnitines for fatty-acid oxidation defects, organic acids for the organic acidaemias. Tandem mass spectrometry measures dozens of them from one dried blood spot a few days after birth, and because early treatment prevents irreversible damage the conditions meet the criteria for screening. Phenylketonuria, once a common cause of profound intellectual disability, now produces children of normal intelligence when a low-phenylalanine diet begins in the first weeks.

5. **Treatment follows the four consequences in reverse.** Restrict the substrate (phenylalanine, galactose, protein); supply the missing product (glucose at night in glycogen storage disease, cortisol in congenital adrenal hyperplasia, biotin or cobalamin when the cofactor is the problem); remove the toxic metabolite (sodium benzoate and phenylbutyrate to excrete nitrogen in urea cycle disorders, dialysis in a crisis); block an upstream step so a less toxic metabolite accumulates instead (nitisinone in tyrosinaemia type I); replace the enzyme (intravenous recombinant enzyme in Gaucher, Fabry and Pompe disease, which reaches liver and spleen but not brain); or replace the gene's home (liver transplantation, haematopoietic stem cell transplantation for some storage disorders).

6. **Inborn errors reveal normal physiology and explain common disease.** Familial hypercholesterolaemia showed how cholesterol is cleared and gave the statins their rationale. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency, affecting some 400 million people, explains oxidant-induced haemolysis from drugs and fava beans and persists because it protects against malaria. Lactase non-persistence, haemochromatosis and the pharmacogenetic variants that govern drug metabolism are inborn differences on a continuum with disease. Many late-onset mitochondrial and lysosomal variants contribute to [neurodegeneration](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/neurodegenerative-disease.md): GBA mutations, which cause Gaucher disease when homozygous, raise Parkinson disease risk when heterozygous.

7. **Organelle disorders are inborn errors of a compartment rather than a single reaction.** Lysosomal diseases fail to degrade; peroxisomal diseases (Zellweger spectrum, X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy) fail to oxidise very-long-chain fatty acids or to make plasmalogens; mitochondrial diseases fail to make Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP), so they hit tissues in proportion to their energy demand, and because the respiratory chain is encoded by both genomes they may be inherited in any pattern.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Inborn error of metabolism | An inherited deficiency of an enzyme or transporter in a metabolic pathway |
| Substrate accumulation | Toxicity from the metabolite upstream of the block |
| Product deficiency | Harm from the missing downstream metabolite |
| Storage disorder | Accumulation of undegraded macromolecule in lysosomes |
| Metabolic decompensation | Acute crisis when fasting or illness overwhelms a partially blocked pathway |
| Newborn screening | Population testing of dried blood spots by tandem mass spectrometry |
| Enzyme replacement therapy | Intravenous recombinant enzyme taken up by cells via receptor-mediated endocytosis |
| Substrate reduction | Dietary or pharmacological limitation of the compound upstream of the block |
| Heterozygote advantage | Selection maintaining a harmful recessive allele because carriers gain (as against malaria) |

## Common misconceptions

- **"They are too rare to matter."** Collectively about 1 in 2,000 births, they are a common cause of unexplained neonatal collapse, and every screening programme and much of biochemistry's clinical relevance rest on them.
- **"Enzyme replacement cures storage disease."** Intravenous enzyme does not cross the blood–brain barrier, so neurological disease progresses; it treats the visceral components.
- **"A normal newborn screen excludes metabolic disease."** Screening covers a few dozen conditions; many present later or are not screened, and a child with episodic encephalopathy or hypoglycaemia still needs metabolic investigation.
