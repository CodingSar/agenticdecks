## Essence

The published literature is not a random sample of the research that was done. Studies with positive, striking results are more likely to be completed, submitted, accepted, published quickly and cited; studies that find nothing are more likely to vanish. Within a study, the analysis that reached significance is more likely to be reported than the ten that did not. The result is a literature that systematically overstates how well treatments work, and a replication record in which a large share of celebrated findings cannot be reproduced. Research integrity is the set of practices (registration, full reporting, data sharing, management of conflicts of interest, and the ethics of experimenting on people) that make the literature trustworthy, and the single idea behind them is that a result is only evidence if you also know what was tried and not reported.

## Core tenets

1. **Publication bias is selection bias at the level of studies.** Whether a study is published depends on its result. Follow-up of trials registered with ethics committees shows positive studies published at roughly twice the rate of negative ones, and sooner. A systematic review that pools only published trials therefore inherits a selection that no statistical adjustment can undo. The literature on antidepressants is the canonical case: of trials submitted to the United States regulator, nearly all positive ones were published and most negative ones were not or were published as positive.

2. **Selective reporting within a study is as damaging as non-publication of the whole.** Trials measure many outcomes and analyse them many ways. Comparison of protocols with publications shows primary outcomes switched, outcomes dropped and analyses changed after the data were seen, almost always toward significance. The same freedom in observational research (choice of covariates, cut-offs, subgroups) produces the "garden of forking paths": each choice is defensible, and the combination guarantees a finding.

3. **Low power and flexible analysis together predict that most published positive findings in a field are false.** If a field tests many hypotheses of which few are true, with small studies (low power) and analytic flexibility, the proportion of significant results that reflect real effects can fall below half. This is Ioannidis's argument (2005), and it is [Bayes' theorem](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/01-probability-and-measurement/bayesian-reasoning.md) applied to the literature: a significant result from an unlikely hypothesis, tested weakly, is most likely a false positive. Fields with strong prior theory and large trials (cardiology) replicate better than fields with neither.

4. **Pre-registration and mandatory reporting convert a search for findings into a test of hypotheses.** Registering the hypothesis, primary outcome and analysis plan before data are collected removes the freedom that generates false positives; registries and results-reporting laws (trial registration required for publication since 2005; results posting required in several jurisdictions) make non-publication visible. Registered reports, in which a journal accepts a study on its protocol, remove the incentive to find something. Compliance remains incomplete, and an unregistered or unreported trial is evidence that has been withheld.

5. **Financial and intellectual conflicts bias results through design choices, not usually through fraud.** Industry-funded trials reach conclusions favourable to the sponsor more often than independently funded trials of the same drugs, and the mechanism is choice of comparator (placebo or an under-dosed rival), outcome, population, duration and analysis, each individually defensible. Disclosure makes the interest visible but does not remove its effect; independence of funding, data access and analysis does more. Intellectual investment in a hypothesis biases in the same way.

6. **Fabrication, falsification and plagiarism are rare but their damage is disproportionate.** Surveys suggest a few percent of researchers admit serious misconduct at least once; retractions are rising but remain a small fraction of papers. The damage comes from the trust placed in a single result: the fraudulent 1998 paper linking the measles vaccine to autism was retracted in 2010 and still depresses vaccination coverage. Replication, data sharing and image forensics detect fraud; the incentive structure (publication counts, grants, careers) produces it.

7. **Research ethics is the constraint that makes experiments on people permissible.** After the Nuremberg trials (1947) and the Tuskegee syphilis study (exposed 1972), the Declaration of Helsinki and national regulation established the requirements: informed consent, independent ethical review, favourable balance of risk and benefit, fair selection of participants, and equipoise, the genuine uncertainty about which arm is better that justifies randomising a patient. A trial that is unlikely to produce valid knowledge is itself unethical, because it exposes participants to risk for nothing, which binds [integrity](../../15-medicine-in-society/02-ethics-law-and-the-profession/medical-ethics.md) and methodology together.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Publication bias | Published studies are an unrepresentative sample of those conducted, favouring positive results |
| Outcome switching | Changing the reported primary outcome from the one pre-specified |
| p-hacking and the garden of forking paths | Analytic choices made after seeing data that produce significance |
| Pre-registration | Public, time-stamped statement of hypothesis, outcomes and analysis before data collection |
| Registered report | Peer review and acceptance of a study on its protocol, before results |
| Replication | Repeating a study to see whether the finding recurs; the test a single result cannot pass alone |
| Conflict of interest | An interest (financial, career, intellectual) that could bias judgement |
| Equipoise | Genuine uncertainty about which arm is better; the ethical basis of randomisation |
| Retraction | Formal withdrawal of a published paper for error or misconduct |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Peer review guarantees a published result is correct."**: reviewers see a manuscript, not the data, the protocol or the unpublished sibling studies; peer review catches poor reasoning, not selective reporting.
- **"Disclosure of conflicts removes their effect."**: it informs the reader; it does not change the design choices already made.
- **"Fraud is the main threat to the literature."**: fraud is rare; ordinary selective publication and flexible analysis by honest researchers distort far more of what is published.
