## Essence

Evidence-based medicine, as Sackett defined it in 1996, is the integration of the best available research evidence with clinical expertise and the patient's values. The phrase "best available" implies a ranking: some evidence deserves more confidence than other evidence, and a guideline must say how much. Grading systems make the ranking explicit, and the move from a naive hierarchy of study designs to a judgement about the *certainty of an estimate for a specific outcome* is the main intellectual advance of the last thirty years. A guideline then converts certainty plus the balance of benefit, harm, cost and values into a recommendation whose strength is stated separately from the quality of the evidence behind it.

## Core tenets

1. **The hierarchy of designs is a starting point, not a verdict.** The classical pyramid (systematic reviews of trials above trials, above cohort studies, above case-control studies, above case series and opinion) ranks designs by their vulnerability to confounding. It is right that a trial usually beats a cohort study on that axis. It is wrong when applied mechanically: a small, poorly concealed trial with a surrogate outcome is weaker than a large well-conducted cohort study with a hard endpoint, and some questions (rare harms, long-term effects) can only be answered observationally.

2. **Certainty is rated per outcome, and it can go down or up.** The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach starts randomised evidence at high certainty and observational evidence at low, then lowers the rating for risk of bias, inconsistency between studies, indirectness (wrong population, comparator or outcome), imprecision (wide intervals) and publication bias, and raises observational evidence for large effects, dose–response gradients, or when plausible confounding would have reduced the effect seen. The output is a judgement, documented so it can be disputed.

3. **Surrogate outcomes are indirect evidence, and they have misled before.** Blood pressure, cholesterol, tumour shrinkage and bone density are measured because the outcomes that matter (stroke, death, fracture) take years. A surrogate is valid only if the treatment's effect on it predicts its effect on the patient-important outcome. Antiarrhythmic drugs suppressed ventricular ectopy after infarction and increased mortality (the Cardiac Arrhythmia Suppression Trial, 1989); the surrogate moved the wrong way. Evidence resting on surrogates is downgraded for indirectness.

4. **Strength of recommendation is a different axis from certainty of evidence.** A strong recommendation means almost all informed patients would choose the action; a weak (conditional) one means the choice depends on values and circumstances. High-certainty evidence of a small benefit with real harms yields a weak recommendation; moderate-certainty evidence of a large benefit with no harm can yield a strong one. Conflating the two axes is how guidelines came to be read as rules.

5. **A guideline is a synthesis plus a judgement about values, and both must be visible.** A trustworthy guideline is developed by a panel that includes methodologists and patients, manages conflicts of interest, starts from systematic reviews, states the evidence certainty and the values assumed, and is updated when evidence changes. Guidelines built by consensus of experts without this machinery encode the opinions and interests of the room, and recommendations from different bodies on the same question diverge mainly in values and in which evidence was considered.

6. **Guidelines describe populations; the clinician treats one person.** Recommendations are written for the average patient in the trials; the patient with three other diseases, a short life expectancy or strong preferences was often excluded from them. Applying a guideline is therefore a step in [clinical reasoning](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/01-the-clinical-encounter/clinical-reasoning-and-cognitive-bias.md), not a substitute for it, and a guideline that cannot be departed from with a stated reason is a protocol, which is a different instrument with different uses (resuscitation, drug infusion).

7. **The evidence available depends on who paid to generate it.** Trials are expensive; most are funded by manufacturers, for drugs with patents, against placebo rather than the cheaper alternative, on outcomes chosen to show benefit. Questions about surgery, diagnostic strategies, deprescribing and old drugs have thin evidence not because the answers are known but because nobody profits from them. Grading can only rank the evidence that exists; the gaps are a bias of the whole enterprise, discussed in [research integrity](research-integrity-and-publication-bias.md).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Evidence-based medicine | Integrating best research evidence with clinical expertise and patient values |
| Hierarchy of evidence | Ranking of study designs by vulnerability to bias; a heuristic, not a rule |
| GRADE | System rating certainty per outcome (high to very low) and recommendations as strong or weak |
| Risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication bias | The five reasons for rating certainty down |
| Surrogate outcome | A measurable stand-in for a patient-important outcome; valid only if validated |
| Patient-important outcome | Death, disability, symptoms, quality of life: what the patient would notice |
| Strong versus conditional recommendation | Whether nearly all informed patients would choose the action, or the choice depends on values |
| Clinical practice guideline | Systematically developed statements to assist decisions about care |
| Conflict of interest | A financial or intellectual interest that could bias a judgement |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A randomised trial always beats an observational study."**: on confounding, yes; on bias in other domains, generalisability and the outcomes measured, not necessarily.
- **"A guideline recommendation is an instruction."**: it is a population-level judgement that the clinician must apply, and may justifiably depart from, for one patient.
- **"No evidence of effect means evidence of no effect."**: absence of trials usually reflects who funds research, not what is true.
