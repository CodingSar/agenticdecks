# Screening and prevention

> **Layer:** [10 Epidemiology and Evidence](../README.md) › Screening and prevention
> **Children:** [Levels of prevention](levels-of-prevention.md) · [Screening principles and biases](screening-principles-and-biases.md) · [Risk prediction and stratification](risk-prediction-and-stratification.md)

## What this area is about

Everything else in this layer measures disease after it has happened. This area asks how to intervene before it does: by removing causes, by finding disease in its silent phase, or by identifying the people most likely to develop it. It is where epidemiology stops describing populations and starts acting on them, and where the measures of the first parent and the evidence standards of the second are put to their hardest test, because the people acted on are well and will mostly never benefit.

The one idea that unifies the children is **the natural history of disease as a timeline on which intervention can be moved earlier, at a price that rises as the people reached become healthier**. Prevention levels name the position on the timeline. Screening is the attempt to move diagnosis into the preclinical phase and is judged by biases that are peculiar to that attempt. Risk prediction is the attempt to choose whom to act on, so that the number needed to treat is bearable. Rose's distinction between the population strategy and the high-risk strategy runs through all three.

```
   exposure ----> preclinical disease ----> clinical disease ----> outcome
      ^                  ^                        ^
   primary           secondary (screening)     tertiary
   prevention        finds it here             limits damage

   whom to reach?   population strategy  (shift everyone a little)
                    high-risk strategy   (risk prediction, then treat the tail)
```

Read levels of prevention first for the timeline and the two strategies. Screening then shows what happens when the secondary level is attempted at scale: the test characteristics from the evidence parent collide with low prevalence, and the apparent successes are manufactured by bias. Risk prediction closes by showing how the high-risk strategy chooses its targets, and why even a good model captures a minority of future cases.

## Connections to sibling areas

- **[Epidemiology](../01-epidemiology/README.md):** supplies the natural-history and attributable-fraction reasoning behind the levels, the cohorts to which prediction models are fitted, and the catalogue of time-related biases (lead time and length bias are errors of time zero). Prevention is the applied arm of causal epidemiology: a cause identified there is a target here.
- **[Evidence-based medicine](../02-evidence-based-medicine/README.md):** supplies the test characteristics (sensitivity, specificity, predictive value at low prevalence) that decide whether a screening test can work, the randomised trial of invitation that alone can show it does, and the grading that decides whether a programme or a risk threshold is recommended. Screening is the field in which observational evidence is most misleading and trial evidence most necessary.

## Cross-cutting threads

- [Harm and benefit](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#harm-and-benefit): screening and preventive treatment harm the many well to benefit the few; the number needed to treat is the currency.
- [Probability and uncertainty](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#probability-and-uncertainty): a risk score is a calibrated probability; a screening result is a shift in probability at low prevalence.
- [Time courses](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#time-courses): the natural history timeline, sojourn time, lead time and length bias are all about when on the course one intervenes.
- [Cause and mechanism](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#cause-and-mechanism): prediction is association, prevention requires cause; the two are easily confused.
- [The host and its environment](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#the-host-and-its-environment): primordial prevention acts on the environment that produces the risk factors.
