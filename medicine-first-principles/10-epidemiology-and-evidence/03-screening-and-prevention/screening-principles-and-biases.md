## Essence

Screening is the application of a test to people without symptoms in order to find disease earlier than it would have presented, on the assumption that earlier treatment changes the outcome. It is the most intuitive idea in medicine and the one most reliably misjudged, because every naive measure of its success (more cases found, earlier stage at diagnosis, longer survival after diagnosis) improves automatically whether or not anyone lives longer. Three biases do this: lead-time bias (the clock starts earlier), length bias (slow disease is easier to catch) and overdiagnosis (disease that would never have mattered is counted as a cure). Only a randomised trial comparing mortality in people invited and not invited to screening escapes them, and the organising question is whether the harm done to the many who are well is justified by the benefit to the few who are not.

## Core tenets

1. **Screening is worthwhile only if a set of conditions hold together.** Wilson and Jungner's criteria (1968) still state them: the condition is important; it has a recognisable latent phase; its natural history is understood; there is an accepted treatment that works better when given early; a suitable, acceptable test exists; facilities for diagnosis and treatment are available; the cost is balanced against other spending; and the programme is continuing, not once-off. Failure of any one undermines the whole; the commonest failures are a test that is not accurate enough at low prevalence and a treatment that does not work better early.

2. **Lead-time bias: detecting disease earlier lengthens the time from diagnosis to death even when death is not delayed.** If a cancer would present at age 65 and kill at 70, a screen that finds it at 62 raises "five-year survival" from 100% of patients dying within five years to 100% surviving beyond five years, with the date of death unchanged. Survival from diagnosis is therefore a meaningless outcome for screening. The only valid outcome is mortality from the disease in the whole screened population, counted from the start of screening.

3. **Length bias: a periodic test preferentially catches slow-growing disease.** Disease with a long preclinical phase spends more time in the detectable window, so a screening round is more likely to find indolent cases than aggressive ones, which present between rounds (interval cancers). Screen-detected cases therefore have a better prognosis than symptomatic cases even if screening changes nothing, because they are a different, milder disease. Comparing outcomes of screen-detected with symptomatic cases is comparing different diseases.

4. **Overdiagnosis is length bias taken to its limit: detecting disease that would never have caused symptoms or death.** Autopsy series find prostate cancer in a large fraction of elderly men who died of something else, and thyroid cancer in a substantial minority of adults; most of these would never have mattered. Screening finds them, they are counted as cancers, treated, and their "survivors" swell the apparent success. The person is harmed by the diagnosis and the treatment and could not have benefited. Overdiagnosis is invisible to the individual (nobody knows which case it was) and visible only in population statistics: incidence rises with screening, mortality does not fall by as much.

5. **At low prevalence, most positive screening results are false.** A test with 90% sensitivity and 95% specificity applied where the prevalence is 0.5% gives, per 10,000 people, 45 true positives and about 498 false positives: more than ten false alarms per case found. Each false positive costs anxiety, further tests and sometimes an invasive procedure. The [positive predictive value](../02-evidence-based-medicine/diagnostic-accuracy-studies.md) governs the harm side of screening, and it is low because the screened population is overwhelmingly healthy.

6. **Screening harms the healthy majority to help a minority, so it is a population policy, not a clinical decision.** Benefit accrues to the few whose disease is caught in a treatable window; harm (false positives, overdiagnosis, overtreatment, radiation, cost) accrues to everyone screened. Mammography is the standard case: estimates vary widely between analyses, but a widely cited 2012 United Kingdom review estimated about three overdiagnosed cancers for every breast-cancer death prevented over twenty years of invitation. Whether that trade is worth it is a judgement for the population and the individual, which is why screening invitations now carry decision aids rather than exhortation.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Screening | Testing asymptomatic people to detect disease in its preclinical phase |
| Wilson and Jungner criteria | The conditions a disease, test, treatment and programme must meet for screening to be justified |
| Lead time | The interval by which screening advances the diagnosis; survival measured from diagnosis is inflated by it |
| Length bias | Preferential detection of slow-growing disease by periodic testing |
| Overdiagnosis | Detection of disease that would never have caused symptoms or death |
| Interval cancer | Disease presenting with symptoms between screening rounds |
| Positive predictive value at screening | Proportion of positives that are true; low because prevalence is low |
| Sojourn time | The duration of the detectable preclinical phase |
| Disease-specific mortality | Deaths from the target disease per population, the valid outcome of a screening trial |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Screening found my cancer early, so it saved my life."**: for any individual this cannot be known; the case may have been overdiagnosed, or lead time may have added years of diagnosis without adding years of life. Only trials can say whether the programme saves lives on average.
- **"Five-year survival has improved since screening started, so screening works."**: lead-time and length bias guarantee this improvement whether or not mortality has changed.
- **"A more sensitive test is a better screening test."**: sensitivity bought by lowering the threshold raises false positives and overdiagnosis; the balance depends on prevalence and the cost of each error.
