# Levels of prevention

## Essence

Every disease has a natural history: a period of exposure and susceptibility, a preclinical phase in which the process is under way but silent, a clinical phase, and an outcome. Prevention is intervening earlier on that timeline than the disease would otherwise present, and the levels of prevention are named by where on the timeline they act: primary (before the process starts), secondary (during the silent phase), tertiary (after diagnosis, to limit damage). Each level has its own evidence, its own economics and its own characteristic harm. The idea that organises the field is Rose's: the people who are at high risk and the people who generate most of the cases are usually different groups, so strategies aimed at individuals and strategies aimed at whole populations are not interchangeable.

## Core tenets

1. **The levels are positions on the natural history, not degrees of importance.** *Primary* prevention removes a cause or raises resistance before disease begins: vaccination, clean water, tobacco control, folate before conception. *Secondary* prevention detects disease in its preclinical phase and treats it earlier than it would have presented: screening, and treatment of asymptomatic hypertension. *Tertiary* prevention reduces the consequences of established disease: rehabilitation after stroke, foot care in diabetes. The levels assume the [natural history](../../08-pathology/05-disease-as-process/etiology-pathogenesis-and-natural-history.md) has a detectable, treatable preclinical phase, which not every disease has.

2. **A small shift in a whole population's risk distribution prevents more cases than treating the high-risk tail.** Most heart attacks occur in people with moderately raised blood pressure, because they are many, not in the few with very high pressure. Lowering the population mean by a few mmHg (salt reduction) prevents more events than treating everyone above a threshold. This is the *prevention paradox*: a measure that brings large benefit to the population offers little to each participant, who therefore has little motive to comply.

3. **The high-risk strategy and the population strategy have mirror-image strengths.** Targeting the high-risk individual fits the medical model: the intervention is appropriate to the person, motivation is high, benefit per person is large and the cost is confined to those likely to gain. But it requires finding them (screening), it is palliative rather than radical (it does not remove the cause that keeps producing new high-risk people), and its effect on the population is small. The population strategy is radical and large but behaviourally hard, because it asks the healthy majority to change for a benefit they will never see.

4. **The number needed to treat for prevention is large, so small harms matter.** Treating 100 people with mildly raised blood pressure for five years prevents perhaps one to two strokes; the other ninety-eight take the drug, bear its side-effects and gain nothing they can detect. The lower the baseline risk, the larger the number needed to treat and the more the balance tips toward the number needed to harm. This is why the threshold for preventive treatment is a value judgement about risk tolerance, not a fact about biology, and why [risk prediction](risk-prediction-and-stratification.md) exists to raise the baseline risk of those treated.

5. **Primordial and structural prevention act on the causes of the causes.** Smoking, diet, inactivity and pollution are shaped by price, availability, advertising, urban design and income. Tobacco taxation has reduced smoking more than any clinical intervention; fluoridation, seatbelt law and lead-free petrol prevented disease without a single consultation. These levers belong to policy, not the clinic, which is why prevention is also a subject of [health systems](../../15-medicine-in-society/01-health-systems/README.md) and [social determinants](../../15-medicine-in-society/03-populations-and-the-future/social-determinants-and-health-inequity.md).

6. **Behaviour change is the weakest link and the most studied.** Advice alone changes little; what works combines knowledge with changes to the environment, defaults and incentives, and repeated support ([behaviour change](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/04-non-drug-therapeutics/behaviour-change-and-the-placebo-effect.md)). Preventive interventions also tend to be taken up by the educated and affluent first, so a programme that works on average can widen inequality (the inverse care law in preventive form) unless it is designed to reach those at highest risk.

7. **Prevention needs higher-grade evidence than treatment, because the people it touches are well.** A treatment is offered to someone suffering, who accepts risk for relief; a preventive measure is offered to someone with no symptoms, who may be harmed for a benefit that was never going to materialise for them. The asymmetry sets a stricter standard: randomised trials with hard outcomes, long follow-up and full accounting of harms. Hormone replacement therapy for cardiovascular prevention, supported by cohort studies and reversed by trial (2002), is the standard lesson.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Natural history | The course of a disease from exposure through preclinical and clinical phases to outcome |
| Primary prevention | Preventing the disease process from starting (removing cause, raising resistance) |
| Secondary prevention | Detecting and treating disease in its preclinical phase |
| Tertiary prevention | Limiting disability and complications of established disease |
| Primordial prevention | Acting on the social and economic conditions that produce risk factors |
| Population strategy | Shifting the whole distribution of risk; large population benefit, small individual benefit |
| High-risk strategy | Identifying and treating individuals above a risk threshold |
| Prevention paradox | A measure that benefits the population greatly offers little to each individual |
| Number needed to treat (preventive) | People treated for a period for one event averted; large when baseline risk is low |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Prevention is always cheaper than cure."**: most preventive interventions cost money; some save it (vaccination, tobacco tax), and the question is value for money, judged in [health economics](../../15-medicine-in-society/01-health-systems/health-economics-and-priority-setting.md).
- **"Targeting the highest-risk people prevents the most disease."**: it prevents the most disease *per person treated*; most cases arise from the larger number of people at moderate risk.
- **"If a risk factor causes disease, telling people about it prevents it."**: knowledge changes behaviour weakly; environments, prices and defaults change it more.
