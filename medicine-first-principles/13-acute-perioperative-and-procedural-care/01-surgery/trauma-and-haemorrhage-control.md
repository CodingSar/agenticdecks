## Essence

Trauma is injury by energy. Kinetic energy (½mv²) delivered to tissue tears, crushes and shears it; the body's reply is bleeding, then [shock](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/hemorrhage-shock-and-sepsis.md), then a coagulopathy that makes the bleeding worse. Trauma is the leading cause of death below about 40 years of age worldwide, and haemorrhage is the leading cause of preventable trauma death. The organising idea is that the clock runs on blood volume: the sequence is to find the bleeding and stop it, to replace what was lost with what was lost (blood, not salt water), and to break the self-amplifying loop of acidosis, hypothermia and coagulopathy before it closes. Everything else (the fracture, the laceration, the image) can wait for the bleeding to stop.

## Core tenets

1. **Injury is energy transfer, and the mechanism predicts the pattern.** Blunt energy is spread over an area and decelerates organs against their attachments: the aorta tears at the ligamentum arteriosum, the liver and spleen shear from their pedicles, the brain strikes the skull. Penetrating energy follows a track, and a high-velocity missile adds cavitation that damages tissue far from the track. [Material properties](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/mechanics-and-materials.md) decide which structure fails first: bone before ligament in the young, ligament before bone in the old, solid organs before hollow ones. Knowing the mechanism tells the examiner where to look before the signs appear.

2. **Bleeding compensates, then collapses.** Baroreceptor-driven vasoconstriction and tachycardia ([blood pressure regulation](../../06-physiology/03-cardiovascular-system/hemodynamics-and-blood-pressure-regulation.md)) hold the arterial pressure until about a third of the blood volume is gone, so a young adult can lose over a litre with a normal blood pressure. The early signs are those of compensation: tachycardia, narrowed pulse pressure, cool skin, anxiety, falling urine output. Hypotension is late and means the reserve is spent. Lactate and base deficit measure the oxygen debt the pressure hides. The five places a patient can bleed to death are the chest, the abdomen, the pelvis and retroperitoneum, long bones, and the floor (external), and each has a bedside test: examination, [focused ultrasound](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/03-imaging-and-physiological-measurement/ultrasound.md), a pelvic radiograph, a look at the limbs and the clothing.

3. **The lethal triad feeds itself.** Hypoperfusion makes lactic acidosis; acidosis slows clotting enzymes. Blood loss and exposure make hypothermia; cold platelets and enzymes do not work. Both, plus consumption and dilution, make coagulopathy; coagulopathy makes more bleeding. A quarter or more of severely injured patients arrive already coagulopathic (acute traumatic coagulopathy), driven by shock and tissue injury activating protein C and fibrinolysis ([coagulation](../../06-physiology/08-blood/haemostasis-and-coagulation.md)), before any dilution. Treatment attacks every arm at once: stop the bleeding, warm the patient, give clotting factors with the red cells, and give an antifibrinolytic such as tranexamic acid early.

4. **Replace blood with blood, and not too much of anything else.** Crystalloid restores pressure briefly but dilutes clotting factors, cools the patient, and raises the pressure enough to dislodge the first clot. Damage-control resuscitation therefore gives red cells, plasma and platelets in near-physiological ratios through a massive transfusion protocol ([blood products](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/04-non-drug-therapeutics/fluids-nutrition-and-blood-products.md), [transfusion medicine](../../12-organ-system-specialties/06-haematology/transfusion-medicine.md)), and tolerates a lower-than-normal pressure (permissive hypotension) until the surgeon has control, except where the brain is injured and needs its perfusion pressure. The target is a patient who is warm, not acidotic and clotting, not a patient with a normal blood pressure.

5. **Stop the bleeding before fixing anything else, and fix physiology before fixing anatomy.** External haemorrhage is stopped by pressure, a tourniquet or a haemostatic dressing; a pelvic fracture by a binder; a chest by a drain and, if it keeps bleeding, a thoracotomy; the abdomen by packing and clamping. Damage-control surgery does only what stops bleeding and contamination (pack the liver, staple off perforated bowel, leave the abdomen open), then hands the patient to [critical care](../03-critical-care/shock-and-haemodynamic-support.md) to correct the triad, and returns a day or two later for definitive repair. The lesson, learned in the 1980s and 1990s, is that a long operation in a cold, acidotic, coagulopathic patient kills, however elegant.

6. **The brain and the airway run on a shorter clock than the blood.** Head injury is the other great killer, and the injured brain is destroyed not only by the primary impact but by secondary insults: a single episode of hypotension or hypoxia roughly doubles mortality after severe head injury. Rising intracranial pressure inside a fixed skull ([compartments](../../05-tissues-and-anatomy/02-body-organization/body-cavities-fascia-and-compartments.md)) reduces cerebral perfusion pressure, so an expanding haematoma is a race to computed tomography and neurosurgical decompression. A compromised airway (facial injury, burns, reduced consciousness) kills in minutes, which is why the [ABCDE sequence](../04-emergency-medicine/triage-and-the-abcde-approach.md) puts it first even in the exsanguinating patient.

7. **The injury continues after the bleeding stops.** Reperfusion of ischaemic tissue releases the [inflammatory cascade](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/02-innate-immunity/complement-phagocytes-and-the-inflammatory-cascade.md) systemically; crushed muscle releases myoglobin and potassium that injure the kidney; fat and marrow from long bones embolise to the lung; immobility and the inflammatory state cause venous thrombosis. The trimodal distribution of trauma deaths (at the scene, within hours from haemorrhage and brain injury, and days to weeks later from sepsis and [multi-organ failure](../03-critical-care/sepsis-and-multi-organ-failure.md)) describes three different pathologies needing three different systems: prevention, resuscitation and critical care.

8. **Who else is involved.** Trauma is the most multidisciplinary emergency in medicine. [Emergency medicine](../04-emergency-medicine/README.md) runs the primary survey; [anaesthesia](../02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/airway-and-ventilation-management.md) secures the airway; [radiology](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/03-imaging-and-physiological-measurement/radiography-and-computed-tomography.md) images the whole body in minutes and embolises bleeding vessels; [haematology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/06-haematology/README.md) delivers the massive transfusion and measures the coagulopathy; [orthopaedics](orthopaedics-fractures-and-joints.md) stabilises the pelvis and long bones; neurosurgery, vascular, cardiothoracic and plastic [subspecialties](surgical-subspecialties.md) take their organs; [critical care](../03-critical-care/README.md) owns the days after; [rehabilitation](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/rehabilitation-and-disability.md) owns the years. Trauma networks exist because this team cannot be assembled in every hospital.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mechanism of injury | How energy was delivered (blunt, penetrating, blast, burn); predicts the pattern of injuries |
| Haemorrhagic shock | Inadequate oxygen delivery from loss of circulating volume; classified by the fraction of blood volume lost |
| Lethal triad | Acidosis, hypothermia and coagulopathy, each worsening the others |
| Acute traumatic coagulopathy | Impaired clotting present on arrival, driven by shock and tissue injury rather than dilution |
| Damage-control resuscitation | Permissive hypotension, early blood products in balanced ratios, antifibrinolytic, warming |
| Damage-control surgery | Abbreviated operation to stop bleeding and contamination, physiological correction, delayed definitive repair |
| Massive transfusion | Replacement of about one blood volume within 24 hours, or half within a few hours, by protocol |
| Secondary brain injury | Damage after the impact from hypotension, hypoxia, raised intracranial pressure, fever or seizures |
| Primary survey | The ordered search for immediately lethal injuries: airway, breathing, circulation, disability, exposure |
| Trimodal distribution | Trauma deaths at the scene, within hours, and after days to weeks, each with different causes |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A normal blood pressure means the patient is not bleeding significantly."** Compensation holds pressure until roughly a third of the blood volume is gone; tachycardia, pulse pressure, skin, lactate and urine output fall first.
- **"Fluids are the treatment for haemorrhage."** The treatment is haemostasis. Clear fluid dilutes clotting factors, cools the patient and can lift the pressure enough to restart bleeding; blood products are the replacement.
- **"Tourniquets cost limbs."** Modern evidence from military and civilian practice shows limb loss from a tourniquet applied for under about two hours is rare, while exsanguination from a limb is common and preventable.
