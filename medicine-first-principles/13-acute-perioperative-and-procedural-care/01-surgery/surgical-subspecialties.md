## Essence

General surgery split into subspecialties during the twentieth century for two reasons: anatomy, because the techniques for a coronary artery, a brain and a kidney have little in common, and volume, because outcomes for complex operations improve with the number a team performs. The result is a map of about ten crafts, each defined by an organ system or a technique, each sharing the principles of [the surgical decision](the-surgical-decision.md) but adding one idea of its own: the vascular surgeon thinks in flow and pressure, the cardiothoracic surgeon in the physiology of stopping and restarting the heart, the neurosurgeon in a fixed box, the transplant surgeon in immunology, the plastic surgeon in blood supply to tissue that is moved. The common trend across all of them is that incisions shrink: endoscopes, catheters, laparoscopes and robots deliver the same anatomical change with less injury, and the border with the medical and radiological specialties moves every decade.

## Core tenets

1. **Vascular surgery treats the pipe: the lesion is a loss of flow or a loss of wall.** [Atherosclerosis](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/atherosclerosis.md) narrows arteries and, by Poiseuille's law ([flow](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/fluids-pressure-and-flow.md)), flow falls with the fourth power of radius, so a stenosis is silent until it is critical and then symptomatic on demand (claudication, like angina of the leg) before it is symptomatic at rest. The aneurysm is the opposite failure: the wall weakens, and by Laplace's law wall tension rises with radius, so growth accelerates and rupture risk climbs steeply above about 5.5 cm for the abdominal aorta. The treatments are to bypass, to open from within (angioplasty and stents) or to exclude (endovascular grafts), and much of the specialty has moved from the scalpel to the catheter.

2. **Cardiothoracic surgery operates on organs that cannot stop, so it stops them artificially.** Cardiopulmonary bypass takes venous blood, oxygenates it and pumps it back into the aorta, letting the surgeon arrest the heart with a cold potassium-rich solution (cardioplegia) and work on a still, bloodless field. The price is a whole-body [inflammatory response](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/02-innate-immunity/complement-phagocytes-and-the-inflammatory-cascade.md) to the foreign circuit, a coagulopathy, and a period of global ischaemia that the myocardium must survive. Coronary bypass and [valve replacement](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/valvular-and-structural-heart-disease.md) are the core operations; lung resection for cancer is the thoracic half, limited by predicted postoperative lung function. Percutaneous coronary intervention and transcatheter valves are the medical specialty taking back ground.

3. **Neurosurgery works inside a closed box where pressure, not bleeding, is the enemy.** The skull is rigid, so by the Monro–Kellie doctrine any added volume (haematoma, tumour, oedema, hydrocephalus) must displace blood or cerebrospinal fluid, and once that reserve is spent intracranial pressure rises exponentially and cerebral perfusion pressure falls ([compartments](../../05-tissues-and-anatomy/02-body-organization/body-cavities-fascia-and-compartments.md)). Herniation of brain through the tentorium or foramen magnum follows. The operations therefore remove volume (evacuate a clot, resect a tumour, shunt fluid) or make room (decompressive craniectomy). The spine is the other half: the cord and its roots run in a canal that fractures, discs and tumours can narrow, and the clock for a compressed cord is hours.

4. **Urology is the specialty of a tube under pressure and a filter that depends on it.** Obstruction anywhere from calyx to urethra raises pressure back to the glomerulus and stops [filtration](../../06-physiology/05-renal-system/glomerular-filtration-and-clearance.md); an obstructed and infected kidney is a septic emergency that needs drainage before antimicrobials can work. Stones, prostatic enlargement and tumours of the kidney, bladder, prostate and testis make up most of the work, nearly all of it endoscopic (through the urethra) or percutaneous (through the flank), with lithotripsy fragmenting stones by [shock waves](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/waves-sound-light-and-radiation.md) without an incision.

5. **Plastic and reconstructive surgery is applied blood supply.** Tissue can be moved only if it stays perfused: a graft (skin without its own vessels) survives by diffusion and then ingrowth, so it needs a vascular bed and fails over bone or infection; a flap carries its own artery and vein and can cover anything, including a microvascular free flap re-anastomosed at a distant site. The specialty covers defects left by trauma, burns and cancer resection, where the wound would otherwise heal by contraction and scar ([wound care](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/04-non-drug-therapeutics/principles-of-surgery-and-wound-care.md)). Burns are its emergency: the [capillary leak](../../06-physiology/03-cardiovascular-system/microcirculation-and-capillary-exchange.md) of a large burn causes shock, the dead skin is a barrier lost and an infection waiting, and inhalation injury obstructs the airway.

6. **Transplant surgery is the one place where the operation is the easy part.** Removing a failing organ and plumbing in another is a technical exercise; keeping it is immunology. The recipient's T cells recognise foreign major histocompatibility complex molecules ([transplant immunology](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/04-immunity-in-disease/transplant-and-tumour-immunology.md)) and reject the graft within days unless suppressed for life, and the suppression trades rejection for infection and cancer. Supply is the limiting factor: donors after brain death or circulatory death, living donors for kidney and liver, and the ischaemic time between removal and reperfusion set what is possible. Kidney transplantation extends life and is cheaper than [dialysis](../../12-organ-system-specialties/03-nephrology/chronic-kidney-disease-and-dialysis.md); liver and heart transplantation are the only treatment for end-stage failure.

7. **Minimally invasive surgery changes the cost side, not the benefit side.** Laparoscopy, thoracoscopy, endoscopy and robotics do the same operation through smaller wounds with less tissue injury, a smaller stress response, less pain, fewer wound complications and faster recovery, at the price of longer learning curves and the loss of touch. Where the technique can deliver the same anatomical result, the surgical decision tips towards operating because the injury is cheaper; the same shift is driving operations out of surgery altogether into interventional radiology, cardiology and gastroenterology.

8. **Who else is involved.** Each subspecialty has a medical twin that refers to it and takes the patient back: [cardiology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/README.md) for cardiothoracic, [neurology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/README.md) for neurosurgery, [nephrology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/03-nephrology/README.md) for urology and transplantation, [oncology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/07-oncology/README.md) for every cancer resection, [dermatology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/11-dermatology/README.md) for [skin cancer](../../12-organ-system-specialties/11-dermatology/skin-cancer-and-skin-infections.md) and burns. [Radiology](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/03-imaging-and-physiological-measurement/README.md) is both diagnostic partner and competitor; [anaesthesia](../02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/README.md) and [critical care](../03-critical-care/README.md) make the biggest operations survivable; [ophthalmology and Ear, Nose and Throat (ENT) surgery](../05-ophthalmology-and-ent/README.md) are subspecialties that became specialties.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Volume–outcome relationship | Better results for complex operations in centres and surgeons that do more of them |
| Critical stenosis | Narrowing at which flow falls enough to cause symptoms on demand (about 70% diameter loss) |
| Laplace's law | Wall tension rises with radius and pressure; why aneurysms grow faster as they enlarge |
| Cardiopulmonary bypass | Extracorporeal circuit that oxygenates and pumps blood while the heart is arrested |
| Monro–Kellie doctrine | Fixed skull volume: brain, blood and cerebrospinal fluid trade space; added volume raises pressure |
| Obstructive uropathy | Back-pressure from a blocked urinary tract stopping filtration; an infected obstructed kidney is an emergency |
| Graft versus flap | Tissue moved without (graft) or with (flap) its own blood supply |
| Rejection | T-cell recognition of foreign major histocompatibility molecules destroying a transplanted organ |
| Ischaemic time | Interval between stopping an organ's circulation and reperfusion; limits what can be transplanted |
| Minimally invasive surgery | Same anatomical operation through smaller wounds: less injury, smaller stress response |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Subspecialties exist because the organs are different."** They exist as much because of volume: concentrating rare operations in few hands measurably lowers mortality, which is why trauma, cancer and transplant services are centralised.
- **"A smaller incision is a smaller operation."** The internal operation and its risks are the same; what shrinks is the access injury, which is why keyhole surgery still needs the same anaesthetic and the same judgement.
- **"Transplantation is limited by surgical skill."** It is limited by donor supply, ischaemic time and the immune system; the operation itself was solved in the 1950s and 1960s, immunosuppression was not solved until the 1980s.
