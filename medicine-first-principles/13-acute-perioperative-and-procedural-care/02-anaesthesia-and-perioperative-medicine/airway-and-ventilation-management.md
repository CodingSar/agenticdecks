## Essence

The airway is the first thing an anaesthetic takes away and the first thing that kills when it is lost. Consciousness keeps the pharynx open by muscle tone, keeps the lungs clear by the cough and gag reflexes, and keeps carbon dioxide and oxygen in range by a chemoreceptor drive that hypnotics and opioids suppress. Remove consciousness and within a minute the tongue falls back, the reflexes go, and the drive to breathe fades; the oxygen stored in the lungs then runs out in a time set by the functional residual capacity and the metabolic rate. Airway management is the set of manoeuvres and devices that keep gas moving between the atmosphere and the alveoli through all of this, and ventilation management is what replaces the breathing the drugs have stopped. Both rest on one principle: have a plan for failure before the first drug is given, because the time available is minutes.

## Core tenets

1. **The airway is held open by muscle tone, so loss of consciousness obstructs it.** The pharynx is a collapsible tube between two rigid ones (nose and larynx). In the awake state the genioglossus and pharyngeal dilators contract with each inspiration against the negative pressure that would collapse it ([lung mechanics](../../06-physiology/04-respiratory-system/ventilation-and-lung-mechanics.md)); hypnotics, opioids, alcohol, stroke and sleep itself reduce that tone. The tongue and soft palate then fall against the posterior pharyngeal wall. Obstruction shows as snoring, then paradoxical see-saw movement of chest and abdomen, then silence. Extending the head, lifting the jaw forward and inserting an oropharyngeal or nasopharyngeal airway pull the soft tissue off the wall; this is the whole of basic airway management and it is sufficient in most patients.

2. **The protective reflexes go next, and a full stomach becomes a lethal one.** The laryngeal closure, cough and gag reflexes keep secretions, blood and vomit out of the lungs. Under anaesthesia they are abolished and the lower oesophageal sphincter relaxes, so gastric contents can reflux and be inhaled: acid causes a chemical pneumonitis, particulate matter obstructs bronchi, and both can progress to the [acute respiratory distress syndrome](../03-critical-care/respiratory-failure-and-mechanical-ventilation.md). Fasting reduces the volume; a cuffed tracheal tube is the only device that seals the airway against it. In the patient who cannot be fasted (trauma, bowel obstruction, labour, the emergency department) the interval between losing the reflexes and sealing the trachea is compressed to seconds by a rapid sequence induction.

3. **Preoxygenation buys time, and the body's shape and state decide how much.** The lungs hold about 2–3 L of gas at end-expiration (the functional residual capacity), normally 21% oxygen. Breathing 100% oxygen for a few minutes replaces the nitrogen with oxygen, raising the store from a few hundred millilitres to about two litres; at a resting consumption of around 250 mL/min this extends safe apnoea from under a minute to several minutes ([gas exchange and transport](../../06-physiology/04-respiratory-system/gas-exchange-and-transport.md)). Pregnancy, obesity, infancy, sepsis and lung disease reduce the store or raise the consumption and shrink the margin to a minute or two, which is why these patients desaturate first. Carbon dioxide rises during apnoea at a few mmHg per minute, slowly enough that oxygen, not carbon dioxide, is the limit.

4. **Devices form a ladder, and the plan matters more than the rung.** A face mask with a two-handed seal ventilates almost anyone with an open airway; a supraglottic device sits over the larynx and holds the airway open without skill at laryngoscopy; a tracheal tube passed through the vocal cords under direct or video laryngoscopy seals and secures the airway; if none can oxygenate the patient, the cricothyroid membrane is opened from the front of the neck. The rare catastrophe is "cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate", and audits (the 2011 UK National Audit Project) found that deaths came less from difficult anatomy than from repeated attempts, delay in moving down the ladder, and failure to plan. Prediction of the difficult airway from examination (mouth opening, neck movement, jaw size, obesity, previous surgery or radiotherapy) is useful but imperfect, so the plan is made for every patient.

5. **Positive pressure ventilation reverses the physiology of breathing.** Spontaneous breathing lowers intrathoracic pressure and pulls air and venous blood into the chest; a ventilator pushes gas in and raises intrathoracic pressure, which impedes venous return and lowers cardiac output, especially in the hypovolaemic patient, and distributes gas to the non-dependent lung while blood runs to the dependent lung, worsening [ventilation–perfusion mismatch](../../06-physiology/04-respiratory-system/ventilation-perfusion-matching.md). Anaesthesia itself collapses dependent lung (atelectasis) within minutes by loss of diaphragm tone and absorption of oxygen behind closed airways; positive end-expiratory pressure and recruitment breaths hold it open. The anaesthetist sets a tidal volume near 6–8 mL/kg of ideal body weight and a rate that delivers the carbon dioxide clearance the patient's metabolism needs, then reads the result from the capnograph.

6. **Capnography proves everything else.** Carbon dioxide appears in expired gas only if it is produced by metabolism, carried to the lungs by the circulation, delivered to alveoli by ventilation and exhaled through an unobstructed device. A sustained waveform therefore confirms that a tube is in the trachea, that the heart is pumping and that the circuit is intact, and its shape and trend show bronchospasm, rebreathing, falling cardiac output and return of spontaneous circulation during [resuscitation](../04-emergency-medicine/resuscitation-and-cardiac-arrest.md). Oesophageal intubation, the classic unrecognised disaster, is excluded by the absence of carbon dioxide within a few breaths, and no other sign (chest movement, breath sounds, misting of the tube) is reliable.

7. **Oxygen and ventilation have their own dose–response curves.** Hypoxaemia kills in minutes, so oxygen is given freely in the acute phase; but a high inspired fraction over hours promotes absorption atelectasis and oxidative injury, and in the chronic carbon dioxide retainer it removes the hypoxic drive and worsens ventilation–perfusion matching, raising carbon dioxide ([control of breathing](../../06-physiology/04-respiratory-system/control-of-breathing.md)). Large tidal volumes stretch alveoli and injure them, which is why ventilation is "protective" and [critical care](../03-critical-care/respiratory-failure-and-mechanical-ventilation.md) spends days on this balance. Extubation reverses the induction sequence and is the more dangerous of the two, because the patient must regain tone, reflexes and drive together while the anaesthetist can no longer act for them.

8. **Who else is involved.** [Ear, nose and throat surgery](../05-ophthalmology-and-ent/nose-throat-and-head-and-neck.md) shares the airway as its operating field and performs the tracheostomy; maxillofacial surgery deals with the fractured face that cannot be masked; [emergency medicine](../04-emergency-medicine/README.md) secures airways in patients who are bleeding, vomiting and unassessed; [critical care](../03-critical-care/README.md) keeps the tube in for days and decides when it comes out; [neonatology](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/the-newborn-and-neonatology.md) manages the smallest airway with the least reserve; [pulmonology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/02-pulmonology/README.md) and [neurology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/README.md) supply the patients whose lungs or bulbar muscles cannot protect themselves.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Upper airway obstruction | Collapse of the pharynx against the posterior wall when muscle tone is lost |
| Airway manoeuvre | Head tilt, chin lift, jaw thrust: pulling the soft tissue off the pharyngeal wall |
| Aspiration | Inhalation of gastric contents or blood into the lungs after loss of protective reflexes |
| Rapid sequence induction | Induction and paralysis in quick succession to minimise the unprotected interval in a full-stomach patient |
| Preoxygenation | Replacing lung nitrogen with oxygen to extend safe apnoea time |
| Functional residual capacity | Gas left in the lungs at end-expiration; the oxygen store during apnoea |
| Supraglottic device | An airway sitting above the larynx that holds the pharynx open without laryngoscopy |
| Cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate | Failure of all upper-airway routes; front-of-neck access is the rescue |
| Capnography | Continuous measurement of expired carbon dioxide; confirms ventilation, circulation and tube position |
| Positive end-expiratory pressure | Pressure maintained at end-expiration to keep alveoli open |
| Atelectasis | Collapse of lung units; begins within minutes of induction |

## Common misconceptions

- **"If the chest moves and breath sounds are heard, the tube is in the trachea."** Both are unreliable; only sustained expired carbon dioxide proves tracheal placement, and oesophageal intubation unrecognised for minutes is fatal.
- **"Apnoea is dangerous because carbon dioxide rises."** Carbon dioxide rises slowly; oxygen desaturation is what kills within minutes, and preoxygenation is what delays it.
- **"Difficult airways can be predicted."** Bedside tests have low sensitivity and specificity; most unexpected difficulty occurs in patients predicted to be easy, so every induction needs a failure plan.
- **"Giving oxygen is always harmless."** In the acute phase hypoxia is the danger; over hours, excess oxygen collapses alveoli, injures lung and, in the chronic retainer, raises carbon dioxide.
