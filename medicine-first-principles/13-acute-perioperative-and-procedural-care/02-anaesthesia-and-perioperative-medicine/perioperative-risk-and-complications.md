## Essence

Most people who die after an operation die of a medical illness that the operation provoked: a myocardial infarction, a pneumonia, a pulmonary embolus, a kidney that stopped, a delirium that led to a fall. Perioperative medicine is the discipline that predicts these, prevents what can be prevented, and recognises the rest early. Its central quantity is physiological reserve: the gap between what a patient's heart, lungs, kidneys and brain can do and what the stress response of surgery will demand of them. Risk is the probability that demand exceeds reserve, and it is a product of the patient, the procedure and the urgency. Measuring that product honestly, before the first incision, is what turns [the surgical decision](../01-surgery/the-surgical-decision.md) from a hope into an estimate.

## Core tenets

1. **Risk is patient reserve against procedural demand, modified by urgency.** The American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status grade (I healthy to V moribund) is a crude but durable summary of the patient; the procedure is graded by its tissue injury, blood loss and fluid shifts; emergency surgery multiplies the risk of the same operation several-fold because there is no time to optimise and the disease is already consuming reserve. Scores built from these by [regression on large cohorts](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/03-screening-and-prevention/risk-prediction-and-stratification.md) give a probability of death or major complication that frames the conversation with the patient. Over 300 million operations are performed each year worldwide (2010s estimate), and the deaths that follow are concentrated in a small high-risk fraction.

2. **Functional capacity is reserve measured directly.** A patient who can climb two flights of stairs (about four metabolic equivalents) is demonstrating that their heart and lungs can roughly quadruple oxygen delivery, which is the order of demand major surgery imposes. Cardiopulmonary exercise testing ([functional testing](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/03-imaging-and-physiological-measurement/functional-testing-spirometry-exercise-and-sleep.md)) measures the same thing precisely as oxygen uptake at the anaerobic threshold, and a low value predicts complications better than any resting investigation, because resting tests measure the organ and the exercise test measures the reserve ([supply and demand](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#supply-and-demand)).

3. **The perioperative myocardial infarction is usually a mismatch, not a plaque rupture.** Tachycardia, anaemia, hypotension, hypoxaemia and the catecholamine surge all raise oxygen demand or cut supply in a myocardium with fixed [coronary stenoses](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/ischemic-heart-disease.md); the result is subendocardial ischaemia, most often in the first two or three days, and most often silent, because the patient is analgesed and the pain is attributed to the wound. A troponin rise after surgery (myocardial injury after non-cardiac surgery) occurs in a substantial minority of older patients and carries a markedly higher mortality even without symptoms. The prevention is therefore not a procedure on the coronaries but avoidance of the mismatch: keep the pulse slow, the haemoglobin adequate, the pressure up and the oxygen on.

4. **The operated lung collapses, and the diaphragm is the reason.** General anaesthesia causes atelectasis, and an upper abdominal or thoracic incision inhibits the diaphragm reflexly for days, so the patient takes small breaths, does not cough and does not clear secretions. Collapsed lung becomes [pneumonia](../../12-organ-system-specialties/02-pulmonology/pneumonia-and-respiratory-infections.md). The determinants are the incision site, pre-existing lung disease ([chronic obstructive pulmonary disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/02-pulmonology/asthma-and-copd.md)), smoking, obesity, age and poor pain control; the preventions are regional analgesia that allows deep breathing, sitting up, early mobilisation, and stopping smoking weeks before.

5. **The postoperative patient embodies Virchow's triad.** Immobility slows venous flow, the wound and the inflammatory response make blood hypercoagulable, and surgery and cannulae injure endothelium ([thrombosis](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/thrombosis-embolism-and-infarction.md)). Without prophylaxis, deep vein thrombosis follows major orthopaedic and pelvic surgery in a large fraction of patients and pulmonary embolism is a leading cause of sudden postoperative death. Mechanical compression and a low-molecular-weight heparin such as enoxaparin, started at the right moment relative to the bleeding risk of the wound, reduce this substantially; the balance of bleeding against clotting is a shared decision with [haematology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/06-haematology/bleeding-and-clotting-disorders.md) for the patient already anticoagulated.

6. **The kidney records every episode of hypoperfusion.** Postoperative [acute kidney injury](../../12-organ-system-specialties/03-nephrology/acute-kidney-injury.md) follows hypovolaemia, hypotension, sepsis, contrast and nephrotoxic drugs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatories, aminoglycosides) acting on a kidney that may already have reduced reserve. Oliguria after surgery is partly physiological (antidiuretic hormone and aldosterone are part of the stress response) and partly a warning; the distinction is made from the trend in creatinine and the response to volume. Fluid is a drug with a [dose–response](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#dose-response): too little starves the kidney, too much salt and water causes oedema of the gut, the lung and the wound and delays recovery, and the modern aim is a zero balance rather than a generous one ([fluids](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/04-non-drug-therapeutics/fluids-nutrition-and-blood-products.md)).

7. **The brain is the organ with the least measured reserve.** Delirium after surgery affects a large fraction of older patients, especially after hip fracture and cardiac surgery; it is driven by the inflammatory response, pain, drugs (anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids), sleep disruption, hypoxia and withdrawal, acting on a brain with reduced cognitive reserve. It lengthens stay, precipitates falls and is associated with lasting cognitive decline. [Frailty](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/frailty-multimorbidity-and-polypharmacy.md), a measure of accumulated deficits across systems, predicts delirium, complications and death better than chronological age, and is the reason the geriatrician belongs in the perioperative team.

8. **Recovery is engineered, and who else is involved.** Enhanced recovery pathways assemble the evidence into a bundle: optimise anaemia, glycaemia, nutrition and exercise beforehand (prehabilitation); minimise fasting; use regional and multimodal analgesia; avoid salt and water excess; feed and mobilise within a day; remove drains and catheters early. Each step attacks one of the complications above, and together they shorten stay and reduce complications across specialties. The team is anaesthesia, [surgery](../01-surgery/README.md), [cardiology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/README.md) for the risk assessment and the perioperative infarction, [geriatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/README.md) for frailty and delirium, [critical care](../03-critical-care/README.md) for the planned admission of the highest-risk patient, and [primary care](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/README.md) for the optimisation before and the recovery after, which is where most of the time is spent.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ASA physical status | Six-point grade of the patient's systemic health used in every perioperative risk estimate |
| Functional capacity | Exercise tolerance in metabolic equivalents; a direct measure of cardiorespiratory reserve |
| Cardiopulmonary exercise testing | Measurement of oxygen uptake and anaerobic threshold during graded exercise |
| Myocardial injury after non-cardiac surgery | Postoperative troponin rise from supply–demand ischaemia, usually silent, prognostically important |
| Postoperative pulmonary complications | Atelectasis, pneumonia, respiratory failure after surgery; worst after upper abdominal and thoracic incisions |
| Venous thromboembolism prophylaxis | Mechanical and pharmacological measures against the postoperative clotting state |
| Postoperative acute kidney injury | Fall in filtration after surgery from hypoperfusion, sepsis or nephrotoxins |
| Postoperative delirium | Acute fluctuating disturbance of attention and cognition after surgery, commonest in the old and frail |
| Frailty | Reduced reserve across multiple systems; predicts perioperative harm independently of age |
| Enhanced recovery | Evidence-based bundle of perioperative measures that shortens recovery and cuts complications |
| Prehabilitation | Improving fitness, nutrition and anaemia in the weeks before elective surgery |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A normal resting electrocardiogram and echocardiogram mean the heart is fit for surgery."** Resting tests measure structure; risk depends on reserve, which only exercise or its history reveals.
- **"Postoperative chest pain is the wound."** Perioperative infarction is usually painless or masked; a troponin rise after surgery is common in the old and predicts death.
- **"More intravenous fluid is safer for the kidney."** Beyond restoring volume, excess salt and water causes oedema that harms gut, lung and wound; the dose–response curve is U-shaped.
- **"Age is the risk."** Frailty and functional capacity, not years, predict harm; a fit eighty-year-old outperforms a frail sixty-year-old.
