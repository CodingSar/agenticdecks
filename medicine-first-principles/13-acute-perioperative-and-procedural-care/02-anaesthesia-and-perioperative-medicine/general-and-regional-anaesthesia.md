## Essence

Anaesthesia is the reversible removal of four things an operation would otherwise provoke: consciousness, the perception of pain, movement, and the autonomic reflexes that make the heart race and the pressure climb under the knife. General anaesthesia removes them at the brain and spinal cord with drugs that reach every synapse; regional anaesthesia removes pain and movement from one part of the body by stopping the nerves that serve it from conducting, leaving the patient awake. The single idea that makes both work is that the nervous system is a set of ion channels, and a drug that shifts the balance of a channel's gating can switch off a whole function while leaving the cells alive to recover when the drug leaves. The art is in the pharmacokinetics: these drugs act in seconds and wear off by redistribution in minutes, so an anaesthetic is a continuous titration against a monitored patient rather than a dose.

## Core tenets

1. **Anaesthesia is four separable effects, best produced by separate drugs.** Hypnosis (unconsciousness and amnesia), analgesia, immobility and suppression of autonomic reflexes were once all extracted from one agent (ether) at the cost of a deep, dangerous dose. Balanced anaesthesia uses a hypnotic for the first, an opioid or a regional block for the second, a neuromuscular blocker for the third, and the sum, plus adjuvants, for the fourth, each at a fraction of the dose that would be needed alone. The therapeutic gain follows from [dose–response](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/02-pharmacodynamics/dose-response-and-therapeutic-index.md): each drug is kept on the flat, safe part of its own curve.

2. **General anaesthetics act on ligand-gated channels, mostly to enhance inhibition.** Propofol, the volatile agents (sevoflurane, isoflurane), benzodiazepines and barbiturates potentiate the γ-aminobutyric acid type A (GABA-A) receptor, prolonging chloride currents that hyperpolarise neurons ([synaptic transmission](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/synaptic-transmission-and-neurotransmitters.md)); ketamine and nitrous oxide instead block the excitatory N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) glutamate receptor, which is why ketamine preserves breathing and blood pressure and produces dissociation rather than sleep. The correlation of anaesthetic potency with lipid solubility (the Meyer–Overton rule, 1899–1901) is now understood as binding to hydrophobic pockets on these proteins, not as a non-specific effect on membranes. At the system level, the drugs disrupt thalamocortical and cortico-cortical communication so that information no longer integrates ([consciousness](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/higher-functions-memory-language-and-consciousness.md)); the same drugs at lower concentrations erase memory before they erase awareness.

3. **Potency and speed are set by partial pressure and partition, not by dose.** For inhaled agents the brain follows the alveolar partial pressure, so the anaesthetist controls depth with the inspired concentration and the Minimum Alveolar Concentration (MAC) is the agent's median effective dose for immobility (about 2% for sevoflurane, roughly halved by an opioid, falling with age). Speed of onset and offset follow the blood:gas partition coefficient ([diffusion](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/diffusion-osmosis-and-transport.md)): a poorly soluble agent equilibrates fast. For intravenous agents, induction is a [distribution](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/01-pharmacokinetics/distribution-and-protein-binding.md) event (the bolus reaches the brain in one circulation time) and emergence is redistribution from brain to muscle and fat, not elimination. After a long infusion the peripheral compartments are full and offset slows, the context-sensitive half-time, which is why the same drug that gives a five-minute wake-up after a bolus gives an hour after a day of sedation in [intensive care](../03-critical-care/organ-support-and-icu-syndromes.md).

4. **Neuromuscular blockers act at one synapse and bring one danger.** They occupy the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor at the neuromuscular junction: non-depolarising agents (rocuronium, atracurium) as competitive antagonists reversed by raising acetylcholine (neostigmine) or by encapsulating the drug (sugammadex); suxamethonium as an agonist that depolarises then desensitises, acting within a minute and wearing off within minutes by plasma cholinesterase. A paralysed patient cannot breathe or signal, so paralysis obliges the anaesthetist to ventilate and to measure hypnosis independently of movement; awareness under paralysis is the specialty's rare, feared failure. Residual block after the operation is common and silent, and quantified by a nerve stimulator.

5. **Regional anaesthesia blocks sodium channels in a nerve, and the fibres fall in a predictable order.** Local anaesthetics (lidocaine, bupivacaine) are weak bases that cross the axon membrane in the uncharged form, then bind the open and inactivated states of the voltage-gated sodium channel from inside, stopping the [action potential](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/membrane-potential-and-the-action-potential.md). Small and unmyelinated fibres (autonomic, pain, temperature) block first and large motor fibres last, producing a differential block in which a patient may feel touch and pressure but not pain. Injected around a peripheral nerve or plexus, in the epidural space, or into the cerebrospinal fluid (spinal), the same drug anaesthetises a limb, a dermatomal band or the lower half of the body. The dose is limited by toxicity: as plasma concentration rises the same channel block reaches the brain (seizures) and then the myocardium (arrhythmia and collapse, worst with bupivacaine), and infected or acidic tissue resists the block because less drug is uncharged.

6. **A neuraxial block switches off the sympathetic chain as well as pain.** Spinal and epidural anaesthesia block the sympathetic preganglionic fibres leaving the thoracolumbar cord ([autonomic nervous system](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/the-autonomic-nervous-system.md)), so arterioles and veins below the block dilate, venous return and systemic resistance fall and the blood pressure drops, severely in a hypovolaemic patient or when the block rises to the cardiac accelerator fibres. The same sympathetic block is the benefit: by cutting the afferent limb of the [stress response](../01-surgery/the-surgical-decision.md) it blunts the catabolic surge more completely than any opioid, improves gut blood flow and reduces thrombosis, which is why epidural analgesia changed outcomes in major abdominal surgery and why labour analgesia is an epidural rather than an opioid ([obstetrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/labour-delivery-and-the-puerperium.md)).

7. **Pain relief is layered because pain is layered.** Tissue injury activates nociceptors, sensitises them through inflammatory mediators, and after hours sensitises the dorsal horn centrally ([pain](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/pain.md)). Multimodal analgesia attacks each level: local anaesthetic at the wound or nerve, a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory at the mediators, paracetamol centrally, an opioid at μ-receptors in the dorsal horn and brain, ketamine at the NMDA receptor to limit central sensitisation. Opioids alone are the worst single answer: they depress breathing through the same brainstem that drives it ([control of breathing](../../06-physiology/04-respiratory-system/control-of-breathing.md)), cause nausea and ileus, and acute tolerance and hyperalgesia with prolonged use ([tolerance and dependence](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/02-pharmacodynamics/tolerance-dependence-and-withdrawal.md)).

8. **Because the drugs remove the body's own monitoring, the anaesthetist supplies it.** An anaesthetised patient does not report breathlessness, pain or faintness, and the chemoreceptor and baroreceptor reflexes are blunted, so the specialty's minimum standard is continuous measurement of oxygenation (pulse oximetry), ventilation (capnography), circulation ([electrocardiography](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/03-imaging-and-physiological-measurement/electrocardiography-and-electrophysiological-testing.md), blood pressure), temperature and, when paralysed, neuromuscular function and depth of hypnosis. The introduction of pulse oximetry and capnography in the 1980s reduced anaesthetic mortality to the order of 1 in 100,000 or better in healthy patients in high-income countries, one of the clearest effects of monitoring on outcome in medicine. Who else is involved: [surgery](../01-surgery/README.md) sets the stimulus the anaesthetic must match, [critical care](../03-critical-care/README.md) inherits the sedation pharmacology, pain medicine extends the regional techniques to chronic pain, and [palliative care](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/palliative-care-and-symptom-control.md) uses the same opioid and adjuvant logic for symptom control.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Balanced anaesthesia | Hypnosis, analgesia, immobility and reflex suppression from separate drugs at lower doses |
| GABA-A potentiation | The main mechanism of hypnotics: enhanced inhibitory chloride currents |
| NMDA antagonism | Mechanism of ketamine and nitrous oxide: blocked excitatory glutamate signalling, dissociation |
| Minimum alveolar concentration | Alveolar concentration of an inhaled agent at which half of subjects do not move to a surgical incision |
| Blood:gas partition coefficient | Solubility of an inhaled agent in blood; low solubility means fast onset and offset |
| Context-sensitive half-time | Time for plasma concentration to halve after stopping an infusion; grows with infusion duration |
| Neuromuscular blocker | Drug acting at the nicotinic receptor of the neuromuscular junction to abolish movement |
| Differential block | Order of fibre block by local anaesthetics: autonomic and pain before touch and motor |
| Neuraxial block | Spinal or epidural anaesthesia; blocks sympathetic outflow as well as sensation |
| Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity | Seizures and cardiovascular collapse when plasma concentration rises; dose- and drug-dependent |
| Multimodal analgesia | Combining agents acting at different levels of the pain pathway to reduce opioid need |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Anaesthesia is sleep."** Sleep is an active, reversible brain state with preserved arousal to stimuli; anaesthesia is a drug-induced loss of information integration with no arousal, closer to a reversible coma, and its electroencephalogram looks different.
- **"A patient wakes up because the drug is eliminated."** After a bolus, emergence is redistribution to muscle and fat with most of the drug still in the body; elimination determines offset only after prolonged infusion.
- **"If the patient does not move, they are unconscious."** Neuromuscular block abolishes movement independently of hypnosis; awareness under paralysis is rare precisely because hypnosis is monitored separately.
- **"Local anaesthetic is safe because it is local."** The dose that is safe at the nerve is toxic in the plasma; inadvertent intravascular injection or an excessive total dose causes seizures and cardiac arrest.
