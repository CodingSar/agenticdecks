## Essence

The ear solves two physics problems. The first is impedance: sound in air carries little energy per unit area and would mostly reflect off the fluid of the inner ear, so the eardrum and three middle-ear bones concentrate it onto a window twenty times smaller, matching air to water. The second is frequency analysis: the cochlea is a tapered, fluid-filled tube whose stiffness varies along its length, so each frequency makes a different place resonate and hair cells there transduce it into nerve impulses, a mechanical Fourier transform. The same fluid system, bent into three semicircular canals and two otolith organs, measures head rotation and gravity. Hearing fails either in the conduction of sound to the cochlea (conductive loss, usually fixable) or in the hair cells and nerve (sensorineural loss, usually permanent but now bypassable by an implant); balance fails when the inner ear, the eyes and the proprioceptors disagree, which the brain experiences as vertigo.

## Core tenets

1. **The middle ear is an impedance transformer, and its failures are conductive.** The tympanic membrane (about 55 mm²) collects sound and the ossicular chain levers it onto the oval window (about 3 mm²), giving a pressure gain of roughly 20–30 decibels; without it most of the acoustic energy would be reflected at the air–fluid boundary ([acoustics](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/waves-sound-light-and-radiation.md)). Anything that loads or disconnects this system (wax in the canal, fluid behind the drum in otitis media with effusion, a perforation, fixation of the stapes in otosclerosis, a disrupted chain after trauma or cholesteatoma) causes a conductive loss of up to about 60 dB, in which bone-conducted sound bypasses the fault and is heard better than air-conducted sound. The tuning-fork tests (Rinne, Weber) and the audiogram's air–bone gap read this directly, and the treatments are mechanical: remove, drain, graft, replace the bone with a prosthesis.

2. **The cochlea is a place-coded frequency analyser, and its failures are sensorineural.** The basilar membrane is stiff and narrow at the base, which resonates to high frequencies, and compliant and wide at the apex, for low ones; a travelling wave peaks at the place tuned to each frequency and the inner hair cells there open mechanically gated channels and drive the auditory nerve, while outer hair cells actively amplify and sharpen the tuning ([sensory systems](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/sensory-systems.md)). Hair cells do not regenerate in mammals. Noise, age (presbycusis), aminoglycosides and platinum chemotherapy, genetic mutations and viral infection destroy them, high-frequency cells first, so the typical sensorineural loss is a high-frequency slope on the audiogram with poor speech discrimination in noise, and no air–bone gap. About 1.5 billion people live with some hearing loss (World Health Organization, 2021), and noise is the main preventable cause ([occupational medicine](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/sports-occupational-and-lifestyle-medicine.md)).

3. **Hearing is restored by machines, and the implant bypasses the dead transducer.** A hearing aid amplifies and compresses sound into the surviving dynamic range; it helps most losses but cannot restore the frequency resolution that dead outer hair cells provided, so speech in noise stays hard. When too few hair cells survive, a cochlear implant converts sound into electrical pulses delivered by an electrode array threaded along the cochlea, stimulating the auditory nerve directly at the places that encode each frequency band ([devices and implants](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/04-non-drug-therapeutics/devices-implants-and-biomaterials.md)). With a dozen or two channels it restores open-set speech understanding in most recipients, and in a congenitally deaf child implanted in the first year or two of life it allows spoken language to develop normally, because the auditory cortex, like the visual, has a critical period. A sudden sensorineural loss of unknown cause is treated as an emergency with corticosteroids within days, on modest evidence, because the window for recovery is short.

4. **The vestibular system measures acceleration, and vertigo is a disagreement.** Each semicircular canal senses angular acceleration in one plane as endolymph lags the skull and bends the hair cells of its crista; the otolith organs sense gravity and linear acceleration through calcium carbonate crystals resting on their hair cells. The output drives the vestibulo-ocular reflex, which moves the eyes equal and opposite to the head to keep the image still, and the postural reflexes. Vertigo, the illusion of movement, arises when the two ears disagree or the vestibular signal conflicts with vision and proprioception, and the eyes betray it as nystagmus. Because the reflex is hard-wired, examining the eyes during head movement (the head impulse test, the pattern of nystagmus, skew deviation) tells whether the fault is in the labyrinth or in the brainstem and cerebellum, and in acute vertigo this bedside triad distinguishes a peripheral cause from a posterior circulation [stroke](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/stroke.md) more reliably than early imaging.

5. **The common vertigos each have a mechanism in the fluid system.** Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo is otolith crystals that have escaped into a canal (usually the posterior) and move with gravity, so a head position provokes seconds of spinning; it is cured in most patients by a sequence of head movements that rolls the crystals back out. Vestibular neuritis is a sudden unilateral loss of labyrinthine input, usually viral, causing days of continuous vertigo that the brain compensates for over weeks by recalibrating. Ménière's disease is episodic vertigo with fluctuating low-frequency hearing loss, tinnitus and fullness, attributed to a build-up of endolymph pressure (hydrops) that distends the membranous labyrinth ([pressure](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/fluids-pressure-and-flow.md)). The treatments follow: repositioning, compensation with exercises, and for Ménière's salt restriction, diuretics and, if disabling, ablation of the offending labyrinth.

6. **The middle ear is a closed space connected to the nose, and the child's connection fails.** The Eustachian tube equalises middle-ear pressure with the pharynx; in children it is short, horizontal and easily blocked by adenoids and upper respiratory infections ([scale](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#scale-and-surface-to-volume)). Negative pressure draws fluid into the cleft (otitis media with effusion, "glue ear"), which causes a conductive loss at the age when language is being learned, and bacterial infection behind a closed drum produces acute otitis media, one of the commonest [childhood illnesses](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/common-childhood-illness.md). Most resolve; a grommet ventilates the ear that does not. The rare complications follow the anatomy ([head and neck](../../05-tissues-and-anatomy/03-regional-anatomy/head-neck-and-spine.md)): mastoiditis behind, meningitis and brain abscess above, facial nerve palsy through the canal that runs across the middle ear, and cholesteatoma, a pocket of skin that erodes bone over years.

7. **Hearing loss is a disease of the whole person.** Untreated loss in older adults is associated with social isolation, depression and a roughly doubled rate of cognitive decline, and is estimated to be the largest potentially modifiable risk factor for dementia in mid-life (Lancet Commission, 2020); whether correcting it alters the trajectory is being tested. Falls, a [geriatric syndrome](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/geriatric-syndromes.md), rise with vestibular decline. Tinnitus, the perception of sound without a source, usually accompanies cochlear damage and is amplified by attention and distress, so its treatment is largely [behavioural](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/04-non-drug-therapeutics/behaviour-change-and-the-placebo-effect.md). Newborn hearing screening, now universal in many countries, finds the one or two per thousand born with significant loss in time for the critical period.

8. **Who else is involved.** [Paediatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/README.md) and [primary care](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/README.md) see nearly all otitis media and refer the persisting effusion; [neurology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/README.md) and [emergency medicine](../04-emergency-medicine/README.md) share acute vertigo, where the question is always whether this is the brainstem; audiology measures and fits; [geriatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/README.md) for falls and cognition; [oncology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/07-oncology/README.md) for the ototoxicity of platinum drugs; neurosurgery for the vestibular schwannoma on the eighth nerve; [occupational medicine](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/sports-occupational-and-lifestyle-medicine.md) for noise; and [rehabilitation](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/rehabilitation-and-disability.md) for the deaf child and the dizzy adult, because the brain can be trained to compensate for a labyrinth it cannot replace.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Impedance matching | Concentration of sound energy by the drum and ossicles so it enters the cochlear fluid instead of reflecting |
| Conductive hearing loss | Failure to transmit sound to the cochlea; an air–bone gap on the audiogram; usually correctable |
| Sensorineural hearing loss | Hair cell or nerve failure; no air–bone gap; usually permanent |
| Tonotopy | Place coding of frequency along the basilar membrane, high at the base, low at the apex |
| Presbycusis | Age-related high-frequency sensorineural loss from hair cell attrition |
| Cochlear implant | Electrode array stimulating the auditory nerve directly, bypassing dead hair cells |
| Vestibulo-ocular reflex | Eye movement equal and opposite to head movement; its examination localises vertigo |
| Nystagmus | Rhythmic eye oscillation revealing a vestibular or central imbalance |
| Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo | Displaced otolith crystals in a semicircular canal; brief positional vertigo cured by repositioning |
| Endolymphatic hydrops | Raised endolymph pressure, the proposed mechanism of Ménière's disease |
| Otitis media with effusion | Fluid in the middle ear from Eustachian tube dysfunction; conductive loss in children |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Hearing aids restore normal hearing."** They amplify into a damaged system; frequency resolution lost with hair cells is not restored, so noise remains hard.
- **"Vertigo is an ear problem."** Most is, but a posterior circulation stroke presents identically; the eye examination during head movement, not the symptom, separates them.
- **"Ear infections in children need antibiotics."** Most acute otitis media resolves without them and most effusions clear in months; treatment is for the persistent, the complicated and the very young.
- **"Deafness is a disability of the ear."** Untreated, it isolates, depresses and accelerates cognitive decline; its consequences are social and neurological.
