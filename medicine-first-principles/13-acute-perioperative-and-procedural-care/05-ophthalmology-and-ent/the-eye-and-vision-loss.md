## Essence

The eye is a camera attached to a piece of brain. Light is refracted by the cornea and lens onto the retina, where photoreceptors transduce it and three layers of neurons compress it into the optic nerve. Vision fails in one of four ways: the image is focused in the wrong place (refractive error), the media it passes through are opaque (cataract, corneal scar, haemorrhage), the pressure inside the globe damages the nerve (glaucoma), or the retina and nerve themselves fail (macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, vascular occlusion, detachment). Each failure has a physical signature on examination, and the diagnostic method is to follow the light from the front of the eye to the brain until the fault is found. Most blindness worldwide is from the first two causes and is reversible with a pair of spectacles or a fifteen-minute operation, which makes vision loss the clearest case in medicine of disability determined by access rather than by biology.

## Core tenets

1. **Refraction is geometry, and most visual impairment is a focusing error.** The cornea provides about two thirds of the eye's optical power (~43 of ~60 dioptres) and the lens the rest, adjusting its curvature to focus near objects (accommodation) until it stiffens with age (presbyopia, universal by the fifties). If the eye is too long or its optics too strong the image falls in front of the retina (myopia); too short, behind it (hypermetropia); an asymmetric cornea gives astigmatism ([optics](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/waves-sound-light-and-radiation.md)). Uncorrected refractive error is the leading cause of visual impairment worldwide and is corrected by a lens in front of the eye, on it, or by reshaping the cornea with a laser. Myopia prevalence is rising sharply in East Asian populations (over 80% of young adults in some cities, 2010s), with time outdoors in childhood the best-supported protective factor.

2. **Opaque media block the light, and the commonest is the lens.** The lens is a transparent protein crystal with no blood supply and no cell turnover; over decades its proteins oxidise, aggregate and scatter light, and the cataract that results is the leading cause of blindness globally (though not in high-income countries, where it is operated on). Diabetes, corticosteroids, ultraviolet exposure, trauma and smoking accelerate it. Cataract surgery replaces the lens with an acrylic implant through a 2–3 mm incision under topical or local anaesthetic in about fifteen minutes, and restores sight in nearly all cases: it is the most frequently performed operation in the world and among the most cost-effective. Corneal opacity from infection (trachoma, herpes), vitamin A deficiency or trauma is the other transmissive cause and is treated by transplantation of a tissue with the lowest rejection rate of any graft, because the cornea has no vessels.

3. **Intraocular pressure is aqueous production against drainage, and the optic nerve pays for the imbalance.** The ciliary body secretes aqueous humour at about 2–3 microlitres a minute into the posterior chamber; it flows through the pupil and drains through the trabecular meshwork into Schlemm's canal, and the balance sets a pressure of about 10–21 mmHg that keeps the globe's shape ([pressure and flow](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/fluids-pressure-and-flow.md)). In open-angle glaucoma drainage resistance rises slowly, pressure damages retinal ganglion cell axons at the optic nerve head, and the visual field is lost from the periphery inwards over years without symptoms, so it is found by screening pressure, the optic disc and the field, and treated with drops that reduce production (a β-blocker such as timolol) or increase outflow (a prostaglandin analogue such as latanoprost), by laser, or by making a new drain. In acute angle-closure the iris blocks the drainage angle in an anatomically crowded eye, pressure climbs to 50–80 mmHg within hours, and the patient has a painful red eye, a fixed mid-dilated pupil, haloes, vomiting and a nerve that is being infarcted: an emergency measured in hours.

4. **The retina is the body's most metabolically active tissue per gram, and it fails by ischaemia.** Photoreceptors consume oxygen at a rate exceeding the brain's, supplied by the choroid from behind and the retinal circulation from in front. Occlusion of the central retinal artery, usually by an embolus from a carotid plaque or the heart ([embolism](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/thrombosis-embolism-and-infarction.md)), produces sudden painless total loss of vision in one eye, a pale retina and a cherry-red macula, and the retina survives only about ninety minutes of complete ischaemia; it is a stroke of the eye and demands the same vascular work-up. Giant cell arteritis ([vasculitis](../../12-organ-system-specialties/09-rheumatology-and-clinical-immunology/connective-tissue-disease-and-vasculitis.md)) infarcts the optic nerve head in patients over fifty and blinds the second eye within days unless high-dose corticosteroids are started, so a raised inflammatory marker in an older patient with visual loss is treated before the biopsy. Retinal vein occlusion produces a slower loss with haemorrhages and oedema.

5. **Diabetes and hypertension write their history on the retinal vessels, and the eye is where microvascular disease is visible.** Chronic hyperglycaemia damages retinal capillary pericytes and endothelium ([diabetes](../../12-organ-system-specialties/05-endocrinology/diabetes-mellitus.md)): microaneurysms, haemorrhages and leakage (oedema at the macula, which blurs central vision), then capillary closure and ischaemia, which drives vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) release and new, fragile vessels that bleed into the vitreous and pull the retina off (proliferative retinopathy). Nearly all people with type 1 diabetes and most with type 2 have some retinopathy after twenty years. The treatment reverses the mechanism: laser destroys ischaemic peripheral retina to cut the VEGF drive, and intravitreal anti-VEGF antibodies (ranibizumab, aflibercept) block it directly and have transformed macular oedema and the wet form of age-related macular degeneration, in which the same growth factor drives choroidal vessels through a degenerating retinal pigment epithelium. Annual retinal photography is one of the few [screening](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/03-screening-and-prevention/screening-principles-and-biases.md) programmes with clear benefit: it has removed diabetes from first place as a cause of working-age blindness in England (2010s).

6. **A detached retina is a tissue separated from its oxygen supply, and the symptoms are flashes, floaters and a curtain.** The vitreous gel shrinks with age and can tear the retina as it peels away; fluid passes through the tear and lifts the neurosensory retina off the pigment epithelium that nourishes it. Photoreceptors begin to die within days, so the sequence of new flashes and floaters followed by a shadow spreading across the field is an emergency for repair by laser, gas or a surgical buckle before the macula lifts. The same symptoms with no tear are a benign posterior vitreous detachment, which is why every new floater is examined with a dilated pupil.

7. **The eye is the visible brain, and its examination is neurology.** The optic disc swells when intracranial pressure rises (papilloedema), pales when the nerve is damaged, and the pupil reflexes test the afferent and efferent arcs of the midbrain. Field defects localise lesions: one eye for the retina or nerve, the same half of both fields for the brain behind the chiasm ([stroke](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/stroke.md)), both temporal fields for the chiasm compressed by a pituitary tumour. Optic neuritis is often the first attack of [multiple sclerosis](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/demyelinating-and-neuromuscular-disease.md). The developing visual system has a critical period: a child whose eye is deprived of a focused image (by a squint, a cataract, an uncorrected refractive difference) in the first years loses the cortical connections for that eye permanently (amblyopia), so paediatric eye problems are treated by the age of about seven or not at all ([child development](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/growth-development-and-the-child-as-patient.md)).

8. **Who else is involved.** [Endocrinology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/05-endocrinology/README.md) and [primary care](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/README.md) control the glucose and pressure that the retina records; [neurology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/README.md) shares the optic nerve, the fields and the pupils; [cardiology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/README.md) and vascular surgery find the source of the retinal embolus; [rheumatology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/09-rheumatology-and-clinical-immunology/README.md) shares uveitis and arteritis; [paediatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/README.md) screens the newborn and the squinting child; [geriatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/geriatric-syndromes.md) because visual loss causes falls, isolation and depression; [emergency medicine](../04-emergency-medicine/README.md) sees the red eye and the sudden loss first; and [global health](../../15-medicine-in-society/03-populations-and-the-future/global-health-and-pandemics.md), because the cheapest effective operations in medicine are the ones least available where most blindness is.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Refractive error | Image focused in front of (myopia), behind (hypermetropia) or unevenly on (astigmatism) the retina |
| Accommodation and presbyopia | Lens curvature change for near focus, and its loss with age |
| Cataract | Opacification of the lens by protein aggregation; the leading cause of blindness worldwide |
| Intraocular pressure | Balance of aqueous production and trabecular drainage; normal about 10–21 mmHg |
| Open-angle versus angle-closure glaucoma | Slow drainage resistance and silent field loss versus sudden iris block, pain and rapid nerve damage |
| Central retinal artery occlusion | Embolic infarction of the retina; sudden painless loss, a stroke of the eye |
| Diabetic retinopathy | Microvascular damage, leak, ischaemia and VEGF-driven new vessels |
| Anti-VEGF therapy | Intravitreal antibodies blocking vascular endothelial growth factor; for macular oedema and wet macular degeneration |
| Retinal detachment | Separation of the neurosensory retina from its pigment epithelium; flashes, floaters, a curtain |
| Amblyopia | Permanent cortical visual loss from a deprived eye during the critical period of development |
| Papilloedema | Optic disc swelling from raised intracranial pressure |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Glaucoma is high eye pressure."** It is optic nerve damage with a characteristic field loss; pressure is the main modifiable risk factor, but a third or more of patients have pressures in the normal range, and many with high pressure never develop it.
- **"A painless loss of vision can wait until morning."** Central retinal artery occlusion, arteritic optic neuropathy and retinal detachment are painless and lose tissue by the hour.
- **"Cataracts must be 'ripe' before surgery."** The indication is the patient's visual function; modern small-incision surgery is done when the cataract interferes with life, and waiting makes it harder.
- **"Children grow out of a squint."** A constant squint deprives one eye and causes amblyopia that is irreversible after the critical period; it needs assessment early.
