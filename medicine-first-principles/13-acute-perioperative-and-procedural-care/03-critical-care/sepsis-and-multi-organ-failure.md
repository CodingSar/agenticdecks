## Essence

Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection (the 2016 consensus definition). The emphasis is deliberate: the microbe starts it, but the damage is done by the body's own [inflammatory cascade](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/02-innate-immunity/complement-phagocytes-and-the-inflammatory-cascade.md) running without its usual limits. The same response that walls off a local abscess, released systemically, dilates and leaks every capillary bed, clots the microcirculation, poisons mitochondria and raises oxygen demand, so organs that were never infected fail in sequence. Sepsis accounts for an estimated 11 million deaths a year worldwide (2017 estimate), about a fifth of all deaths, and most of them in low-income settings. Treatment has two halves that cannot substitute for each other: kill and drain the source, and support the organs through the hours and days it takes the response to subside.

## Core tenets

1. **The host response, not the organism, is the disease.** Pathogen-associated molecular patterns (endotoxin, peptidoglycan, viral nucleic acid) and the damage-associated patterns released by injured cells bind [pattern recognition receptors](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/02-innate-immunity/barriers-and-pattern-recognition.md) on macrophages and endothelium, releasing tumour necrosis factor, interleukins 1 and 6, and activating complement, coagulation and the kinin system ([cytokines](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/02-innate-immunity/cytokines-interferons-and-nk-cells.md)). Locally this is [acute inflammation](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/acute-inflammation.md) and it is protective. Systemically the same mediators act on every vessel at once. Anti-inflammatory counter-regulation follows, and many patients who survive the early storm enter an immunosuppressed phase in which secondary infections and reactivated viruses kill them. Blocking single mediators (anti-endotoxin, anti-tumour necrosis factor) has repeatedly failed in trials, because the network is redundant and the timing of each patient's response is unknown.

2. **Sepsis is distributive shock with a leaking, clotting microcirculation.** Nitric oxide synthase induced in vascular smooth muscle dilates arterioles and makes them unresponsive to catecholamines; the endothelial glycocalyx is shed and the [capillary barrier](../../06-physiology/03-cardiovascular-system/microcirculation-and-capillary-exchange.md) leaks protein and water into tissue, so intravascular volume falls while the patient swells. Endothelial tissue factor and impaired anticoagulant pathways (protein C, antithrombin) activate [coagulation](../../06-physiology/08-blood/haemostasis-and-coagulation.md) in the microvessels: platelets and fibrinogen are consumed, microthrombi obstruct flow, and in its extreme the picture is disseminated intravascular coagulation, bleeding and clotting at once. The result is heterogeneous perfusion: some capillaries are shunting with high flow while their neighbours are stopped, so tissue is hypoxic even when the global delivery is normal or high. Early septic shock is a warm, flushed, hyperdynamic patient with a wide pulse pressure; later the myocardium itself is depressed by cytokines and the patient goes cold.

3. **Cells fail even when oxygen arrives: cytopathic hypoxia.** Mitochondrial respiration is inhibited by nitric oxide and reactive oxygen species, and cells down-regulate metabolism in what may be a protective hibernation ([cellular stress response](../../03-cell-biology/03-cells-in-community/the-cellular-stress-response.md)). This is why tissue oxygen tension can be normal or high in sepsis while lactate climbs, why organs that failed show surprisingly little cell death at autopsy, and why organ failure in survivors is largely reversible. Lactate in sepsis is partly anaerobic and partly driven by adrenergic stimulation of glycolysis, so it marks severity and response to treatment rather than oxygen debt alone.

4. **Organs fail in a recognisable order, and the count predicts death.** The lung fails first and most often ([acute respiratory distress syndrome](respiratory-failure-and-mechanical-ventilation.md)), from endothelial leak into alveoli; the kidney ([acute kidney injury](../../12-organ-system-specialties/03-nephrology/acute-kidney-injury.md)) from microcirculatory failure and tubular stress rather than frank necrosis; the brain (encephalopathy, delirium) from inflammatory and metabolic effects; the liver (cholestasis, falling synthetic function); the gut (ileus, a leaky [barrier](../../06-physiology/06-gastrointestinal-system/the-gut-barrier-and-microbiome.md) that lets bacteria and endotoxin into the portal blood and feeds the fire); the marrow and coagulation; and the heart. The Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score counts dysfunction across six systems, and mortality rises steeply with each organ added, from under 10% with one to well over half with four or more. The definition of sepsis is operationalised as an acute rise in this score of two or more points with infection.

5. **Time to effective antimicrobial and source control is the dose that matters.** Observational data show mortality rising with each hour of delay in effective antimicrobial therapy in septic shock, so therapy is started empirically on the probable source and local resistance patterns before cultures are back ([antimicrobials](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/antimicrobials.md)), then narrowed or stopped once they are, which is where [stewardship](../../12-organ-system-specialties/08-infectious-diseases/antimicrobial-stewardship.md) begins. Antimicrobials cannot penetrate an abscess, an obstructed and infected kidney or bile duct, dead bowel or an infected prosthesis: these need a drain, a stent, a resection or removal ([the acute abdomen](../01-surgery/the-acute-abdomen.md)), and a patient who does not improve on the right drug has an undrained source until proved otherwise. Blood, urine and site cultures taken before the first dose are what make narrowing possible later.

6. **Support follows the shock leaf, with a few sepsis-specific turns.** Fluid restores the volume lost to the leak but only while the patient is [fluid responsive](shock-and-haemodynamic-support.md); noradrenaline restores the pressure that induced nitric oxide has removed; vasopressin is added when catecholamine responsiveness fades. Low-dose hydrocortisone shortens shock in patients who need escalating vasopressors, probably by restoring vascular responsiveness in a relatively insufficient adrenal response, with a modest or absent effect on mortality in the large trials. Lactate clearance, urine output, mentation and skin mottling, not a protocolised central venous saturation, guide the first hours; the protocol tested in 2001 that seemed to halve mortality was not better than good usual care when retested in three large trials in 2014–2015, a lesson in how [trials](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/02-evidence-based-medicine/randomized-controlled-trials.md) correct physiology-based enthusiasm.

7. **Recognition is the step most often missed, because early sepsis looks like many things.** The bedside screen is physiological: a rapid respiratory rate, altered mentation and a low blood pressure (the quick SOFA) identify the infected patient at risk of dying, and the respiratory rate is the most sensitive and most often unrecorded sign. Fever may be absent in the old, the immunosuppressed and the very sick, and the white cell count may be low, normal or high. The patients who die of sepsis are often those on general wards whose deterioration was recorded but not acted on, which is why early-warning scores and rapid response systems exist ([patient safety](../../15-medicine-in-society/01-health-systems/patient-safety-and-quality-improvement.md)).

8. **Who else is involved.** [Infectious diseases and microbiology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/08-infectious-diseases/README.md) choose, narrow and stop the antimicrobials and chase the source; [surgery](../01-surgery/README.md), interventional radiology and urology drain it; [nephrology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/03-nephrology/README.md) shares the kidney; [haematology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/06-haematology/README.md) manages the coagulopathy and the neutropenic septic patient on chemotherapy; [obstetrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/pregnancy-and-its-complications.md), because maternal sepsis is a leading cause of maternal death and physiology in pregnancy hides it; [paediatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/common-childhood-illness.md), where sepsis presents with non-specific signs and compensates until it collapses; and [palliative care](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/death-dying-and-end-of-life-decisions.md), because sepsis in a patient with no reserve is the common final illness, and the decision not to escalate is made with them.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sepsis | Life-threatening organ dysfunction from a dysregulated host response to infection |
| Septic shock | Sepsis with hypotension needing vasopressors and raised lactate despite fluid |
| Pathogen- and damage-associated molecular patterns | Microbial and host-injury molecules that trigger innate immune receptors |
| Cytokine cascade | Tumour necrosis factor, interleukins and their downstream mediators acting systemically |
| Glycocalyx shedding and capillary leak | Loss of the endothelial barrier; volume moves into tissue |
| Disseminated intravascular coagulation | Systemic activation of clotting with consumption of platelets and factors; thrombosis and bleeding |
| Cytopathic hypoxia | Failure of cellular oxygen use despite adequate delivery; mitochondrial inhibition |
| Sequential Organ Failure Assessment | Score of dysfunction across respiration, coagulation, liver, circulation, brain and kidney |
| Source control | Drainage, debridement, removal or diversion of the focus of infection |
| Immunoparalysis | Late suppressed immune state after the inflammatory phase; secondary infection |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Sepsis is a bloodstream infection."** Blood cultures are positive in only a minority; sepsis is defined by organ dysfunction from the host response, whatever the site, and a sterile pancreatitis can produce an identical picture.
- **"A patient without fever is not septic."** The old, the immunosuppressed and the sickest are often afebrile or hypothermic; respiratory rate and mentation are more reliable early signs.
- **"Antibiotics are the treatment."** They are half of it; an undrained abscess, an obstructed infected kidney or dead bowel will kill on the right antibiotic, and support is what keeps the patient alive meanwhile.
- **"Organs that fail in sepsis are dead."** Most failure is functional and reversible; survivors regain kidney, liver and lung function, which is why support is worth providing.
