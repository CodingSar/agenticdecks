# Respiratory failure and mechanical ventilation

## Essence

The lung does two separable jobs: it loads oxygen onto blood and it unloads carbon dioxide. Respiratory failure is the failure of either, and the two fail for different reasons, show different gases and need different support. Oxygenation fails when blood passes alveoli that are collapsed, flooded or unventilated (shunt and low ventilation–perfusion ratio); the arterial oxygen falls while carbon dioxide, far more diffusible and cleared by any ventilated lung, stays normal or low. Ventilation fails when the bellows cannot move enough gas (a depressed drive, weak muscles, an obstructed or stiff system, or an exhausted patient); carbon dioxide rises and oxygen falls with it. A mechanical ventilator replaces the bellows and can raise the pressure that holds alveoli open, but it cannot make a diseased lung exchange gas, and the pressures and volumes it applies injure the lung it is meant to rest. The whole of modern ventilation is the management of that trade-off while the cause is treated.

## Core tenets

1. **Two failures, two gases.** Type 1 (hypoxaemic) failure is a low arterial oxygen tension with a normal or low carbon dioxide; its mechanism is shunt and ventilation–perfusion mismatch ([V/Q matching](../../06-physiology/04-respiratory-system/ventilation-perfusion-matching.md)) from pneumonia, oedema, collapse, embolism or the acute respiratory distress syndrome. Type 2 (hypercapnic) failure is a raised carbon dioxide; its mechanism is alveolar ventilation below the body's production, from a depressed centre (opioids, brain injury), a failed pump (neuromuscular disease, exhaustion, chest wall), or a load the pump cannot meet (severe obstruction, as in [exacerbated chronic obstructive pulmonary disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/02-pulmonology/asthma-and-copd.md)). The arterial blood gas and the alveolar gas equation separate them: a raised alveolar–arterial oxygen gradient means the lung is the problem; a normal gradient with hypoxaemia means the ventilation is.

2. **Shunt does not respond to oxygen; low ventilation–perfusion does.** Blood passing an alveolus with no ventilation cannot pick up oxygen however rich the inspired gas, because it never meets it; blood passing a poorly ventilated alveolus will, once the inspired fraction is high enough to saturate it ([gas exchange](../../06-physiology/04-respiratory-system/gas-exchange-and-transport.md)). So an oxygen-refractory hypoxaemia means true shunt (consolidation, collapse, flooded alveoli, an intracardiac defect) and its treatment is to reopen or bypass the units, not to raise the oxygen further. Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) recruits collapsed alveoli and keeps them open through expiration, converting shunt into ventilated lung; its cost is raised intrathoracic pressure, reduced venous return and overdistension of the lung that was already open.

3. **The acute respiratory distress syndrome is inflammatory oedema, and it behaves like a small, wet, heavy lung.** Any severe insult, pulmonary (pneumonia, aspiration, inhalation) or systemic (sepsis, trauma, pancreatitis, transfusion), can trigger diffuse alveolar damage: neutrophils and [inflammatory mediators](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/02-innate-immunity/complement-phagocytes-and-the-inflammatory-cascade.md) injure the endothelium and epithelium, protein-rich fluid floods the alveoli, surfactant fails and the dependent lung collapses under its own weight. The result is bilateral opacities, severe shunt and a lung whose ventilable volume may be a third of normal, so that a "normal" tidal volume is a huge one for the lung that is actually open.

4. **The ventilator injures the lung by stretch, and protective ventilation is the main thing that has improved survival.** Alveoli overdistended by large volumes or high transpulmonary pressures release cytokines, leak and rupture (volutrauma and barotrauma); alveoli that collapse and reopen with each breath are sheared (atelectrauma); the inflammation spills into the circulation and injures other organs (biotrauma). A landmark trial (ARDS Network, 2000) showed that limiting tidal volume to about 6 mL/kg of predicted body weight and plateau pressure to about 30 cmH2O reduced mortality by about a quarter compared with 12 mL/kg, accepting a higher carbon dioxide to do so. Lung-protective ventilation, prone positioning for the most severe cases (which recruits the dorsal lung and evens out the stress), and avoiding fluid excess are the interventions with evidence; most drugs tried for the syndrome have failed.

5. **The ventilator alters the circulation, and the heart and lung must be read together.** Positive pressure raises intrathoracic pressure, which reduces venous return and right ventricular preload while raising right ventricular afterload through compressed pulmonary vessels, so a hypovolaemic or right-heart-failing patient can collapse at intubation. The same mechanism helps the failing left ventricle by reducing its afterload and venous return, which is why non-invasive positive pressure treats cardiogenic pulmonary oedema so well.

6. **Non-invasive support treats the pump; invasive support treats the lung.** A tight mask delivering pressure support offloads fatigued respiratory muscles and reduces the work of breathing; in exacerbated obstructive disease with hypercapnia and in cardiogenic oedema it reduces intubation and death. Both fail, and delay harms, when the lung itself is the problem and the patient is tiring, so the decision to intubate is made on trajectory (rising carbon dioxide, rising rate, falling consciousness) rather than on a single gas. Once intubated, the sedation needed to tolerate the tube and the ventilator becomes its own [problem](organ-support-and-icu-syndromes.md).

7. **Weaning is a test of the balance between load and capacity, and the tube itself is a load.** Each day the question is whether the cause has resolved enough for the patient to breathe alone: a spontaneous breathing trial on minimal support shows whether rate, volume, gas exchange and distress stay acceptable. Failure is usually a failed pump (weak, wasted, deconditioned muscles after days of ventilation; a weak heart that cannot meet the demand of breathing) rather than a failed lung. Prolonged ventilation brings ventilator-associated pneumonia, diaphragm atrophy and delirium, so daily sedation interruption and breathing trials shorten it; a tracheostomy, by removing the laryngeal tube, lets the patient be awake, swallow and wean slowly. When the lung cannot exchange gas at any safe setting, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation takes over gas exchange entirely for days to weeks, a bridge whose far side must be recovery or transplantation.

8. **Who else is involved.** [Pulmonology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/02-pulmonology/README.md) supplies most of the causes and takes back the patient who needs long-term ventilation or has [pulmonary vascular disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/02-pulmonology/pulmonary-vascular-disease-and-respiratory-failure.md); [anaesthesia](../02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/airway-and-ventilation-management.md) places the tube; [cardiology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/heart-failure.md) for the heart that cannot be separated from the lung; [neurology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/demyelinating-and-neuromuscular-disease.md) for the Guillain–Barré syndrome and myasthenic crisis that fail the pump; [infectious diseases](../../12-organ-system-specialties/08-infectious-diseases/README.md) for the pneumonia and the ventilator-associated one; Ear, Nose and Throat (ENT) surgery for the [tracheostomy](../05-ophthalmology-and-ent/nose-throat-and-head-and-neck.md); [rehabilitation](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/rehabilitation-and-disability.md) for the wasted survivor; and, since 2020, [public health](../../15-medicine-in-society/03-populations-and-the-future/global-health-and-pandemics.md), for whom ventilator capacity became a national variable.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Type 1 respiratory failure | Hypoxaemia with normal or low carbon dioxide; a gas-exchange (lung) problem |
| Type 2 respiratory failure | Hypercapnia; a ventilation (pump, drive or load) problem |
| Shunt | Blood passing unventilated alveoli; hypoxaemia that does not respond to oxygen |
| Acute respiratory distress syndrome | Acute diffuse inflammatory lung injury with bilateral opacities and severe shunt |
| Positive end-expiratory pressure | Pressure held at end-expiration to recruit and stabilise alveoli |
| Ventilator-induced lung injury | Damage from overdistension, cyclic collapse and the inflammation they release |
| Lung-protective ventilation | Low tidal volume and limited plateau pressure, accepting higher carbon dioxide |
| Non-invasive ventilation | Pressure support through a mask; offloads the pump without a tube |
| Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation | External gas exchange through a membrane when the lung cannot do it at safe settings |

## Common misconceptions

- **"If the oxygen saturation is low, give more oxygen."** Shunt does not respond to inspired oxygen; refractory hypoxaemia needs recruitment (pressure, position) or bypass, and excess oxygen has its own toxicity.
- **"A normal-sized breath is a safe breath."** In the acute respiratory distress syndrome the ventilable lung may be a third of normal; a physiological tidal volume overstretches it. Volume is scaled to predicted, not actual, weight.
- **"The ventilator treats the lung."** It replaces the pump and can hold alveoli open; it does nothing to the pneumonia, oedema or inflammation, and it injures the lung while waiting for them to resolve.
- **"Patients fail to wean because their lungs are still bad."** Most weaning failure is a weak pump (wasted muscle, a weak heart, delirium, sedation), which is why daily trials and minimising sedation matter.
