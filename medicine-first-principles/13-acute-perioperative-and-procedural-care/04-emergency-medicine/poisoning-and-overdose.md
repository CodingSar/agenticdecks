## Essence

Poisoning is pharmacology past the top of the dose–response curve. Every drug, and most chemicals, has a mechanism, a kinetic profile and a toxic threshold ([toxicology](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/05-drug-development-and-toxicology/toxicology-and-poisoning.md)); an overdose is that mechanism amplified until it becomes the disease, with absorption, distribution and elimination following the same rules as a therapeutic dose but often saturated or delayed. The emergency physician usually does not know what was taken, how much or when, so the approach is the one used for every undifferentiated patient: support the physiology by [ABCDE](triage-and-the-abcde-approach.md), read the mechanism from the pattern of signs (the toxidrome), and reserve the few true antidotes for the poisons whose mechanism they reverse. Most poisoned patients survive with supportive care alone; the ones who die are those whose toxin acts silently for hours before the damage appears.

## Core tenets

1. **The toxidrome is the receptor pharmacology read from the bedside.** A poison acting on a receptor system produces the signs of that system's over- or under-activity ([receptor theory](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/02-pharmacodynamics/receptor-theory-agonists-and-antagonists.md), [autonomic nervous system](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/the-autonomic-nervous-system.md)). Opioids: pinpoint pupils, slow breathing, depressed consciousness. Anticholinergics: dilated pupils, dry hot skin, tachycardia, delirium, retention. Cholinergics (organophosphates): salivation, lacrimation, urination, diarrhoea, bronchorrhoea, bradycardia, fasciculation. Sympathomimetics (cocaine, amfetamines): dilated pupils, tachycardia, hypertension, sweating, agitation, hyperthermia. Sedative-hypnotics: depressed consciousness with preserved pupils and only mild respiratory depression. The pattern narrows the cause before any test, and the exceptions (a mixed overdose, a co-ingested alcohol) are the rule in practice.

2. **The kinetics of an overdose are not the kinetics of a dose.** Absorption is slowed by gastric stasis from the drug itself (anticholinergics, opioids) and by sustained-release formulations, so peak effect may come hours after ingestion ([absorption](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/01-pharmacokinetics/absorption-and-bioavailability.md)). Elimination saturates: paracetamol, salicylate, phenytoin and ethanol switch from first-order to zero-order kinetics at high concentrations, so the half-life lengthens as the dose rises ([elimination](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/01-pharmacokinetics/elimination-half-life-and-dosing.md)). A level taken too early is falsely reassuring, and a patient who looks well at two hours may be in liver failure at two days.

3. **Supportive care is the main treatment, and the airway is the main cause of death.** Most deaths from overdose, in and out of hospital, are from respiratory depression and aspiration in an unprotected airway. Supporting breathing and protecting the airway ([airway management](../02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/airway-and-ventilation-management.md)), maintaining the circulation, controlling seizures and temperature, and waiting for elimination treats the majority of poisonings, because the body clears most drugs within a day or two if it is kept alive. The resuscitation of a poisoned patient is prolonged where the heart has stopped from a reversible toxic cause, because the myocardium is healthy and the toxin will clear.

4. **Decontamination and enhanced elimination help in a few cases and harm in others.** Activated charcoal adsorbs many drugs in the gut and reduces absorption if given within about an hour of ingestion, but not metals, alcohols or corrosives, and it is aspirated by a patient who cannot protect their airway. Gastric lavage has little evidence and real harm. Urinary alkalinisation ion-traps salicylate; haemodialysis removes small, water-soluble, poorly protein-bound toxins with a small volume of distribution (salicylate, lithium, methanol, ethylene glycol, metformin) by the same [diffusion](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/diffusion-osmosis-and-transport.md) that clears urea in renal failure. Each technique has an indication that follows from the toxin's kinetics, not from the fact of poisoning.

5. **Antidotes reverse a mechanism, and there are few of them.** Naloxone displaces opioids from μ-receptors and restores breathing within minutes but outlasts few opioids, so the patient who wakes may stop breathing again. Flumazenil reverses benzodiazepines at the risk of seizures in the dependent. Atropine blocks the muscarinic flood of organophosphate poisoning while oximes reactivate the enzyme. N-acetylcysteine replenishes glutathione so that the toxic paracetamol metabolite is conjugated instead of binding hepatocyte proteins, and it prevents nearly all liver failure if started within about eight hours, which is why paracetamol has a treatment line on a concentration–time graph (Rumack–Matthew, 1975) rather than a toxidrome. Fomepizole blocks alcohol dehydrogenase so that methanol and ethylene glycol are excreted before they are turned into formate and oxalate. Sodium bicarbonate narrows the QRS in sodium-channel blockade from tricyclic antidepressants. Digoxin-specific antibodies bind the drug. Each antidote is the pharmacology of the poison inverted ([specificity](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#specificity-and-recognition)).

6. **The silent poisons are the dangerous ones.** Paracetamol, iron, sustained-release calcium-channel blockers, toxic alcohols, sulfonylureas, colchicine and paraquat produce few early signs while the injury (hepatic necrosis, gut and liver damage, delayed cardiovascular collapse, acidosis and blindness, delayed hypoglycaemia, multi-organ failure, pulmonary fibrosis) is being set in motion. For these the history, the level and the arithmetic of dose per kilogram decide treatment in an asymptomatic patient, and observation is the intervention. Carbon monoxide, which binds haemoglobin with over 200 times the affinity of oxygen and leaves the saturation monitor reading normal, is the environmental example ([toxic injury](../../08-pathology/04-genetic-metabolic-and-environmental-disease/environmental-and-toxic-injury.md)).

7. **Most adult poisoning is self-harm and most childhood poisoning is exploration, and the second patient is the mind.** Deliberate self-poisoning is the commonest reason for a poisoning attendance in high-income countries and the commonest method of suicide attempt; a medically trivial overdose may carry a high risk of later death by suicide, and the psychosocial assessment once the patient is medically safe is part of the treatment, not an afterthought ([mood disorders](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/03-psychiatry/mood-and-anxiety-disorders.md)). Recreational overdose is the acute face of [addiction](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/03-psychiatry/addiction-and-substance-use.md); the opioid epidemic in North America (over 80,000 opioid-involved deaths in the United States in 2021) made naloxone a public health tool. In a child, an ingestion raises the question of supervision and [safeguarding](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/adolescent-health-and-safeguarding.md); in an adult, an occupational or environmental exposure raises a public health one.

8. **Who else is involved.** Poison information centres supply the toxicology that no clinician can carry in memory; [critical care](../03-critical-care/README.md) ventilates the sedated and supports the poisoned heart; [nephrology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/03-nephrology/README.md) dialyses the dialysable; [hepatology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/04-gastroenterology-and-hepatology/liver-disease.md) and transplant surgery for paracetamol liver failure; [cardiology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/arrhythmias.md) for the arrhythmias of sodium- and potassium-channel blockade; [psychiatry](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/03-psychiatry/README.md) for the self-harm and the dependence; [paediatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/README.md) for the child; [public health](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/01-epidemiology/surveillance-and-outbreak-investigation.md) for the cluster of poisonings that signals a contaminated supply.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Toxidrome | A cluster of signs produced by a class of poison acting on one receptor system |
| Zero-order kinetics | Elimination at a fixed rate when enzymes are saturated; half-life rises with dose |
| Supportive care | Airway, breathing, circulation, temperature and seizure control while the body eliminates the toxin |
| Activated charcoal | Adsorbent that reduces gut absorption if given early to a patient who can protect their airway |
| Enhanced elimination | Alkalinisation or dialysis for toxins whose kinetics permit it |
| Antidote | An agent that reverses a specific mechanism: receptor antagonist, enzyme blocker, metabolic substrate, binding antibody |
| Treatment nomogram | Concentration against time since ingestion, used for paracetamol to decide on antidote |
| Silent poison | A toxin with few early signs and delayed organ injury; treated on dose and level, not symptoms |
| Deliberate self-poisoning | Overdose as self-harm; the commonest adult poisoning and a marker of suicide risk |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Every poison has an antidote."** A handful do; most poisoning is treated by supporting the patient while the body clears the drug.
- **"A patient who looks well after an overdose is safe."** Paracetamol, iron, sustained-release preparations and toxic alcohols injure silently for hours; the level and the dose, not the appearance, decide.
- **"Empty the stomach."** Gastric lavage has no proven benefit and causes aspiration; charcoal within an hour, in a patient with a protected airway, is the only decontamination with a case.
- **"A small overdose is a small problem."** The medical risk and the psychiatric risk are independent; a trivial ingestion may carry a high risk of later suicide.
