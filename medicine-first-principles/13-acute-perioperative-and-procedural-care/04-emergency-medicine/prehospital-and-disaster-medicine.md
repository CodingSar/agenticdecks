## Essence

Most time-critical physiology fails outside a hospital, and the interval before the hospital is where survival from cardiac arrest, haemorrhage and stroke is largely decided. Prehospital medicine is the application of [triage and ABCDE](triage-and-the-abcde-approach.md) with fewer hands, less equipment, no laboratory and a moving floor, and its central question is what to do at the scene and what to defer to the hospital, which is a question about time constants. Disaster medicine is the same discipline when the number of patients exceeds the system's capacity: the goal shifts from the best outcome for each patient to the greatest number of survivors, and triage becomes explicit [rationing](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#harm-and-benefit). In both, the treatment is the system (the dispatcher, the bystander, the ambulance, the network of hospitals) as much as any single act, and its performance is measured in minutes.

## Core tenets

1. **The system is the treatment, and its performance is a time.** For cardiac arrest, survival depends on the minutes to compressions and to the first shock, so the interventions that work are public training, dispatcher-assisted compressions, public-access defibrillators and the density of responders ([resuscitation](resuscitation-and-cardiac-arrest.md)). For stroke and myocardial infarction, the measure is minutes from onset to reperfusion, and the ambulance is a diagnostic and routing instrument (a 12-lead electrocardiogram transmitted ahead, a stroke screen that bypasses the nearest hospital for a thrombectomy centre). For trauma, the measure is time to haemorrhage control, and the system is a trauma network that takes the severely injured past the local hospital to a major trauma centre, which has been shown to reduce mortality by around a fifth where it has been introduced (England, 2012 onwards).

2. **Do at the scene what cannot wait, and nothing that can.** The "golden hour" is a slogan rather than a measured constant, but the principle behind it is real: an exsanguinating patient needs a surgeon, not a longer stay in a field. Life-saving interventions that are time-critical and feasible at the scene are performed (airway manoeuvres, decompression of a tension pneumothorax, a tourniquet, a pelvic binder, adrenaline for anaphylaxis, glucose for hypoglycaemia, naloxone); those that are time-consuming and better done in hospital are deferred (a definitive airway in a patient who is oxygenating, intravenous access when the hospital is minutes away, splinting every fracture). The choice between "stay and play" and "scoop and run" is made per patient from the clock on their physiology and the distance to the care they need.

3. **Prehospital physiology is read with eyes and hands, and it is often enough.** Without a laboratory, the paramedic has the respiratory rate, the pulse, the skin, the capillary refill, the mental state, the pupils, a pulse oximeter, a glucometer, a 12-lead electrocardiogram and increasingly a hand-held ultrasound. These are the inputs to [triage](triage-and-the-abcde-approach.md) and they predict mortality nearly as well as the hospital's tests in the first hour. Field triage tools for trauma combine physiology, anatomy of injury and mechanism to decide which hospital, and their known weakness is the compensating patient, especially the old, whose normal numbers hide a lethal injury.

4. **Mass casualty triage inverts the usual ethic.** When casualties outnumber resources, the aim is to save the greatest number, so the unsalvageable are not treated first and the walking are sorted last. Sieve-and-sort systems assign categories by a few seconds of assessment (can walk; breathing; respiratory rate; pulse or capillary refill) and are repeated as resources arrive. The hardest category is the expectant: patients who would consume resources that would save several others, and who receive comfort rather than resuscitation. This is [priority setting](../../15-medicine-in-society/01-health-systems/health-economics-and-priority-setting.md) at its most explicit, and the rules are written in advance precisely so that they are not improvised under duress.

5. **Disasters have a predictable sequence of medical problems.** The first hours are trauma: crush injury, haemorrhage, burns, and in an earthquake the rhabdomyolysis and [hyperkalaemia](../../12-organ-system-specialties/03-nephrology/electrolyte-and-acid-base-disorders.md) of trapped limbs, which need fluid before release and dialysis after. The first days are the interruption of ordinary care: insulin, dialysis, oxygen, labour, the chronic diseases that the hospital was treating. The first weeks are infection from displacement, water and crowding ([chain of infection](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/01-microbes-and-infection/transmission-and-the-chain-of-infection.md)), though epidemics after natural disasters are less common than feared and dead bodies are not their source. Mental health effects persist for years. Preparedness means stockpiles, plans for the surge in each phase, and [surveillance](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/01-epidemiology/surveillance-and-outbreak-investigation.md) to detect the second and third phases early.

6. **Chemical, biological and radiological incidents add a step before ABCDE: protect the responder.** A contaminated patient contaminates the rescuer, so decontamination (removal of clothing removes most of the contaminant; washing removes most of the rest) and personal protective equipment come before treatment, and the toxidrome ([poisoning](poisoning-and-overdose.md)) identifies the agent. Radiation injury is dose-dependent and delayed ([radiation biology](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/04-non-drug-therapeutics/radiotherapy-and-radiation-biology.md)): the lymphocyte count over the first days predicts the marrow dose, and the acute problems are the conventional injuries from the blast. A pandemic is the disaster that arrives slowly and lasts longest, and its triage question is the allocation of ventilators and intensive care beds ([global health and pandemics](../../15-medicine-in-society/03-populations-and-the-future/global-health-and-pandemics.md)).

7. **Environment is a cause and a treatment.** Hypothermia, heat illness, drowning, altitude and envenomation are the diseases of the field ([thermoregulation](../../06-physiology/01-integrative-principles/thermoregulation-and-energy-balance.md)). Hypothermia protects the brain in arrest but defeats clotting in haemorrhage; heat stroke is a failure of thermoregulation that kills by multi-organ failure and is treated by cooling within minutes. Climate change is increasing heat deaths, floods and wildfires, so disaster medicine is becoming a branch of [planetary health](../../15-medicine-in-society/03-populations-and-the-future/environmental-and-planetary-health.md).

8. **Who else is involved.** Prehospital care is delivered mostly by paramedics and dispatchers, with physicians for the sickest; [emergency medicine](README.md) receives and often leads it; [anaesthesia](../02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/README.md) and [critical care](../03-critical-care/README.md) staff the retrieval and transfer services that move critically ill patients between hospitals; [surgery](../01-surgery/README.md) defines where the trauma centre is; [cardiology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/README.md) and [neurology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/stroke.md) define the reperfusion networks; [infectious diseases](../../12-organ-system-specialties/08-infectious-diseases/README.md) and public health run the outbreak response; [psychiatry](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/03-psychiatry/README.md) the aftermath; and the [health system](../../15-medicine-in-society/01-health-systems/organisation-and-financing-of-health-systems.md) decides whether any of it is funded before the disaster rather than after.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Chain of survival | The sequence of system responses whose timing decides outcome from cardiac arrest |
| Trauma network | A regional system routing the severely injured past local hospitals to a major trauma centre |
| Scoop and run versus stay and play | Minimal scene time with rapid transport versus treatment at the scene; chosen per patient |
| Field triage | Sorting by physiology, anatomy and mechanism to choose destination and priority |
| Mass casualty incident | Casualties exceeding resources, so that triage aims at the greatest number of survivors |
| Expectant category | Casualties whose treatment would cost more lives than it saved; given comfort care |
| Surge capacity | The ability of a system to expand beds, staff and supplies beyond normal demand |
| Decontamination | Removal of a chemical, biological or radiological agent from a patient before treatment |
| Crush syndrome | Rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalaemia and renal failure after release of a compressed limb |
| Retrieval medicine | Critical care delivered during transport between scene or hospital and definitive care |

## Common misconceptions

- **"More treatment at the scene is better."** Every minute at the scene is a minute away from the surgeon or the catheter laboratory; the scene is for what cannot wait.
- **"Epidemics follow natural disasters."** They are uncommon and come from displacement and water, not from the dead; the predictable killers are untreated chronic disease and interrupted care.
- **"Disaster triage is ordinary triage done faster."** It changes the goal from the individual to the population and withholds treatment from some who could be saved alone.
- **"The golden hour is a physiological constant."** It is a useful slogan; the real time constants differ by injury, from minutes for the airway to many hours for some fractures.
