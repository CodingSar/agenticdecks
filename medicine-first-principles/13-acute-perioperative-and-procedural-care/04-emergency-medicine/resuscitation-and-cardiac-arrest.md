# Resuscitation and cardiac arrest

## Essence

Cardiac arrest is the cessation of effective cardiac output: no pulse, no perfusion, unconsciousness within seconds and, without intervention, irreversible brain injury within minutes. It is the limit of [shock](../03-critical-care/shock-and-haemodynamic-support.md), where oxygen delivery is zero, and the whole of resuscitation is an attempt to restore some delivery mechanically (chest compressions) while the cause of the arrest is reversed (defibrillation for a shockable rhythm, correction of the few reversible causes for the rest). The organ that decides the outcome is the brain, which tolerates only a few minutes of no flow and is then injured further by the reperfusion that saves the heart. Survival to discharge from out-of-hospital arrest is of the order of 10% in most high-income systems (2010s), and nearly every survivor was treated within minutes by a bystander and a defibrillator.

## Core tenets

1. **The brain sets the clock.** Neurons have no energy store and the highest oxygen consumption per gram of any tissue, so consciousness is lost within about ten seconds of no flow, and irreversible injury begins within four to six minutes at normal temperature ([cell injury](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/cell-injury-adaptation-and-death.md)). Each minute without compressions or defibrillation reduces survival from a shockable arrest by roughly 7–10%. Hypothermia slows the clock, which is why a cold-water drowning can survive a long arrest, and why the brain, not the heart, is the reason for speed.

2. **Compressions are a mechanical pump with a small output, and interruptions reset it.** External chest compression raises intrathoracic pressure and squeezes the heart, generating a cardiac output of perhaps a quarter to a third of normal and a coronary perfusion pressure that builds over several compressions and collapses within seconds of a pause. This is why the quality of compressions (depth around 5 cm, rate 100–120 per minute, full recoil, minimal interruption) predicts survival better than any drug, and why ventilation, pulse checks and rhythm analysis are compressed into the shortest possible pauses. End-tidal carbon dioxide on the capnograph is the one continuous measure of the output compressions are achieving, and its sudden rise is often the first sign that the heart has restarted ([capnography](../02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/airway-and-ventilation-management.md)).

3. **Rhythm decides the treatment: shockable or not.** Ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia are disorganised or very fast re-entrant electrical activity in a heart that still has excitable tissue ([electrophysiology](../../06-physiology/03-cardiovascular-system/cardiac-electrophysiology.md), [arrhythmias](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/arrhythmias.md)). A defibrillator passes a current that depolarises the bulk of the myocardium simultaneously, extinguishing every wavefront so that the sinus node can take over; it works only while the tissue has energy left to respond, which is why the success of a shock falls with each minute of fibrillation. Asystole and pulseless electrical activity (organised electrical activity with no output) are not shockable: there is no wavefront to extinguish, and the treatment is compressions and the search for a cause.

4. **The non-shockable arrest has a short list of reversible causes.** Hypoxia, hypovolaemia, hyper- or hypokalaemia and other metabolic derangements, hypothermia, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins and thrombosis (coronary or pulmonary) account for most pulseless electrical activity. Each has a bedside test and an action: ventilate, give blood or fluid, correct potassium, warm, decompress, drain, give the antidote, thrombolyse. [Point-of-care ultrasound](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/03-imaging-and-physiological-measurement/ultrasound.md) during a rhythm check can show a tamponade, an empty heart, a dilated right ventricle or a standing heart in seconds. An arrest from a cause that cannot be found or reversed is rarely survived.

5. **Drugs matter less than the public believes.** Adrenaline, an α1 and β agonist, raises aortic diastolic pressure and therefore coronary perfusion pressure during compressions and increases the chance of restoring a circulation; in a large trial (PARAMEDIC2, 2018) it increased survival to 30 days by a small absolute margin without improving survival with good neurological outcome, because it buys back hearts more easily than brains. Amiodarone and lidocaine improve the chance of shocking a refractory fibrillation into a rhythm. No drug has matched the effect of early compressions and early defibrillation, which is why the chain of survival starts with the bystander.

6. **Return of circulation is the beginning of the injury, not the end.** Reperfusion after global ischaemia releases reactive oxygen species and inflammatory mediators, and the post-cardiac arrest syndrome combines brain injury, myocardial stunning, a systemic inflammatory and vasoplegic state like [sepsis](../03-critical-care/sepsis-and-multi-organ-failure.md), and the persisting cause. Post-arrest care in [critical care](../03-critical-care/README.md) protects the brain from secondary injury: oxygen and carbon dioxide in the normal range, blood pressure adequate for cerebral perfusion, control of fever and seizures, and coronary angiography where the cause was coronary. Therapeutic hypothermia to 33 °C, adopted after small trials in 2002, was not better than simply preventing fever in later large trials (TTM, 2013; TTM2, 2021), another case of [trials](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/02-evidence-based-medicine/randomized-controlled-trials.md) correcting a plausible mechanism.

7. **Prognosis is read from the brain over days, and the decision not to start is read from the patient over years.** After arrest, the depth of coma, brainstem reflexes, electroencephalography, somatosensory evoked potentials, serum neuron-specific enolase and imaging are combined, not before about 72 hours and off sedation, because early prediction is unreliable and self-fulfilling ([prognosis](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/01-the-clinical-encounter/prognosis-and-shared-decision-making.md)). Before any arrest, the question is whether resuscitation would be wanted and would work: in a patient dying of an irreversible illness, an arrest is the mode of death rather than a reversible event, and a recorded decision not to attempt resuscitation spares them an undignified and futile intervention ([end-of-life decisions](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/death-dying-and-end-of-life-decisions.md)). Resuscitation without consent is justified by presumed consent to a treatment that works; where it would not, the presumption fails ([consent and capacity](../../15-medicine-in-society/02-ethics-law-and-the-profession/consent-capacity-and-confidentiality.md)).

8. **Who else is involved.** [Cardiology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/README.md) finds and opens the occluded artery that caused most adult arrests and implants the defibrillator that prevents the next; [neurology](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/README.md) prognosticates and diagnoses death by neurological criteria when the brain does not recover; [anaesthesia](../02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/README.md) secures the airway; [critical care](../03-critical-care/README.md) delivers post-arrest care; [neonatology](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/the-newborn-and-neonatology.md) and [paediatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/README.md) resuscitate hearts that usually stopped because of hypoxia rather than arrhythmia and so need ventilation first; [palliative care](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/README.md) makes the decision not to start; and the public, trained in compressions and given access to defibrillators, is the intervention with the largest effect of all ([prevention](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/03-screening-and-prevention/levels-of-prevention.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cardiac arrest | Loss of effective cardiac output; unresponsive, not breathing normally, no pulse |
| Chain of survival | Early recognition and call, early compressions, early defibrillation, post-arrest care |
| Shockable rhythm | Ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia; excitable tissue that defibrillation can reset |
| Pulseless electrical activity | Organised electrical activity without output; a mechanical or metabolic cause |
| Coronary perfusion pressure | Aortic diastolic minus right atrial pressure; built by uninterrupted compressions |
| Defibrillation | Simultaneous depolarisation of the myocardium by an electric current to extinguish re-entrant wavefronts |
| Reversible causes | Hypoxia, hypovolaemia, hypo/hyperkalaemia and metabolic, hypothermia, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins, thrombosis |
| Return of spontaneous circulation | Restoration of a pulse and output; the start of post-arrest care |
| Post-cardiac arrest syndrome | Brain injury, myocardial stunning, systemic inflammation and the persisting cause after reperfusion |
| Neuroprognostication | Multimodal prediction of neurological outcome after about 72 hours off sedation |
| Do-not-attempt-resuscitation decision | An advance decision that resuscitation would not work or would not be wanted |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A flat line is what you shock."** Asystole has no excitable wavefront to reset; defibrillation is for fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia. Television has taught the opposite.
- **"Drugs restart the heart."** Compressions and defibrillation do; adrenaline modestly increases return of circulation without improving neurologically intact survival.
- **"Survival after cardiac arrest is common."** Out-of-hospital survival to discharge is around 10%, and most survivors had bystander compressions and early defibrillation; in-hospital survival is somewhat higher.
- **"Cooling the brain saves it."** Preventing fever matters; cooling to 33 °C was not better than normothermia in the large trials.
