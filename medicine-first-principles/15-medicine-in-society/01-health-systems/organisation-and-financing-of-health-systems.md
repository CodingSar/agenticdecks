## Essence

Illness is unpredictable, expensive and concentrated: a small fraction of people account for most spending in any year, and almost nobody can pay for a cancer or an intensive-care stay out of income. Every health system answers the same problem: how to **pool** that risk across many people and years, then how to buy care with the pool so that providers do what patients need rather than what pays best. The choices are few: who contributes (taxes, payroll, premiums, cash at the point of use), how large the pool is, who purchases, and how providers are paid. Each payment method is an incentive, and clinicians respond to incentives, so financing shapes clinical behaviour as surely as physiology does.

## Core tenets

1. **Health spending is skewed, so insurance is the only way to afford it.** In high-income countries the costliest 5% of people account for roughly half of health spending in a year, and the healthiest half for a few percent. Nobody knows in advance which group they will join. Pooling converts an unpayable, uncertain personal cost into a payable collective one; the larger the pool, the smaller the variance per member, by the same law of large numbers that makes a trial informative ([probability](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/01-probability-and-measurement/probability.md)).

2. **Voluntary insurance unravels through adverse selection.** Those who expect to be ill buy cover; those who expect to be well do not; premiums rise; the healthy leave; the pool shrinks to the sick (Akerlof, 1970; Rothschild and Stiglitz, 1976). Every durable system fights this with compulsion: taxes (the United Kingdom, the Nordic countries), mandatory social insurance (Germany, France, Japan), or a mandate with subsidies. Insurers fight it by excluding the sick, which is why most systems forbid risk-rating and pre-existing-condition exclusions.

3. **Moral hazard is real but smaller than feared, and cost-sharing harms the poor.** When care is free at the point of use, people use more of it. The RAND Health Insurance Experiment (1974–1982) randomised families to different cost-sharing and found that it cut use by roughly 30% without measurable harm to the average participant, but the poorest and sickest were harmed (worse blood-pressure control, vision, dental health). People cut valuable and worthless care alike because they cannot tell the difference: the information asymmetry that defines medicine (Arrow, 1963).

4. **How providers are paid is a lever on what they do.** Fee-for-service rewards volume, so it produces more imaging, surgery and visits. Capitation (a fixed sum per enrolled person) rewards keeping people well and away, and risks under-provision and cherry-picking. Salary is neutral on volume and may be neutral on effort. Payment per admission by diagnosis rewards shorter stays and risks early discharge and up-coding. No method is incentive-free; systems blend them and measure to catch each one's predictable failure.

5. **Supply induces demand.** Where there are more beds, specialists or scanners per head, more are used, with little relation to need or outcome. Roemer's law ("a bed built is a bed filled", 1959) and Wennberg's small-area studies from the 1970s showed several-fold variation in rates of common operations between neighbouring areas with similar populations. So systems plan capacity rather than leave it to the market.

6. **Primary care is the system's filter and integrator.** Systems built on a strong first-contact, continuous, comprehensive and coordinating tier (Starfield's four pillars) have lower cost, fewer admissions and smaller inequalities than specialist-led ones, because a [generalist](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/family-medicine-and-the-generalist.md) sees illness at its low-prevalence, undifferentiated stage and uses the cheapest test that changes management. Gatekeeping trades convenience for this efficiency.

7. **No system escapes the trilemma of access, cost and quality; each picks a point.** The United States spent about 17.6% of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) on health in 2023 (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services), against an Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) average near 9–10%, with below-average life expectancy and some 26 million uninsured (2023). The United Kingdom spends 10–11% with universal coverage and long waits. Germany and Switzerland spend 11–12% with short waits and high contributions. Each rations something: money, time or coverage. The question is whether it is done openly.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Risk pooling | Spreading the cost of illness across many people so that no one pays the full cost of their own |
| Adverse selection | The sick buy cover and the healthy opt out, until the pool is unviable |
| Moral hazard | Using more care because someone else pays |
| Universal Health Coverage (UHC) | Everyone can obtain needed services of adequate quality without financial hardship |
| Capitation | Fixed payment per enrolled person per period, regardless of use |
| Fee-for-service | Payment per item of care delivered |
| Supplier-induced demand | Use that rises with supply rather than need |
| Catastrophic spending | Out-of-pocket health payments exceeding a set share (often 10%) of household income |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A market in healthcare works like any other market."** Arrow (1963) showed why not: demand is unpredictable, the buyer cannot judge quality, the seller decides what is bought, and the stakes are irreversible.
- **"Spending more buys more health."** Beyond a threshold the relation between spending and life expectancy is flat; the United States is the outlier that proves it.
- **"Universal coverage means government-run hospitals."** Germany, the Netherlands and Japan achieve universal coverage through regulated insurers and largely private providers; the National Health Service model is one design among several.
