## Essence

Predicting medicine's future has a poor record: the end of infectious disease was announced in the 1960s, gene therapy was a decade away in 1990, and the genome was to deliver personalised medicine by 2010. The honest method is the one [history](history-of-medicine.md) suggests: find where the current frame is visibly failing, and find the forces already in motion whose consequences are arithmetic rather than speculation. Three such forces are certain: populations are ageing, so multimorbidity becomes the norm; healthcare costs grow faster than the economies paying for them; and molecular biology and machine learning are becoming cheap enough to use at population scale. Everything else is contingent. The idea that should govern reading any forecast is that **a technology changes medicine only when it changes a decision, survives a trial and can be paid for**.

## Core tenets

1. **Demography is the one certain prediction.** Global life expectancy rose from about 47 years in 1950 to about 73 in 2023; the number of people over 65 passed the number under 5 around 2018 and will be about one in six of humanity by 2050 (United Nations). The consequence is [multimorbidity](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/frailty-multimorbidity-and-polypharmacy.md): most patients over 65 in high-income countries have two or more chronic conditions, and the single-disease, single-specialty structure of layers 12 to 14 fits them badly. The generalist, the geriatrician and the integrated record are responses to arithmetic, not fashion.

2. **Costs rise faster than income, and the reasons are structural.** Health spending has grown faster than Gross Domestic Product in every rich country for seventy years. Baumol's cost disease (labour-intensive services cannot match manufacturing productivity gains), technology that adds cost rather than substituting for it, and ageing all push the same way; Eroom's law (Scannell and colleagues, 2012) notes that the number of new drugs per billion dollars of research spending halved roughly every nine years from 1950 to 2010. The result is that [priority setting](../01-health-systems/health-economics-and-priority-setting.md) becomes harder, not easier, as medicine improves.

3. **Genomic prediction is real for rare variants and modest for common disease.** Sequencing a human genome fell from about US$100 million in 2001 to a few hundred dollars by the 2020s. It transforms the diagnosis of single-gene disease and the choice of cancer therapy. But [common disease is polygenic](../../04-genetics-evolution-and-development/01-inheritance/complex-traits-and-heritability.md): polygenic risk scores identify a small tail at severalfold risk and add little to a conventional [risk model](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/03-screening-and-prevention/risk-prediction-and-stratification.md) for the majority, and they transfer poorly across ancestries because they were built on European data. Expect refinement of who to screen and treat, not a replacement of the social and behavioural causes that dominate incidence.

4. **The therapeutic frontier has moved from small molecules to programmed biology.** Monoclonal antibodies, chimeric antigen receptor T cells, messenger RNA vaccines (designed and in trial within weeks of the SARS-CoV-2 sequence in 2020), gene editing (the first approved CRISPR therapy for sickle-cell disease, 2023) and glucagon-like peptide-1 analogues that produce 15–20% weight loss are [advanced therapies](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/biologics-and-advanced-therapies.md) that act on causes rather than symptoms. Their shared problems are price (gene therapies list at US$2–3 million per patient), manufacturing and trials in rare diseases, so the gate is now economic rather than scientific.

5. **Machine learning will change the work before it changes the outcomes.** Pattern-recognition tasks in radiology, pathology and dermatology are being partly automated; documentation, coding and triage faster. Whether patients live longer depends on [external validation, calibration and trials](../01-health-systems/health-informatics-and-artificial-intelligence.md) that most models have not yet had, and on avoiding the proxy-label bias already demonstrated. The likeliest near-term change is in who does what: clinician time moving from screen to patient, or, if designed badly, further from both.

6. **The frame is shifting from rescue to prediction and prevention, and that shift has an old obstacle.** Continuous monitoring, risk scores and genomics make it possible to treat earlier and in more people. But [screening and overdiagnosis](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/03-screening-and-prevention/screening-principles-and-biases.md) show that finding more disease is not the same as preventing more harm, and the social determinants show that the largest preventable burden lies outside the clinic. More sensitive detection needs harder thinking about thresholds, not just better sensors.

7. **The old threats are returning.** Antimicrobial resistance rises while the antibiotic pipeline has been thin since the 1980s, because a drug that must be used sparingly does not pay; vaccine coverage for measles fell during and after COVID-19 and outbreaks followed; [climate change](environmental-and-planetary-health.md) is extending vector ranges and heat mortality; the next pandemic is a question of when. Medicine's future includes the possibility of losing ground held for eighty years.

8. **The workforce and the form of the encounter are changing as much as the science.** The World Health Organization projects a shortfall of about 10 million health workers by 2030; burnout and documentation burden drive exit in rich countries; task-sharing with nurses, pharmacists and community workers is spreading from low-income settings to high-income ones. Remote consultation, home monitoring and patient-held records move care out of the hospital and shift [self-management](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/chronic-disease-and-self-management.md) to patients. The consultation of layer 11 will persist, but it will not be the only form of medicine, nor always the first.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Multimorbidity | Two or more chronic conditions in one person; the norm in old age |
| Baumol's cost disease | Costs rising in labour-intensive services whose productivity cannot keep pace |
| Eroom's law | Declining number of new drugs per unit of research spending |
| Polygenic risk score | A weighted sum of many common variants estimating genetic liability |
| Advanced therapy | Cell, gene or tissue-engineered treatment, as opposed to a small-molecule drug |
| Overdiagnosis | Detection of disease that would never have caused harm |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Genomics will make medicine personal."** For rare disease and cancer, increasingly yes. For the common diseases that fill clinics, genes explain a minority of risk, and the personal variables that matter most are still income, behaviour and environment.
- **"AI will replace doctors."** It will replace tasks. Every previous diagnostic technology (stethoscope, X-ray, automated analyser) changed what doctors do and increased demand for them.
- **"Medical progress is inevitable."** Resistance, falling vaccination, climate and cost can reverse gains; progress has required institutions, money and trust, and each can erode.
