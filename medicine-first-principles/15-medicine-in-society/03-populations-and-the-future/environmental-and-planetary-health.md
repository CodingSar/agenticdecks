## Essence

The body is an open system that takes in air, water, food and heat from its surroundings, so the state of those surroundings is a medical variable. Environmental health is the older part of this: lead, asbestos, tobacco smoke, particulate air pollution and contaminated water, each a toxin with a [dose–response](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#dose-response) curve. Planetary health (Whitmee and colleagues, *The Lancet*, 2015) is the newer part: human activity is now changing the climate, the land and the biosphere fast enough to alter the distribution of heat, infection, food and displacement for everyone, and healthcare itself contributes to the change. The unifying idea is **exposure at population scale**: a small shift in a risk factor across millions of people moves more deaths than a large shift in a few, so the biggest medical facts of this century are measured in micrograms per cubic metre and degrees of warming.

## Core tenets

1. **Air pollution is the largest environmental cause of death, and it acts through cardiovascular disease more than lung disease.** The World Health Organization (WHO) attributes about 7 million deaths a year to ambient and household air pollution (2019 estimates). Fine particulates (PM2.5, under 2.5 micrometres) pass the alveolar wall into the blood, drive systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction and plaque instability ([atherosclerosis](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/atherosclerosis.md)), and raise stroke and heart-attack rates within days of a bad-air episode; lung cancer and [asthma and COPD](../../12-organ-system-specialties/02-pulmonology/asthma-and-copd.md) are the smaller share. There is no threshold below which PM2.5 is safe, which is why the WHO guideline was lowered to 5 µg/m3 in 2021.

2. **Heat kills by overwhelming thermoregulation, and the victims are predictable.** The body sheds heat by sweating and by shunting blood to the skin ([thermoregulation](../../06-physiology/01-integrative-principles/thermoregulation-and-energy-balance.md)); when air temperature and humidity prevent evaporation, core temperature rises, cardiac output is diverted, and the old, the heart-failing, the dehydrated and those on diuretics or anticholinergics decompensate first. The 2003 European heatwave caused about 70,000 excess deaths; summer 2022 in Europe about 61,000 (Ballester and colleagues, 2023). Most heat deaths are recorded as cardiovascular or respiratory, so they are invisible without an excess-mortality calculation.

3. **Climate change moves the map of infection.** Vector range follows temperature: *Aedes* mosquitoes carrying dengue have spread into southern Europe and higher altitudes in Africa and South America, and dengue incidence has risen several-fold since 2000. Warmer water extends cholera and *Vibrio* seasons; flooding mixes sewage with drinking water; drought and conflict move people into crowding. [Host–pathogen dynamics](../../04-genetics-evolution-and-development/02-evolution/host-pathogen-coevolution-and-resistance.md) do not change, but the contact rates that drive them do.

4. **The indirect pathways are the largest.** Crop yields fall with heat and drought, and undernutrition remains the biggest single risk factor for child death; sea-level rise, storms and desertification displace populations, and displaced people have the mortality of the poorest; mental health worsens after disasters and with chronic insecurity. The *Lancet* Countdown's annual accounting shows these pathways growing faster than the direct ones. The burden falls first on those who contributed least, which makes planetary health a [social gradient](social-determinants-and-health-inequity.md) problem.

5. **Antimicrobial resistance is an environmental problem as well as a prescribing one.** Resistance genes are selected wherever antibiotics reach bacteria: in patients, but also in the roughly two-thirds of global antibiotic use that goes to livestock, in pharmaceutical effluent and in sewage. Resistance selected in a river or a feedlot reaches a ward by the same routes as any other microbe. Resistance was directly responsible for an estimated 1.27 million deaths in 2019 (Murray and colleagues, 2022); the response needs agricultural and environmental regulation as much as [stewardship](../../12-organ-system-specialties/08-infectious-diseases/antimicrobial-stewardship.md).

6. **Healthcare has its own footprint, and it is not small.** The health sector produces an estimated 4–5% of global greenhouse-gas emissions (Health Care Without Harm, 2019; the National Health Service in England about 4% of the country's total), more than aviation. The largest shares are the supply chain (drugs, devices, single-use plastics), buildings and travel. A few clinical choices are outsized: desflurane has a hundred-year global warming potential roughly 2,500 times that of carbon dioxide and sevoflurane about 130 times, so [anaesthetic](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/general-and-regional-anaesthesia.md) gas choice matters; nitrous oxide is both a potent greenhouse gas and the dominant ozone-depleting emission; the hydrofluoroalkane propellant in a pressurised inhaler has a footprint many times that of a dry-powder device delivering the same drug.

7. **Most of what protects the planet also protects the patient.** Active travel instead of driving reduces emissions and cardiovascular disease; diets lower in red meat reduce land use, methane and colorectal cancer; closing coal plants reduces carbon and PM2.5 together; well-insulated homes reduce fuel use and winter deaths. These co-benefits mean that the health case for mitigation stands even for someone who discounts the climate case, and they are large enough that the WHO has called climate change the greatest health threat and the response the greatest health opportunity of the century.

8. **Attribution is probabilistic and is done the way all epidemiology is done.** Nobody's death certificate says "climate". Burdens are computed as population attributable fractions: the [relative risk](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/01-epidemiology/measures-of-frequency-and-association.md) per unit exposure from cohort studies, multiplied by the exposure distribution, against a counterfactual baseline. The estimates carry wide uncertainty, which is a reason for honest intervals, not for inaction, since the direction of effect is not in doubt.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Planetary health | Human health as dependent on the natural systems that human activity is changing |
| PM2.5 | Particulate matter under 2.5 micrometres; the pollutant with the largest health burden |
| Excess mortality | Deaths above the seasonal baseline; how heat deaths are counted |
| Global warming potential | A gas's warming effect relative to carbon dioxide over a set horizon |
| Vector range | The geography in which a disease-carrying organism can survive and transmit |
| Co-benefit | A health gain from an action taken for environmental reasons, or the reverse |
| Population attributable fraction | The share of disease that would be removed if an exposure were removed |
| One Health | Joint surveillance of human, animal and environmental health |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Air pollution is a respiratory problem."** Most attributable deaths are cardiovascular: ischaemic heart disease and stroke outnumber lung cancer and chronic lung disease.
- **"Heat deaths are a problem for hot countries."** Excess mortality per degree is highest where populations and buildings are adapted to cold; the 2003 deaths were in France, Italy and Germany.
- **"Healthcare's footprint is a rounding error."** At 4–5% of global emissions it exceeds aviation, and a few swaps (anaesthetic gases, inhaler types) remove a measurable share at no clinical cost.
