## Essence

Global health is the social gradient drawn between countries instead of within one, plus the fact that microbes do not carry passports. A child born in sub-Saharan Africa in the early 2020s had roughly a 7% chance of dying before age five; one born in Western Europe, about 0.4%. The difference is not biology but water, food, vaccines, trained birth attendants and the money to pay for them. The field has two halves that share one toolkit. The first is the long work of closing that gap: primary health care, vaccination, maternal and child health, and the shift from infectious to chronic disease that follows development. The second is the fast work of pandemics, where a pathogen that emerges anywhere reaches everywhere within weeks, and the only defences that work are collective. The idea that unites them is **interdependence**: no country's health is secure while another's is not.

## Core tenets

1. **Health transitions follow development in a predictable sequence.** As income, sanitation and education rise, deaths from infection and childbirth fall first, fertility falls after mortality (the demographic transition), the population ages, and chronic diseases (cardiovascular, cancer, diabetes, mental illness) become the main burden (the epidemiological transition; Omran, 1971). Non-communicable diseases now cause about three-quarters of deaths worldwide, and most of those deaths are in low- and middle-income countries, which face a double burden: undernutrition and infection in the poor alongside obesity and diabetes in the newly urban.

2. **The gains have been enormous and mostly cheap.** Global under-five mortality fell from about 93 per 1,000 live births in 1990 to about 37 in 2022 (United Nations Inter-agency Group for Child Mortality Estimation). The drivers were oral rehydration, measles and other vaccines, insecticide-treated bed nets, antiretroviral therapy, skilled birth attendance and clean water, each costing tens to a few hundred dollars per life-year saved against thousands in rich-country hospital medicine. Smallpox was eradicated in 1980; wild poliovirus survives (2020s) only in Afghanistan and Pakistan. The Alma-Ata declaration (1978) put primary health care at the centre of this strategy, and the evidence has borne it out.

3. **Money and workers are where the gap is.** Health spending per person ranges from under US$50 a year in the poorest countries to over US$10,000 in the richest (World Health Organization data, 2020s); about a billion people face catastrophic out-of-pocket health costs each year. The World Health Organization (WHO) projected a shortfall of about 10 million health workers by 2030, concentrated in Africa and South Asia, worsened by migration of trained staff to richer countries. Universal Health Coverage, target 3.8 of the Sustainable Development Goals, is the stated remedy; [financing](../01-health-systems/organisation-and-financing-of-health-systems.md) is the constraint.

4. **A pandemic is exponential growth meeting fixed capacity.** With a basic reproduction number R0 and a serial interval of a few days, case numbers double in days ([infectious disease dynamics](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/01-epidemiology/infectious-disease-dynamics.md)); hospital beds, oxygen and staff do not. Control means driving the effective reproduction number below 1 by reducing contacts, shortening infectiousness (test, trace, isolate) or removing susceptibles (vaccination), and the herd-immunity threshold 1 − 1/R0 says how many must be immune. Every intervention has a lag of one incubation period before it shows in the data, so decisions are taken on numbers that are already a week old.

5. **Most new pathogens come from animals, and the rate is rising.** About 60% of emerging infectious diseases are zoonotic (Jones and colleagues, 2008): HIV from primates, influenza from birds and pigs, SARS, MERS and probably SARS-CoV-2 from bats via intermediates, Ebola from bats. Land-use change, intensive livestock farming, wildlife trade and travel increase the contact rate between humans and animal reservoirs and the speed at which a spillover spreads. One Health, the joint surveillance of human, animal and environmental health, follows from this.

6. **Pandemics kill through the system as well as the virus.** The 1918 influenza killed perhaps 50 million; COVID-19 caused an estimated 14.9 million excess deaths in 2020–2021 (WHO), about 2.7 times the reported count, and a large share were indirect: untreated heart attacks, missed cancers, interrupted tuberculosis and HIV programmes, lost childhood vaccinations. A system running near capacity ([no reserve](../01-health-systems/organisation-and-financing-of-health-systems.md)) decompensates early. Preparedness is mostly unglamorous: surveillance, stockpiles, surge plans, trusted communication, and the ability to run a [randomised trial](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/02-evidence-based-medicine/randomized-controlled-trials.md) fast, as the RECOVERY trial did for dexamethasone in 2020.

7. **The social gradient operates during a pandemic too, within and between countries.** COVID-19 mortality was about twice as high in the most deprived areas of England as in the least, and higher in ethnic minorities after adjustment for age, because of crowded housing, public-facing work and prior disease. Globally, by the end of 2021 most adults in high-income countries had been vaccinated while under a tenth of people in low-income countries had received a dose. The International Health Regulations (2005) and the WHO Pandemic Agreement (adopted 2025) are attempts to bind states to share information, samples and countermeasures.

8. **Infections and the tools against them are a global commons.** Antimicrobial resistance, driven by use anywhere, was directly responsible for an estimated 1.27 million deaths in 2019 (Murray and colleagues, 2022); a resistant strain selected on one continent is isolated on another within years. Vaccination protects the unvaccinated through herd effects; an unvaccinated pocket anywhere seeds measles everywhere. [Stewardship](../../12-organ-system-specialties/08-infectious-diseases/antimicrobial-stewardship.md) and coverage are therefore not only clinical decisions but contributions to, or withdrawals from, a shared resource.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Epidemiological transition | The shift from infectious to chronic disease as the main burden with development |
| Double burden | Coexisting undernutrition and infection with obesity and chronic disease |
| Universal Health Coverage | Everyone receives needed services without financial hardship |
| Catastrophic health spending | Out-of-pocket costs above a set share of household budget |
| R0 and Rt | Reproduction numbers in a fully susceptible and in the current population |
| Herd immunity threshold | The immune fraction, 1 − 1/R0, above which transmission declines |
| Zoonosis | An infection that passes from animals to humans |
| One Health | Joint surveillance and action across human, animal and environmental health |
| Excess mortality | Deaths above the expected baseline, however caused |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Global health is about tropical diseases."** The largest burdens in low- and middle-income countries are now cardiovascular disease, cancer, diabetes, road injury and mental illness, alongside the remaining infections.
- **"Rich countries are safe from what happens elsewhere."** Resistance, novel viruses and vaccine-preventable outbreaks arrive by air within days; the 2020s demonstrated it.
- **"Lockdowns and vaccines are alternatives."** They act on different terms of the same equation (contact rate and susceptible fraction) and buy time for each other; neither alone stopped COVID-19.
