## Essence

Medicine's history is not a list of discoveries but a sequence of **frames**, each a theory of what disease is and therefore of where to look for it, and each displaced when it could no longer explain what a new instrument or a new kind of counting revealed. Humoral medicine located disease in the balance of fluids in the whole person; anatomy relocated it to a lesion in an organ; cellular pathology to the cell; germ theory to an external living cause; physiology to a disordered function; molecular medicine to a molecule and a gene. For most of its history medicine could explain more than it could treat; the therapeutic revolution (anaesthesia, antisepsis, antibiotics, trials) is less than two centuries old. The idea that organises the whole story is that **the frame determines the question, and instruments change the frame**.

## Core tenets

1. **Humoral medicine was a coherent theory, and it survived because it was coherent.** Hippocratic medicine (fifth to fourth century BCE), systematised by Galen (second century CE), explained illness as imbalance among four humours tied to the seasons, temperaments and diet, and treated it by restoring balance: diet, purging, bleeding. It was holistic, individualised and attentive to prognosis, which is why it satisfied patients and doctors for two millennia. It failed only when tested against what it could not accommodate: the body opened, and the disease counted.

2. **Anatomy moved disease from the person to the organ.** Vesalius's dissections (*De humani corporis fabrica*, 1543) corrected Galen's animal-derived anatomy; Harvey's demonstration of the circulation (1628) was the first physiological argument by measurement: the heart pumps far more blood per hour than the body could make, so blood must recirculate. Morgagni's correlation of symptoms in life with lesions at autopsy (1761) gave disease a *seat*, and the Paris clinical school (Bichat; Laennec's stethoscope, 1816) made the hospital the place where sign and lesion were matched at scale.

3. **Counting exposed the uselessness of most treatment.** Pierre Louis's numerical method (1835) showed that bleeding did not help pneumonia; Semmelweis (1847) showed that hand-washing cut puerperal fever mortality from around 10% to under 2% on his ward; John Snow (1854) mapped cholera to a water pump before anyone knew what cholera was. Oliver Wendell Holmes's remark (1860) that if the whole materia medica were sunk to the bottom of the sea it would be better for mankind and worse for the fishes was the honest conclusion: medicine had learned to see and count before it could cure.

4. **Germ theory supplied an external cause and the first real cures.** Pasteur's fermentation work and Koch's demonstration of the anthrax bacillus (1876), tubercle bacillus (1882) and postulates for proving causation gave disease a specific, living, transmissible agent. Lister's antisepsis (1867) made surgery survivable; Jenner's vaccination (1796) acquired a mechanism; sanitation a rationale. The frame was so powerful that for a generation it crowded out the chronic diseases it could not explain.

5. **Physiology and biochemistry reframed disease as disordered function.** Claude Bernard's *milieu intérieur* (1865) and Cannon's homeostasis (1932) turned the body into a regulated system, so disease became a failure of regulation rather than only a lesion or an invader. Insulin (1922) was the first replacement of a missing regulator; endocrinology and intensive care are this frame at work. [Feedback](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/03-systems-and-models/feedback-and-control.md) entered medicine here.

6. **The therapeutic revolution compressed into a century.** Ether anaesthesia (1846), antisepsis (1867), radiography (1895), insulin (1922), sulphonamides (1935), penicillin at scale (1941–1945), then steroids, antipsychotics, antihypertensives and the polio vaccines in the 1950s. For the first time what the doctor did mattered more than what the doctor knew. McKeown's analysis (1976) qualifies the triumph: most of the fall in mortality in industrial countries preceded these drugs and came from nutrition, water and housing ([social determinants](social-determinants-and-health-inequity.md)).

7. **The trial made medicine answerable to evidence rather than authority.** The Medical Research Council streptomycin trial (1948), designed by Austin Bradford Hill, was the first properly randomised clinical trial; Doll and Hill's cohort of British doctors (from 1951) established smoking as a cause of lung cancer by observation; Archie Cochrane's *Effectiveness and Efficiency* (1972) demanded that the health service fund only what trials supported; "evidence-based medicine" was named in 1991. This frame changed not what disease is but how a claim about treating it may be made ([randomised trials](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/02-evidence-based-medicine/randomized-controlled-trials.md)).

8. **Molecular and genomic medicine moved the seat of disease to the molecule.** Watson and Crick (1953), the genetic code, recombinant DNA (1970s) and the Human Genome Project (completed 2003) made it possible to define a disease by a mutation and to design a drug for its product: imatinib for the BCR-ABL fusion in chronic myeloid leukaemia (2001) was the proof, and the first approved CRISPR gene-editing therapy (2023) is the current frontier. The frame is powerful for single-gene disease and cancer, and has so far explained less of common disease than predicted, the usual fate of a new frame.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Frame | A theory of what disease is, which determines where it is looked for |
| Humoral theory | Disease as imbalance of bodily fluids in the whole person |
| Anatomical seat | The idea that a disease is located in a specific organ lesion |
| Germ theory | Specific living agents cause specific diseases |
| Therapeutic nihilism | The nineteenth-century conclusion that most treatments did harm |
| Milieu intérieur | Bernard's regulated internal environment; the root of homeostasis |
| Evidence-based medicine | Deciding treatment by systematic evidence rather than authority |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Medicine progressed steadily."** It alternated long plateaus with short revolutions, and for most of its history was likelier to harm than help.
- **"Earlier doctors were fools."** Humoral medicine was consistent and clinically attentive; its practitioners lacked instruments, not intelligence.
- **"Germ theory and antibiotics ended infectious disease."** Mortality from infection had already fallen by most of its total through living conditions; the drugs finished what sanitation and nutrition began.
