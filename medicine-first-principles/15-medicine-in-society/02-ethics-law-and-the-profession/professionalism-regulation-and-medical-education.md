## Essence

A profession is an occupation society allows to control its own entry, standards and discipline in exchange for a promise to put the public's interest first. Medicine is the paradigm: the state grants a monopoly on practice to those the profession certifies, and the profession undertakes to train, examine, license and remove them. Professionalism is the behaviour that honours the bargain (competence, honesty, candour, putting the patient first); regulation is the machinery that enforces it; medical education is how the next generation is made to carry it. The idea holding the three together is that **the licence is a social contract**, renegotiated after every scandal, and kept only if the profession polices itself at least as hard as outsiders would.

## Core tenets

1. **Licensure solved an information problem and created a monopoly.** Before the Medical Act (United Kingdom, 1858) and the Flexner Report (United States, 1910), anyone could call themselves a doctor and the patient could not tell the trained from the quack. A register of the qualified protects the public, and simultaneously restricts supply, raises incomes and lets the profession define what counts as medicine. Both effects are real; Freidson (1970) and Starr (1982) showed that medicine's authority was built as much by organisation as by efficacy.

2. **Self-regulation fails when it protects its members, and has had to be rebuilt after each failure.** Harold Shipman, a United Kingdom general practitioner, murdered at least 215 patients over 23 years before conviction in 2000; the Bristol Royal Infirmary inquiry (2001) found paediatric cardiac surgeons continuing to operate with mortality far above peers while colleagues stayed silent; the Mid Staffordshire inquiry (2013) found hundreds of avoidable deaths in a hospital chasing financial targets. Each led to structural change: lay majorities on regulators, mandatory revalidation of every doctor (United Kingdom, 2012), a statutory duty of candour, protection for whistle-blowers. Regulation is a [feedback loop](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/03-systems-and-models/feedback-and-control.md) with a long delay, which is why it oscillates between complacency and over-correction.

3. **Fitness to practise is about the future, not punishment.** A regulator asks whether the public is safe with this doctor practising, not whether the doctor deserves to suffer. Most referrals concern health (addiction, mental illness), conduct (dishonesty, boundaries) or performance, and most are resolved by conditions, retraining or supervision, with erasure for a minority. This rests on the [patient-safety](../01-health-systems/patient-safety-and-quality-improvement.md) finding that most harm comes from systems; the regulator's job is to find the few individuals who are themselves the hazard.

4. **Competence is layered, and exams measure only the bottom layers.** Miller's pyramid (1990): *knows*, *knows how*, *shows how*, *does*. Written examinations test the first two; simulated encounters the third; only workplace observation tests the fourth, which is the only one that matters to patients. Competency-based education (CanMEDS, 1996 onward; the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education's six competencies, 1999) defines roles beyond medical expert (communicator, collaborator, leader, advocate, scholar, professional) and assesses them through entrustable professional activities: tasks a supervisor will let the trainee do unsupervised.

5. **Expertise is pattern recognition built by supervised repetition, and it has a cost.** The [dual-process account](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/01-the-clinical-encounter/clinical-reasoning-and-cognitive-bias.md) of clinical reasoning explains why training is long: the fast, accurate recognition of the expert is compiled from thousands of cases with feedback. That is why apprenticeship persists, why case volume predicts outcomes for surgeons and hospitals, and why duty-hour limits (an 80-hour week in the United States from 2003) trade fatigue against exposure. Feedback must be prompt and specific to build calibration rather than confidence.

6. **The hidden curriculum teaches more than the formal one.** Students learn professionalism less from lectures than from what they see rewarded and tolerated on the wards (Hafferty, 1994): whether seniors admit uncertainty, how they speak about patients when patients cannot hear, whether an error is reported or buried. Measured empathy declines during clinical years. Culture is taught by example, so regulating individuals without changing the environment reproduces the behaviour.

7. **The profession's own health is a safety issue.** Burnout (emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation, reduced accomplishment) affected roughly 40–60% of United States physicians in surveys through the 2010s and 2020s, with higher rates in emergency medicine and primary care, and it is associated with more errors and earlier exit. Causes are mostly organisational (workload, loss of control, documentation burden); treating burnout as a failure of individual resilience repeats the error of blaming the sharp end.

8. **Medicine is one profession among many at the bedside.** Nurses, pharmacists, physiotherapists and others have their own regulators and codes, and the doctor's historic authority over them has given way to team-based care with distributed accountability. Scope-of-practice boundaries (who may prescribe, who may diagnose) are now the main site of professional dispute; the evidence that well-designed task-sharing is safe is strong. [How the specialties interact](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/how-the-specialties-interact.md) inside medicine mirrors this between professions.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Profession | An occupation with self-governed entry, standards and discipline in exchange for public service |
| Licensure | State authorisation to practise, usually tied to a register |
| Revalidation | Periodic demonstration that a licensed doctor remains fit to practise |
| Fitness to practise | A regulator's judgement of whether a doctor can safely continue |
| Miller's pyramid | Knows, knows how, shows how, does: levels of competence |
| Entrustable professional activity | A task a supervisor can entrust to a trainee without direct supervision |
| Hidden curriculum | What is learned from institutional culture rather than teaching |
| Burnout | Exhaustion, cynicism and inefficacy from chronic occupational stress |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Regulation exists to punish bad doctors."** It exists to protect future patients; punishment is the courts' job. Most regulatory action is remedial.
- **"Professionalism is bedside manner."** Manner is a small part. Honesty about error, maintaining competence and challenging unsafe colleagues are the demanding parts, and the ones scandals expose.
- **"Longer training always makes safer doctors."** Beyond a point, what matters is volume, feedback and supervision, not years. Unsupervised repetition entrenches error as readily as skill.
