## What this area is about

Medicine gives one person licensed power over another's body, information and future, exercised when that other is frightened, ill and ignorant of what the first knows. This area answers the question: **what does the clinician owe the patient and the public, and how does society make sure the debt is paid?** Ethics states the duties; law makes some of them enforceable; the profession, through regulation and education, is the institution that selects, trains, licenses and disciplines the people who carry them.

The one idea that unifies the three children is that **trust is the asset the whole enterprise runs on, and it is maintained by constraint**. A patient tells a stranger about their bowels and their fears, swallows a tablet whose contents they cannot verify and lies still under a knife, because they trust a system of duties they have never read. Every rule in this area, from the four principles to the duty of candour to revalidation, exists to make that trust rational.

Read ethics first: it supplies the vocabulary (autonomy, best interests, proportionality, justice) that the other two use. Consent and confidentiality are the two places where ethics is most fully codified in law, and they are where the engineer's instinct for a clear rule meets the clinician's reality of gradations of understanding, risk and disclosure. Professionalism closes the loop: a profession is a group society allows to set its own standards in exchange for serving the public, and regulation and education are how it keeps that bargain. The ordering is also historical: the Hippocratic duties came first, consent law arrived in the twentieth century, and formal regulation of doctors is a creature of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

## Connections across layers

From below, this area takes [harm and benefit](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/README.md) quantified as risks, [prognosis and shared decision-making](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/01-the-clinical-encounter/prognosis-and-shared-decision-making.md) as the clinical form of respect for autonomy, the [surgical decision](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/the-surgical-decision.md) as the paradigm of consent, [psychiatry's mental state](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/03-psychiatry/the-mental-state-and-psychiatric-formulation.md) as the assessment on which capacity rests, and [end-of-life care](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/death-dying-and-end-of-life-decisions.md) as the hardest test case. It gives back requirements on every clinical act: a valid consent before a procedure, a capacity assessment before overriding a refusal, a lawful basis before sharing a record, and a licence before practising at all.
