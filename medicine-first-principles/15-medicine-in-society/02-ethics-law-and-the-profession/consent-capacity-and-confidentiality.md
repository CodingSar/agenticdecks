# Consent, Capacity and Confidentiality

## Essence

Touching a person without agreement is a battery, and repeating what they told you in confidence is a breach of trust; medicine does both daily and is lawful only because of consent and a confidentiality rule with defined exceptions. Consent is autonomy in legal form: valid when the person has **capacity**, is **informed** about what matters to them, and decides **voluntarily**. Capacity is the hinge: the law presumes it, tests it for a specific decision at a specific time, and only when it is absent lets someone else decide, in the person's interests. Confidentiality is the mirror image: information belongs to the patient and leaves the consultation only with their agreement, by law, or to prevent serious harm to others. The common idea: **the patient, not the clinician, is the authority on what matters to the patient**.

## Core tenets

1. **Consent is a process, not a signature.** Cardozo's dictum (Schloendorff, 1914) that "every human being of adult years and sound mind has a right to determine what shall be done with his own body" makes consent the condition of lawful treatment. A form is evidence that a conversation happened; the conversation, in which the person understands what is proposed and the alternatives including doing nothing, is what makes consent real. Consent can be implied (holding out an arm for a blood test), oral or written, and withdrawn at any point.

2. **Disclosure is judged by the reasonable patient, not the reasonable doctor.** For most of the twentieth century the test was professional (Bolam, 1957: what a responsible body of doctors would disclose). Canterbury v Spence (United States, 1972) and Montgomery v Lanarkshire (United Kingdom Supreme Court, 2015) moved it to the patient: a risk is *material* if a reasonable person in the patient's position would attach significance to it, or if the doctor knows this patient would. A 1% risk of a devastating outcome is material; a 10% risk of a trivial one may not be. Disclosure therefore needs the tools of [shared decision-making](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/01-the-clinical-encounter/prognosis-and-shared-decision-making.md): absolute numbers, natural frequencies, both framings.

3. **Capacity is presumed, decision-specific and independent of the decision's wisdom.** The functional test used in England and Wales (Mental Capacity Act 2005) and in similar form elsewhere: can the person understand the relevant information, retain it long enough, use or weigh it, and communicate a choice? A person with dementia may lack capacity to manage finances yet have it to refuse a blood test. An unwise decision is not evidence of incapacity, or every refusal would be overruled. The assessment belongs to the clinician proposing the treatment, using the [mental state examination](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/03-psychiatry/the-mental-state-and-psychiatric-formulation.md), not to psychiatry by default.

4. **When capacity is absent, someone decides in the person's interests, in a defined order.** A valid advance decision made with capacity binds later refusals; a person appointed by the patient (a lasting power of attorney or healthcare proxy) decides next; otherwise the clinician decides in the patient's *best interests*, which means the patient's own values and prior wishes as far as they can be known, not the clinician's. In an emergency with no information, treatment needed to save life proceeds under necessity. The least restrictive effective option is required. Compulsion against a capacitous refusal exists only in mental-health law (a mental disorder posing risk to self or others) and public-health law (isolation for infectious disease), is time-limited and reviewable, and never extends to an unrelated physical illness.

5. **Children and adolescents are a gradient, not a switch.** Below a fixed age a parent consents, bound by the child's welfare; above it the young person does. In between, the Gillick principle (United Kingdom, 1985) gives a minor with sufficient understanding authority to consent to their own treatment, judged decision by decision. A competent minor's refusal of life-saving treatment is treated more cautiously than their consent in most jurisdictions, and [safeguarding](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/adolescent-health-and-safeguarding.md) duties can override a child's confidentiality.

6. **Confidentiality is a duty with three doors.** Information passes with the patient's consent (explicit, or implied within the treating team); where law requires it (notifiable diseases, court orders, some gunshot wounds); or where disclosure is necessary to prevent serious harm to others (a driver with uncontrolled epilepsy; the Tarasoff duty in the United States, 1976, to protect an identifiable threatened person). The last door is the narrowest and the most often misjudged in both directions. Confidentiality survives death.

7. **Data protection has made confidentiality a systems property.** The General Data Protection Regulation (European Union, 2018) and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (United States, 1996) treat health data as a special category needing a lawful basis, minimisation, security and, in Europe, rights of access and erasure. A duty once kept by a locked cabinet now depends on [access controls, audit and re-identification risk](../01-health-systems/health-informatics-and-artificial-intelligence.md); large breaches threaten trust more than indiscreet conversation.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Valid consent | Agreement by a person with capacity, informed of material risks, given voluntarily |
| Material risk | One a reasonable person in the patient's position would attach significance to |
| Capacity | The ability to understand, retain, weigh and communicate a specific decision |
| Best interests | The standard for deciding for an incapacitous person: their values, not the decider's |
| Advance decision | A refusal made with capacity that binds treatment after capacity is lost |
| Gillick competence | A minor's authority to consent when they understand enough to decide |
| Duty of confidence | The obligation to hold patient information unless a defined exception applies |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A signed form is consent."** It is evidence. Consent obtained without understanding, under pressure, or without disclosure of a material risk is invalid however neatly signed.
- **"A patient who refuses sensible treatment lacks capacity."** The test is the process of deciding, not the outcome. Capacitous people may refuse for reasons the clinician finds irrational.
- **"Next of kin can consent for an adult."** In most jurisdictions relatives have no authority unless formally appointed; they are consulted about the person's wishes, which is different.
