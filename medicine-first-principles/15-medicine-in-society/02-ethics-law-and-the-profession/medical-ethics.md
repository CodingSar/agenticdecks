## Essence

Medical ethics decides what ought to be done when medicine's power meets a particular person, and gives reasons others can examine. It is a method, not a list of prohibitions: who is affected, what is at stake for each, which duties pull which way, and how to weigh them when they conflict, which they usually do. The most widely used framework, Beauchamp and Childress's four principles (1979), names respect for **autonomy**, **beneficence**, **non-maleficence** and **justice**; its value is not that the principles decide cases but that they make the conflict explicit. The one idea underneath all of it is that the patient is a person with their own ends, not a defective organism to be optimised, and the clinician's expertise is in means, not ends.

## Core tenets

1. **Four principles, none of which is sovereign.** *Autonomy*: a competent adult's informed choice about their own body is theirs, even when it is medically wrong. *Beneficence*: act for the patient's good. *Non-maleficence*: do not cause harm disproportionate to benefit, which is why "first, do no harm" is a maxim about proportion, not abstinence. *Justice*: treat like cases alike and distribute burdens and benefits fairly. A Jehovah's Witness refusing blood pits autonomy against beneficence; a scarce intensive-care bed pits beneficence to one against justice to all. The principles locate the conflict; judgement resolves it.

2. **Harm and benefit are probabilities, so ethics needs numbers.** Beneficence without an effect size is sentiment. A treatment with a Number Needed to Treat of 100 and a Number Needed to Harm of 50 is net harmful however kind its intent, and the duty to offer it does not exist. The [randomised trial](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/02-evidence-based-medicine/randomized-controlled-trials.md) is therefore an ethical instrument: it is the only honest way to know whether an intervention helps, and *equipoise* (genuine uncertainty about which arm is better) is what makes randomising a patient permissible.

3. **Intention matters: the doctrine of double effect.** An act with a good and a bad effect is permissible if the bad effect is not the means to the good, is not intended, and is proportionate. Giving an opioid to relieve the pain of a dying patient, foreseeing but not intending that it may shorten life, is accepted everywhere; giving the same drug to end life is euthanasia, legal in a few jurisdictions and a crime in most. The distinction is contested philosophically but load-bearing in law and in [palliative practice](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/death-dying-and-end-of-life-decisions.md).

4. **Withholding and withdrawing are morally equivalent, and neither is killing.** Stopping a ventilator that is not benefiting a patient lets the disease take its course; it is an omission of a treatment there is no duty to continue. Clinicians find withdrawal harder than withholding because of the psychology of action, but the equivalence is what allows a trial of treatment: start intensive care, and stop if it fails. Without it, every treatment started would be a trap.

5. **Research ethics was written in response to abuse.** The Nuremberg Code (1947) followed Nazi experiments; the Declaration of Helsinki (1964, revised repeatedly) and the Belmont Report (1979) followed the Tuskegee syphilis study (1932–1972), in which the United States Public Health Service withheld treatment from Black men to observe the disease's natural history. Their standards (voluntary informed consent, independent review, favourable risk-benefit, fair selection of subjects) exist because clinicians convinced of the value of their work repeatedly failed to see the people in front of them.

6. **Justice extends beyond the bedside, and scarcity makes it unavoidable.** Every health budget is finite ([priority setting](../01-health-systems/health-economics-and-priority-setting.md)), so to treat one is to not treat another. Utilitarian allocation (most health per unit resource), egalitarian allocation (equal chance, by lottery or queue) and prioritarian allocation (the worst-off first) each have a claim; triage in a disaster, organ allocation and pandemic vaccine sequencing are where the choice becomes visible. The ethical requirement is not a particular rule but that the rule be explicit, consistent and defensible in public.

7. **Trust is the asset, and truth-telling maintains it.** Concealing a cancer diagnosis was normal in 1960 (most United States physicians surveyed then did not tell; by 1979 most did). It gave way to a duty of honesty because autonomy is impossible without information and because deception, once discovered, poisons every later consultation. The duty of candour after harm is the same principle applied to the clinician's own errors.

8. **Frameworks organise a case; they do not decide it.** Jonsen, Siegler and Winslade's four topics (medical indications, patient preferences, quality of life, contextual features) structure the facts; the principles name the values; casuistry reasons from settled paradigm cases to new ones. None is an algorithm, which is why hard cases go to an ethics committee rather than one person.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Autonomy | The right of a competent person to decide what happens to their own body |
| Beneficence | The duty to act for the patient's benefit |
| Non-maleficence | The duty not to inflict harm disproportionate to benefit |
| Justice | Fair distribution of benefits, risks and costs; like cases treated alike |
| Paternalism | Overriding a person's choice for their own good |
| Equipoise | Genuine uncertainty about which treatment is better; the condition for randomising |
| Double effect | A bad effect foreseen but not intended, as a side effect of a proportionate good act |
| Candour | The duty to tell a patient when something has gone wrong |

## Common misconceptions

- **"'First, do no harm' means never risk harm."** Every effective treatment can harm. The maxim asks for proportion: do not impose risk the expected benefit does not justify. A surgeon who never operates does harm by omission.
- **"The four principles tell you the answer."** They tell you what is in conflict. Weighing them is judgement, which is why the framework is a checklist, not a calculator.
