# Organelles and compartmentalization

## Essence

A eukaryotic cell is not a bag of enzymes but a set of membrane-bounded compartments, each with its own chemistry. Compartments let incompatible reactions run at once (acid hydrolysis in the lysosome at pH ~4.5, oxidative phosphorylation across the inner mitochondrial membrane, Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) transcription in the nucleus) a few micrometres apart, in the same cytoplasm at pH ~7.2. The price is that every molecule must be addressed, transported and sorted, and that each membrane is a gradient that must be maintained with energy. This is where thermodynamics (gradients across membranes), chemistry (enzymes confined to a compartment) and information (a signal sequence telling a protein where to go) first combine into one self-maintaining unit. Nearly every organelle has a disease named after its failure.

## Core tenets

1. **A membrane is a compartment because it is a barrier, and a barrier is useful only with selective transport.** The lipid bilayer blocks ions and most polar molecules, so the composition on each side can differ. Pumps and channels (layer 02) then make each organelle's lumen distinct: the lysosome is acidified by a vacuolar proton Adenosine Triphosphatase (ATPase), the endoplasmic reticulum holds ~100-fold more calcium than the cytosol, the mitochondrial matrix is alkaline relative to the intermembrane space. Every such difference is a store of free energy that costs ATP to hold and can be released as a signal or as work.

2. **The nucleus separates the genome from the machinery that reads it, and that separation is regulatory.** Messenger Ribonucleic Acid (mRNA) is transcribed and processed inside, exported through nuclear pores (~2,000 per nucleus, each a selective gate for cargo above ~40 kDa), and translated outside. The gap allows splicing before translation and lets the cell control which transcription factors reach the DNA: many signalling pathways end with a protein being allowed through the pore. Nuclear envelope defects (laminopathies) cause premature ageing syndromes and muscular dystrophies.

3. **Mitochondria are a captured bacterium, and that history explains their biology and pathology.** They have their own circular DNA (~16.6 kb, 37 genes), their own ribosomes, a double membrane, and they divide by fission. The inner membrane holds the respiratory chain and is folded into cristae to give surface area; a proton gradient across it drives ATP synthesis ([oxidative phosphorylation](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/02-metabolism/glycolysis-tca-cycle-and-oxidative-phosphorylation.md)). Because mitochondrial DNA is inherited only from the egg and is present in hundreds of copies per cell, mitochondrial diseases follow maternal inheritance and vary with the fraction of mutant genomes (heteroplasmy). Mitochondria are also the switch for intrinsic [apoptosis](../02-the-cell-life-cycle/cell-death-apoptosis-necrosis-and-autophagy.md) and the main source of reactive oxygen species.

4. **The endomembrane system is one continuous flow of membrane.** Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER) to Golgi apparatus to plasma membrane or lysosome, and back by endocytosis. The rough ER folds and glycosylates secreted and membrane proteins; the smooth ER synthesises lipids, stores calcium and, in liver, detoxifies drugs with cytochrome P450 enzymes. The Golgi modifies sugars and sorts cargo. The lysosome is the terminal digestive compartment. Because the lumen of the ER is topologically outside the cell, a protein's orientation set at insertion is preserved all the way to the surface. The detail of movement is in [membrane trafficking](membrane-trafficking-and-secretion.md).

5. **Lysosomes and peroxisomes confine dangerous chemistry.** Lysosomes hold ~60 acid hydrolases that would digest the cytoplasm at neutral pH; they are safe because they only work at pH ~4.5. Peroxisomes oxidise very-long-chain fatty acids and generate and destroy hydrogen peroxide inside a single compartment. When one hydrolase is missing, its substrate accumulates in the lysosome and the cell swells and malfunctions: the ~70 lysosomal storage diseases (Gaucher, Tay-Sachs, Pompe) are each a single missing enzyme, and the organs affected are those that handle the most substrate. Peroxisomal disorders (Zellweger spectrum) follow the same logic.

6. **Compartment size is set by diffusion.** Diffusion time scales with the square of distance ([scale and allometry](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/scale-and-allometry.md)): a small metabolite crosses a 10 µm cell in ~0.1 s but a 1 mm distance in ~1,000 s. Organelles keep reaction partners within micrometres, and large cells (neurons with axons a metre long) must supplement diffusion with active transport along the [cytoskeleton](the-cytoskeleton-and-cell-motility.md). Mitochondria cluster where ATP is used: around the sarcomere, at the synapse, under the brush border.

7. **Every protein carries its address.** A signal sequence sends a nascent chain into the ER; a nuclear localisation signal binds importins; a mannose-6-phosphate tag routes hydrolases to the lysosome; an N-terminal amphipathic helix targets mitochondria ([protein targeting](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/03-genetic-information-flow/translation-and-protein-targeting.md)). Compartmentalisation is thus an information problem as much as a structural one. I-cell disease, in which the mannose-6-phosphate tag cannot be added, leaves lysosomes empty and the enzymes in the blood.

8. **Compartments make the cell's response to injury legible.** Hypoxia first stops the mitochondrion (no ATP), then the sodium pump (cell swells), then calcium floods in from the ER and outside, activating proteases and phospholipases, and finally lysosomes leak and digest the cell. The ordered sequence of organelle failure is the subject of [cell injury](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/cell-injury-adaptation-and-death.md); it is predictable because each organelle has a known job and a known dependency on the others.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Compartment | A membrane-bounded space with its own composition, pH and enzyme set |
| Lumen | The inside of an organelle; for the ER and Golgi, topologically equivalent to the outside of the cell |
| Cristae | Folds of the inner mitochondrial membrane that multiply surface for the respiratory chain |
| Heteroplasmy | Mixture of normal and mutant mitochondrial genomes in one cell; sets disease severity |
| Nuclear pore complex | Large protein gate controlling traffic of RNA and proteins between nucleus and cytoplasm |
| Lysosomal storage disease | Accumulation of a substrate because one lysosomal hydrolase is missing |
| Signal sequence | Short peptide that directs a protein to a compartment |
| Smooth ER | Lipid synthesis, calcium storage and (in liver) drug metabolism by cytochrome P450 |
| Peroxisome | Single-membrane organelle for very-long-chain fatty acid oxidation and peroxide chemistry |

## Common misconceptions

- **"The cytoplasm is a dilute solution."** Protein concentration is ~200–300 g/L; the cytosol is crowded, viscous and organised, and many reactions run in multi-enzyme assemblies or on membranes rather than freely in solution.
- **"Mitochondria just make ATP."** They also set the apoptotic switch, buffer calcium, generate heat and reactive oxygen species, and synthesise haem and steroid precursors; many mitochondrial diseases present as neurological or muscular, not as simple energy failure.
- **"Organelles are fixed structures."** The ER, Golgi and mitochondrial network continuously fuse, divide and exchange membrane; the picture in a textbook is a snapshot of a flow.
