## Essence

The cytoskeleton is the cell's bones, muscles and railways in one system of protein polymers: actin filaments, microtubules and intermediate filaments. It gives the cell shape and stiffness, moves organelles and vesicles along defined tracks, pulls chromosomes apart, pinches the cell in two, and lets white cells crawl and sperm swim. The single idea is **dynamic polymers plus motor proteins**: filaments assemble and disassemble in seconds under the control of Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) or Guanosine Triphosphate (GTP) hydrolysis, and motors walk along them converting ATP into directed force. Muscle contraction, mitosis, phagocytosis, wound healing, axonal transport and cancer invasion are all this machinery at different scales.

## Core tenets

1. **Three polymers, three jobs, chosen by mechanics.** Actin filaments (~7 nm, flexible, concentrated under the membrane) handle shape change and crawling. Microtubules (~25 nm hollow tubes, stiff, radiating from the centrosome) are long-range tracks and the mitotic spindle. Intermediate filaments (~10 nm, rope-like, including keratins, vimentin, neurofilaments and nuclear lamins) resist tension and hold tissues together. Stiffness scales with the fourth power of radius ([mechanics](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/mechanics-and-materials.md)), so a microtubule is ~100 times stiffer than an actin filament, which is why one is a beam and the other a mesh.

2. **Filaments are polar and dynamic, and that is what makes them useful.** Actin and tubulin subunits bind a nucleotide (ATP or GTP), polymerise faster at one end (plus end) and hydrolyse the nucleotide after assembly. The hydrolysed polymer is less stable, so a filament can grow at one end while shrinking at the other (treadmilling) or switch abruptly from growth to collapse (dynamic instability). A cell can therefore rebuild its entire skeleton in minutes, probe its surroundings with microtubule tips, and remodel its leading edge continuously. Drugs that freeze this dynamic kill dividing cells: taxanes stabilise microtubules, vinca alkaloids prevent their assembly, and both arrest mitosis ([anticancer drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/anticancer-drugs.md)).

3. **Motor proteins convert chemical energy into directed movement by coupling ATP hydrolysis to a conformational cycle.** Myosins walk on actin; kinesins (mostly plus-end directed) and dyneins (minus-end directed) walk on microtubules. Each cycle of binding, power stroke, release and recovery moves the motor a fixed step (8 nm for kinesin, ~36 nm for myosin V) and costs one ATP. The direction is built into the motor and the polarity of the track, so a neuron can send vesicles outward along the axon on kinesin and bring worn components back on dynein. Axons a metre long depend on this; disruption of axonal transport is an early event in many neurodegenerative diseases.

4. **Muscle is the cytoskeleton specialised to one task.** In striated muscle actin and myosin II are arranged into sarcomeres so that every power stroke pulls in the same direction; calcium gates the interaction via troponin and tropomyosin. The sliding-filament mechanism, force proportional to overlap, and ATP cost per cycle are all cytoskeletal facts first and physiology second ([skeletal muscle](../../06-physiology/10-musculoskeletal-system-and-skin/skeletal-muscle-contraction-and-energetics.md)). Rigor mortis is myosin frozen on actin when ATP runs out.

5. **Cell crawling is actin polymerisation at the front and myosin contraction at the back.** A migrating neutrophil or fibroblast pushes out a lamellipodium by nucleating branched actin (via the Arp2/3 complex) against the membrane, anchors it to the substrate through integrin-based adhesions, and contracts the rear with myosin II. The direction is set by chemical gradients sensed through receptors that activate small GTPases (Rac at the front, Rho at the rear). Neutrophil chemotaxis to infection, fibroblast migration into a wound and tumour cell invasion through tissue are the same programme with different triggers.

6. **Cilia and flagella are microtubule machines, and their failure is a recognisable syndrome.** Motile cilia beat with a 9+2 microtubule array driven by axonemal dynein; sperm swim with the same engine. Primary ciliary dyskinesia (defective dynein arms) produces chronic sinusitis and bronchiectasis (mucus not cleared), male infertility, and in about half of patients situs inversus, because a motile cilium at the embryonic node sets left–right asymmetry ([patterning](../../04-genetics-evolution-and-development/03-development/morphogens-patterning-and-developmental-genes.md)). Non-motile primary cilia are sensory antennae; their defects (ciliopathies) cause polycystic kidneys and retinal degeneration.

7. **Intermediate filaments are tissue-specific, which makes them diagnostic and mechanical markers.** Epithelia express keratins, mesenchymal cells vimentin, muscle desmin, neurons neurofilaments and glia glial fibrillary acidic protein. A pathologist identifies the origin of an undifferentiated tumour by staining for these. Mechanically they transmit force across tissues through desmosomes and hemidesmosomes; mutations in epidermal keratins cause skin to blister at the slightest shear (epidermolysis bullosa simplex).

8. **The cytoskeleton is a signalling and mechanosensing system, not only a scaffold.** Tension on integrins and cadherins is transmitted through actin to the nucleus and changes gene expression; stem cells on stiff substrates become bone-like, on soft ones fat-like or neural. Protein aggregates that the quality-control system fails to clear (tau tangles, Lewy bodies) are in large part cytoskeleton-associated proteins, linking this leaf to [protein quality control](protein-quality-control-and-degradation.md).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Actin filament (microfilament) | ~7 nm polar polymer of actin; cortex, lamellipodia, contractile rings and sarcomeres |
| Microtubule | ~25 nm hollow tubulin polymer; long-range tracks, spindle, cilia |
| Intermediate filament | ~10 nm tension-bearing rope; keratin, vimentin, desmin, neurofilament, lamin |
| Dynamic instability | Abrupt switching of a microtubule between growth and rapid shrinkage |
| Treadmilling | Net growth at the plus end and loss at the minus end at steady state |
| Motor protein | ATPase that walks along a polar filament: myosin, kinesin, dynein |
| Lamellipodium | Sheet of branched actin pushing the leading edge of a crawling cell |
| Small GTPase (Rho, Rac, Cdc42) | Molecular switches that pattern the actin cytoskeleton in space |
| Axoneme | The 9+2 microtubule core of a motile cilium or flagellum |

## Common misconceptions

- **"The cytoskeleton is rigid, like a skeleton."** Most of it turns over within minutes; its function depends on being disassembled as fast as it is built.
- **"Only muscle cells use actin and myosin to generate force."** Every cell uses them for division, crawling and shape; muscle is the specialised, abundant case.
- **"Cilia are a minor feature."** Nearly every cell has a primary cilium, and defective cilia explain disease in kidney, retina, airway, brain and body asymmetry at once.
