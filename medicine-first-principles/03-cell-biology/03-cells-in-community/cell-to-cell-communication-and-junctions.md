## Essence

A multicellular body works only if cells agree on what to do. Cells talk over four ranges: by direct contact, over micrometres by secreted paracrine factors, over metres by hormones in blood, and over the same metres at millisecond speed by nerves releasing transmitter at a synapse. Junctions organise the contact end: tight junctions seal an epithelium into a barrier, anchoring junctions bind cells into a sheet that bears force, and gap junctions wire cells into an electrical and metabolic unit. The one idea is **range and speed set the mechanism**: diffusion is fast over micrometres and hopeless over centimetres, so the body uses local signals where it can and a circulation or a nerve where it must.

## Core tenets

1. **Signal range follows from diffusion and destruction.** A molecule released into tissue spreads roughly the square root of (diffusion coefficient × lifetime) before it is degraded or bound ([diffusion](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/diffusion-osmosis-and-transport.md)). Nitric oxide (half-life of seconds) acts within tens of micrometres; a matrix-bound cytokine acts on neighbours; a stable hormone must be carried by blood. Paracrine signals are local because the tissue destroys them.

2. **Contact-dependent signalling gives exact spatial control.** Notch on one cell is activated by Delta on an adjacent cell and only that cell; its cleaved intracellular domain enters the nucleus and changes fate. Because a cell committing to one fate raises its Delta and suppresses its neighbours' Notch, a uniform field resolves into a precise alternating pattern (lateral inhibition), which spaces neurons among skin cells and secretory cells among intestinal stem cells. The immune synapse between a T cell and an antigen-presenting cell applies the same principle.

3. **Tight junctions make an epithelium a barrier with a controllable leak.** Strands of claudins and occludin encircle each cell at its apical border and seal the paracellular path. Different claudins form pores of different charge and size, so tightness is tuned: the proximal tubule is leaky, the bladder tight, the blood–brain barrier tightest. Tight junctions also fence apical from basolateral membrane proteins, preserving the polarity that directional transport needs ([membrane transport](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/04-cell-signalling/membrane-transport-and-ion-gradients.md)). Cytokines, toxins and ischaemia open them: the leaky gut of inflammatory bowel disease, the brain oedema of stroke ([gut barrier](../../06-physiology/06-gastrointestinal-system/the-gut-barrier-and-microbiome.md)).

4. **Anchoring junctions distribute force across a sheet.** Adherens junctions (cadherin to actin) and desmosomes (desmosomal cadherins to intermediate filaments) link every cell's cytoskeleton to its neighbours', so a stretch of skin or a heartbeat is carried by the whole tissue ([adhesion](cell-adhesion-and-the-extracellular-matrix.md)). Desmosomal mutations cause arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy: cardiomyocytes detach and die under the strain of exercise and are replaced by fat and fibrosis.

5. **Gap junctions couple cells electrically and metabolically.** Six connexins form a hemichannel; two dock to make a pore ~1.5 nm wide passing ions, second messengers and metabolites below ~1 kDa between cytoplasms. Cardiac muscle is a functional syncytium because of them: the action potential spreads cell to cell without synapses, and conduction velocity depends on coupling ([cardiac electrophysiology](../../06-physiology/03-cardiovascular-system/cardiac-electrophysiology.md)). Remodelled gap junctions in scarred myocardium slow conduction and set up re-entrant arrhythmias. Connexin 26 mutations are the commonest cause of inherited deafness, because cochlear potassium recycling runs through gap junctions.

6. **Endocrine signalling is slow, broadcast and graded; neural signalling is fast, private and digital.** A hormone reaches every cell with the receptor at a concentration set by secretion against clearance, so its effect is graded over minutes to days. A neuron releases transmitter into a 20 nm cleft onto one target in under a millisecond and clears it at once. A hormone sets blood glucose over hours; a nerve withdraws a hand from a flame ([hormones](../../06-physiology/07-endocrine-system/hormone-classes-axes-and-feedback.md)).

7. **The same ligand means different things to different cells.** Acetylcholine slows the heart (muscarinic receptor opening a potassium channel) and contracts skeletal muscle (nicotinic receptor opening a sodium channel); adrenaline constricts skin arterioles and dilates those of muscle. Meaning belongs to the receptor and the wiring of the recipient, not to the molecule, which is why receptor subtype selectivity is the central design problem of pharmacology.

8. **Communication fails in characteristic ways, each a disease class.** A signal without a stimulus (a hormone-secreting tumour), a receptor constitutively on (growth factor receptors in cancer), a receptor blocked (antibodies against the acetylcholine receptor in myasthenia gravis), a junction that leaks, or a cell that stops listening to its neighbours (contact inhibition lost in cancer). This vocabulary is the vocabulary of much of [pathology](../../08-pathology/README.md).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Autocrine / paracrine / endocrine / synaptic | Signalling to self, to neighbours, by blood, or across a synapse |
| Contact-dependent signalling | Ligand and receptor both membrane-bound; Notch–Delta, immune synapse |
| Lateral inhibition | A committed cell suppresses the same fate in its neighbours, creating pattern |
| Tight junction | Claudin-based seal at the apical border; sets paracellular permeability and polarity |
| Adherens junction / desmosome | Cadherin-based anchors to actin / to intermediate filaments |
| Gap junction | Connexin channel joining two cytoplasms; electrical and metabolic coupling |
| Signal range | Distance a messenger acts, set by diffusion against degradation |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Hormones are powerful because they travel far."** Travelling far forces slowness and broadcast; local signals are more precise and faster.
- **"A signalling molecule has a fixed effect."** The effect belongs to the receiving cell; the same molecule can excite one tissue and inhibit another.
