## Essence

Cells become a tissue by sticking to each other and to a scaffold they secrete. The Extracellular Matrix (ECM) is that scaffold: collagen fibres for tensile strength, elastin for recoil, proteoglycans that hold water and resist compression, and adhesive glycoproteins that cells grip. Adhesion molecules on the cell surface (cadherins between cells, integrins to the matrix) link the scaffold through the membrane to the cytoskeleton, so forces are transmitted across a whole organ and every cell can feel where it is and what is pulling on it. The one idea is **mechanical continuity with information in it**: adhesion is not glue but a signalling receptor, and the matrix is not filler but a reservoir of growth factors and a record of the tissue's history. Scar, fibrosis, osteogenesis imperfecta, blistering skin diseases, cancer invasion and wound healing are all stories about this system.

## Core tenets

1. **Collagen gives tensile strength because of its structure, and a single amino acid substitution can destroy it.** Collagen is a triple helix of three polypeptides with glycine at every third position, because only glycine fits at the centre of the helix. Chains are hydroxylated on proline and lysine (needing vitamin C), assembled, secreted as procollagen, trimmed, and cross-linked by lysyl oxidase (needing copper) into fibrils and fibres that bear load like rope ([mechanics](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/mechanics-and-materials.md)). A glycine substitution in type I collagen produces osteogenesis imperfecta: a dominant-negative effect, since one bad chain ruins the whole helix. Scurvy is collagen that cannot be hydroxylated, so wounds reopen and vessels bleed. Collagen is ~30% of body protein.

2. **Elastin and proteoglycans supply the other two mechanical properties.** Elastin is a cross-linked network of hydrophobic chains that stretches and recoils by entropy, giving arteries, lung and skin their elasticity; its loss with age and in emphysema (elastase from neutrophils) is irreversible because adults make little new elastin. Proteoglycans are proteins carrying long sulfated glycosaminoglycan chains ([glycobiology](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/01-macromolecules/carbohydrates-and-glycobiology.md)) whose fixed negative charges bind cations and water, swelling the matrix so it resists compression: cartilage is ~70% water held by aggrecan, and the swelling pressure is what a joint stands on. Heparan sulfate chains also bind growth factors, making the matrix a slow-release store.

3. **Integrins connect matrix to cytoskeleton and signal in both directions.** An integrin is a heterodimer that binds a matrix ligand (fibronectin, laminin, collagen) outside and, through adaptor proteins, actin inside. Binding activates kinases and small GTPases ("outside-in"), so a cell knows it is attached, survives (anoikis prevented), and can proliferate; signals from inside change the integrin's affinity ("inside-out"), which is how a platelet or leukocyte switches from rolling to firm adhesion in seconds. Leukocyte adhesion deficiency, a defect in the beta-2 integrin, leaves neutrophils unable to leave the blood, so infections lack pus.

4. **Cadherins hold cells to each other and define tissue identity.** Cadherins bind cadherins of the same type on neighbouring cells, calcium-dependently, and anchor to actin at adherens junctions and to intermediate filaments at desmosomes. Cells sort by cadherin type and level, so homophilic binding is enough to separate tissues during development. E-cadherin loss is the signature of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, and inherited E-cadherin mutations cause diffuse gastric cancer; autoantibodies against desmosomal cadherins cause pemphigus, in which the epidermis splits into blisters.

5. **The basement membrane is the matrix sheet that organises epithelia and filters.** Every epithelium, muscle fibre and nerve rests on a thin layer of type IV collagen, laminin, nidogen and perlecan. It polarises the cell (basal side defined), is the filter of the glomerulus (its charge and pore size exclude albumin), and is the boundary that a carcinoma must breach to become invasive, which is the formal definition of malignancy in epithelial cancer ([invasion and metastasis](../../08-pathology/03-neoplasia/invasion-metastasis-and-the-tumour-microenvironment.md)). Alport syndrome (type IV collagen mutation) causes kidney failure and deafness; anti-basement-membrane antibodies cause Goodpasture disease.

6. **The matrix is remodelled continuously, and the balance of synthesis and breakdown is a disease axis.** Matrix Metalloproteinases (MMPs), secreted by fibroblasts, macrophages and tumour cells, degrade collagen and other components; tissue inhibitors restrain them. Wound healing, angiogenesis, ovulation, bone growth and uterine involution all need controlled degradation. Too much degradation is emphysema, aneurysm and tumour invasion; too much synthesis is fibrosis. Fibrosis is driven by transforming growth factor beta acting on myofibroblasts, and in liver, lung, kidney and heart it replaces working tissue with collagen that cannot be removed ([tissue repair and fibrosis](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/tissue-repair-regeneration-and-fibrosis.md)).

7. **Cells read the stiffness and geometry of their matrix.** Through integrins and the actin cytoskeleton ([cytoskeleton](../01-cell-structure/the-cytoskeleton-and-cell-motility.md)) a cell pulls on its substrate and senses the resistance; the signal reaches the nucleus via mechanosensitive transcription factors (YAP/TAZ). Stiff matrix promotes proliferation and fibroblast activation, which is why fibrosis is self-perpetuating (stiff scar activates more fibroblasts) and why dense breast tissue and stiff tumour stroma favour malignant growth. Mechanical environment is a signal of the same rank as a growth factor.

8. **Connective tissue disorders show the matrix as a system.** Marfan syndrome (fibrillin-1, the scaffold for elastin and a regulator of transforming growth factor beta) affects aorta, lens and skeleton together; Ehlers–Danlos syndromes (various collagens and processing enzymes) give hyperextensible skin, loose joints and fragile vessels. The organs affected in each syndrome are those where that particular component bears the most load, which is an instance of structure determining function at the level of a whole organism.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Extracellular matrix (ECM) | Secreted network of fibres, proteoglycans and adhesive glycoproteins that cells live in |
| Collagen | Triple-helical fibrous protein giving tensile strength; type I in bone and tendon, IV in basement membranes |
| Elastin | Cross-linked network giving recoil; not replaced in adult life |
| Proteoglycan / glycosaminoglycan | Protein with sulfated sugar chains; holds water and resists compression |
| Integrin | Cell–matrix receptor linking matrix to actin and signalling in both directions |
| Cadherin | Calcium-dependent cell–cell adhesion molecule binding its own type |
| Basement membrane | Thin matrix sheet under epithelia; filter and boundary |
| Matrix metalloproteinase | Zinc protease that remodels matrix; balanced by tissue inhibitors |
| Fibrosis | Replacement of functional tissue by excess collagen |
| Mechanotransduction | Conversion of matrix stiffness and strain into intracellular signals |

## Common misconceptions

- **"The matrix is inert packing between cells."** It stores growth factors, sets stiffness that cells read, and is turned over continuously; it is a signalling environment.
- **"Fibrosis is just too much healing."** It is a distinct self-sustaining state in which stiff matrix and activated myofibroblasts reinforce each other; it does not resolve when the injury stops.
- **"Adhesion molecules only hold cells in place."** Integrins and cadherins are receptors; their engagement decides survival, proliferation and gene expression.
