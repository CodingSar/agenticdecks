## Essence

Two metres of Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) in 46 chromosomes must be shared exactly between two daughters at every one of the ~10^16 divisions of a human lifetime. Mitosis condenses the replicated chromosomes, attaches each sister pair to a bipolar microtubule spindle, verifies that every pair is pulled from both poles, and only then cuts the link between sisters. Meiosis is mitosis modified to halve the chromosome number for sex: one replication, two divisions, with homologous chromosomes paired and recombined in the first. The one idea is **tension-sensing**: a chromosome is correctly attached when pulled from both sides, and anaphase waits until every chromosome reports tension. Errors give aneuploidy, the leading cause of miscarriage and a hallmark of cancer; recombination is why siblings differ.

## Core tenets

1. **Sisters are held together by cohesin from replication to anaphase, and segregation is the controlled destruction of that link.** Cohesin rings encircle the two copies as they are made. In mitosis the spindle pulls sisters apart but cohesin resists until the Anaphase-Promoting Complex destroys securin, releasing separase, which cuts cohesin.

2. **The spindle self-organises, and attachment is corrected by trial and error.** Microtubules growing from the two centrosomes search by dynamic instability ([cytoskeleton](../01-cell-structure/the-cytoskeleton-and-cell-motility.md)) until they capture a kinetochore, the protein machine on each centromere. Correct attachment (sisters to opposite poles) puts the pair under tension; wrong attachments do not. Low tension leaves the kinase Aurora B able to loosen the attachment, so wrong ones are released and retried while right ones are stabilised.

3. **The spindle assembly checkpoint makes anaphase wait for the last chromosome.** An unattached kinetochore emits an inhibitory signal that blocks the Anaphase-Promoting Complex; one unattached kinetochore among 92 holds the whole cell ([checkpoints](the-cell-cycle-and-its-checkpoints.md)). When the signal weakens, through mutation or prolonged drug-induced arrest, cells exit mitosis with the wrong number of chromosomes.

4. **Meiosis makes haploid, genetically unique gametes by two reductions and one shuffle.** In meiosis I, homologous chromosomes pair along their length, exchange segments by crossing over and are pulled to opposite poles while sisters stay together (centromeric cohesin is protected by shugoshin). Meiosis II then separates sisters as in mitosis. Each pair orients independently, giving 2^23 (~8 million) combinations before crossing over is counted, so every gamete is unique. This is the physical basis of Mendel's segregation and independent assortment ([Mendelian inheritance](../../04-genetics-evolution-and-development/01-inheritance/mendelian-inheritance-and-pedigrees.md)) and of linkage: nearby genes travel together in proportion to their distance.

5. **Crossing over is not optional: chiasmata hold homologues together.** A pair with no crossover can separate early and segregate at random. Small chromosomes with few crossovers (21, 22) are most vulnerable, one reason trisomy 21 is the commonest viable autosomal trisomy.

6. **Human female meiosis is slow and error-prone, and the error rate rises with age.** Oocytes begin meiosis in the fetus, arrest in prophase I for 10 to 50 years, and complete meiosis I at ovulation and meiosis II at fertilisation ([gametogenesis](../../06-physiology/09-reproduction-and-the-life-course/gametogenesis-and-the-menstrual-cycle.md)). Cohesin laid down in fetal life must survive those decades unreplaced, and it degrades. Most human aneuploidy is therefore maternal and of meiosis I, and the risk of a trisomic pregnancy rises steeply after about 35 years. Male meiosis runs continuously from puberty with far less aneuploidy but more point mutation.

7. **Aneuploidy is common at conception and lethal in most forms.** A quarter or more of human oocytes are chromosomally abnormal, and aneuploidy is found in about half of first-trimester miscarriages. Autosomal monosomy and most trisomies are incompatible with life; trisomies 21, 18 and 13 and sex-chromosome aneuploidies survive because of small gene content or X inactivation ([cytogenetics](../../04-genetics-evolution-and-development/01-inheritance/chromosomes-and-cytogenetics.md)). The phenotype is dosage: a 1.5-fold change in hundreds of genes at once.

8. **Chromosomal instability is a double-edged hallmark of cancer.** Many tumours missegregate a chromosome every few divisions because of extra centrosomes, weak checkpoints or cohesion defects. This generates diversity for selection to act on, accelerating tumour evolution and drug resistance; but high rates also kill cancer cells, and lagging chromosomes trapped in micronuclei rupture and trigger inflammation. Mitotic error is both an engine of cancer and a therapeutic vulnerability.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Chromatid | One of the two identical copies of a replicated chromosome |
| Cohesin | Ring complex holding sister chromatids together until separase cuts it |
| Kinetochore | Protein machine on the centromere that binds microtubules and reports tension |
| Bi-orientation | Sisters attached to opposite poles; the only configuration under tension |
| Spindle assembly checkpoint | Signal from unattached kinetochores that blocks anaphase |
| Homologous chromosomes | The maternal and paternal copies of one chromosome, paired in meiosis I |
| Crossing over / chiasma | Exchange between homologues; also the link that holds them together |
| Nondisjunction | Failure of chromosomes or chromatids to separate; the cause of aneuploidy |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Chromosomes are pulled apart by the spindle."** They are held together against the spindle's pull; anaphase is triggered by cutting cohesin, not by more force.
- **"Meiosis just halves the chromosome number."** The second division and recombination exist to shuffle alleles, which is the evolutionary point of sex.
- **"Down syndrome is inherited."** About 95% of cases are new nondisjunction events, mostly maternal; only the rare translocation forms run in families.
