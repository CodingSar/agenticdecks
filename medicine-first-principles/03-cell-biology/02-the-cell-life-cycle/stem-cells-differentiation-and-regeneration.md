## Essence

Every cell in the body carries the same genome, yet a neuron, a hepatocyte and a red cell could not be more different. Differentiation is the stable, heritable choice of which genes a cell expresses, and a stem cell is one that has not yet made the final choice: it can renew itself and produce more specialised offspring. Adult tissues that wear out (blood, gut lining, skin, sperm) are maintained by stem cells for life; tissues that do not divide (heart muscle, most of the brain) heal by scar. The single idea is **hierarchy with memory**: a stem cell sits at the top of a lineage tree, its position is held by signals from a niche, and each step down is locked in by chromatin rather than by changing the Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA). Regenerative capacity, cancer stem cells, bone marrow transplantation and cell therapy are all consequences of this organisation.

## Core tenets

1. **Differentiation is epigenetic: the genome is constant, its accessibility is not.** A cell type is a stable pattern of transcription factors, DNA methylation and histone marks that opens some genes and closes others ([gene regulation](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/03-genetic-information-flow/gene-regulation-and-epigenetic-mechanisms.md)). The pattern is copied through mitosis, so a daughter remembers what its mother was. The proof that nothing is lost is nuclear transfer (Gurdon, 1962: a frog from an intestinal nucleus) and reprogramming (Yamanaka, 2006: four transcription factors return a fibroblast to a pluripotent state). Differentiation is a landscape of stable valleys separated by barriers, in Waddington's image, not a one-way chemical conversion.

2. **Potency narrows stepwise, and each step is a transcription factor switch.** The zygote and early blastomeres are totipotent (can form embryo and placenta); embryonic stem cells of the inner cell mass are pluripotent (any cell of the body, not placenta); adult stem cells are multipotent (one tissue's lineages) or unipotent. Each branch is decided by cross-antagonistic transcription factors: in blood, GATA1 versus PU.1 sends a progenitor toward red cells or myeloid cells, and the mutually repressive pair makes the choice stable and binary. A lineage tree is thus a series of bistable switches.

3. **Stem cells self-renew by balancing symmetric and asymmetric divisions, and the niche sets the balance.** A stem cell can produce two stem cells, two committed cells or one of each. Over the population the ratio must average to maintenance; too many symmetric self-renewals is a tumour, too few is exhaustion. The niche (specific neighbouring cells, matrix and local signals such as Wnt, Notch and stem cell factor) holds the cell in place and keeps it undifferentiated; a daughter pushed out of the niche by division differentiates because it loses the signal ([cell communication](../03-cells-in-community/cell-to-cell-communication-and-junctions.md)). In the intestinal crypt, Wnt from Paneth cells at the base maintains the stem cells, and cells moving up the crypt differentiate in the falling gradient, reaching the villus tip and shedding within about 5 days.

4. **Quiescence protects the stem pool.** Most adult stem cells spend most of their time in G0 ([cell cycle](the-cell-cycle-and-its-checkpoints.md)), dividing rarely, while a transit-amplifying compartment of committed progenitors does the bulk of the dividing. This limits replicative [telomere loss](senescence-and-telomeres.md) and mutation in the cells that must last a lifetime, and it is why cytotoxic chemotherapy, which kills cycling cells, spares the stem cells and lets blood counts recover in two to three weeks. A haematopoietic stem cell divides perhaps every few months to a year in an adult.

5. **Regenerative capacity is a tissue's stem cell and progenitor organisation, and it determines how injury heals.** Labile tissues (marrow, epithelia) regenerate fully from stem cells. Stable tissues (liver, kidney tubule, endothelium) are quiescent but their differentiated cells re-enter the cycle on demand: two-thirds of a liver regrows in weeks. Permanent tissues (cardiomyocytes, neurons, lens) have almost no renewal, so a myocardial infarct or a stroke leaves a scar and a deficit. Even a regenerating tissue heals by fibrosis if its scaffold is destroyed ([tissue repair](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/tissue-repair-regeneration-and-fibrosis.md)), which is why cirrhosis follows repeated liver injury despite the hepatocytes' capacity.

6. **Cancer is a caricature of the stem cell hierarchy.** Many tumours contain a minority of cells with stem-like self-renewal that regenerate the tumour after treatment while the bulk of cells cannot; in chronic myeloid leukaemia the transforming mutation (the BCR–ABL fusion) sits in the haematopoietic stem cell, so the disease is cured only when that cell is eliminated ([haematological malignancies](../../12-organ-system-specialties/06-haematology/haematological-malignancies.md)). Tumour cells also reactivate embryonic programmes such as the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition to migrate. The stem cell concept explains relapse after apparent remission.

7. **Stem cells are already medicine.** Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (from marrow, mobilised blood or cord blood) has treated leukaemia and marrow failure since the 1960s and rests on the fact that a small number of stem cells can rebuild an entire blood system. Cultured keratinocyte sheets treat burns; limbal stem cells restore the cornea. Induced pluripotent stem cells avoid the ethical problem of embryos and can be made from the patient, and the first derived therapies (retinal pigment epithelium, dopaminergic neurons, islet cells) entered trials in the 2010s–2020s. Gene therapy of blood disorders (sickle cell disease, beta-thalassaemia) is performed by editing a patient's own haematopoietic stem cells ex vivo and reinfusing them ([advanced therapies](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/biologics-and-advanced-therapies.md)).

8. **Stem cell ageing limits repair in the old.** Haematopoietic stem cells in the elderly are more numerous but less functional and skewed toward myeloid output; muscle satellite cells and neural stem cells decline in number and responsiveness. Clonal haematopoiesis, in which a single mutant stem cell clone takes over a significant fraction of blood production, is found in >10% of people over 70 and raises the risk of leukaemia and, through inflammatory monocytes, of cardiovascular disease. Stem cell exhaustion is counted among the hallmarks of ageing.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Stem cell | A cell that can both self-renew and produce differentiated progeny |
| Potency (toti-, pluri-, multi-, uni-) | The range of cell types a cell can still produce |
| Niche | The local cellular and matrix environment that maintains a stem cell |
| Asymmetric division | One daughter remains a stem cell, the other commits |
| Transit-amplifying cell | Committed progenitor that divides rapidly a limited number of times |
| Lineage commitment | Irreversible narrowing of fate, fixed by cross-antagonistic transcription factors |
| Reprogramming | Returning a differentiated cell to pluripotency with defined factors (iPS cells) |
| Labile / stable / permanent tissue | Tissues that renew continuously, on demand, or not at all |
| Cancer stem cell | Tumour subpopulation with self-renewal that regenerates the tumour |
| Clonal haematopoiesis | Expansion of one mutant stem cell clone in ageing blood |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Differentiated cells have lost genes."** They have silenced them; reprogramming proves the information is intact (red cells, which eject their nucleus, are the exception).
- **"Adult stem cells can become anything."** Adult stem cells are multipotent within a lineage; claims of broad transdifferentiation have mostly not survived rigorous lineage tracing.
- **"More stem cell divisions would mean better regeneration."** Stem cells are protected by dividing rarely; forcing them to cycle exhausts the pool and raises mutation and cancer risk.
