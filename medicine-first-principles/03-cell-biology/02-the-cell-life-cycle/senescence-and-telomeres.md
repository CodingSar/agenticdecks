# Senescence and telomeres

## Essence

A normal human cell in culture divides a limited number of times (the Hayflick limit, ~50 doublings for fetal fibroblasts) and then stops permanently while staying alive: it is senescent. The counter is the telomere, the repetitive cap at each chromosome end that shortens at every replication because Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) polymerase cannot copy the last stretch of a linear molecule. Senescence is also triggered by DNA damage, oncogene activation and stress, so it is best understood as **a permanent exit from the cell cycle that trades cancer risk for the cost of accumulating non-dividing, inflammatory cells**. That trade-off is a leading explanation for why tissues age.

## Core tenets

1. **Linear chromosomes shorten at every replication, and telomeres absorb the loss.** The lagging strand is made in fragments primed by RNA; when the last primer is removed there is no upstream end from which to fill the gap ([DNA replication](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/03-genetic-information-flow/dna-replication-and-repair.md)). Each division loses ~50–100 base pairs from telomeres of ~10–15 kilobases of TTAGGG repeats at birth. Shelterin proteins cap the end so repair machinery does not read it as a break; a telomere too short to be capped is read as exactly that.

2. **Senescence is a DNA damage response made permanent.** A critically short telomere activates ATM and p53, inducing the Cyclin-Dependent Kinase (CDK) inhibitor p21; the p16 pathway locks the retinoblastoma protein in its active state ([checkpoints](the-cell-cycle-and-its-checkpoints.md)). A telomere cannot be repaired, so unlike a checkpoint arrest the stop is stable, reinforced by heterochromatin over proliferation genes.

3. **Telomerase rebuilds telomeres in cells that must divide indefinitely, and cancer reactivates it.** Telomerase is a reverse transcriptase carrying its own RNA template. It is active in germ cells, embryonic cells and, at lower levels, stem cells; most somatic cells silence it. About 85–90% of cancers re-express it and most of the rest lengthen telomeres by recombination, because a tumour that cannot maintain its ends senesces or dies in crisis. Replicative immortality is therefore a [hallmark of cancer](../../08-pathology/03-neoplasia/hallmarks-of-cancer.md), and the telomere clock a tumour suppressor paid for in ageing.

4. **Senescence is a tumour suppressor in the short term.** An activated oncogene (Ras, BRAF) in a normal cell produces not cancer but oncogene-induced senescence through replication stress and p16. Moles are melanocyte clones with an activating BRAF mutation held senescent for decades. A tumour arises only when p53 or p16/Rb is also lost, which is why multiple hits are needed and p16 deletion is among the commonest events in cancer genomes.

5. **Senescent cells secrete.** The Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype (SASP) is a mix of interleukins (IL-6, IL-8), chemokines, matrix metalloproteinases and growth factors. It recruits immune cells to clear the senescent cell and aids wound healing; but when senescent cells accumulate uncleared, the SASP degrades matrix, drives chronic low-grade inflammation, impairs stem cells and can promote nearby premalignant growth.

6. **Accumulated senescent cells are a candidate cause of ageing, and clearing them helps in animals.** Senescent cells reach a few percent of cells in old tissue. In mice, clearing p16-positive cells delays cataract, sarcopenia, atherosclerosis and osteoarthritis and extends healthy lifespan. Senolytic drugs that trigger apoptosis selectively in senescent cells ([cell death](cell-death-apoptosis-necrosis-and-autophagy.md)) entered early human trials in the 2020s; whether they change human ageing is unknown.

7. **Short telomeres are a disease in their own right.** Inherited mutations in telomerase or shelterin cause the telomere biology disorders: dyskeratosis congenita in children, and in adults marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis and cirrhosis, because the tissues that divide most run out first. They show anticipation: each generation inherits shorter telomeres and presents earlier. Leukocyte telomere length is not a validated clinical biomarker of ageing.

8. **Senescence contributes to specific diseases of old age by recognisable mechanisms.** Senescent chondrocytes secrete matrix-degrading enzymes in osteoarthritis; senescent vascular cells destabilise atherosclerotic plaque; senescent alveolar epithelium drives fibroblasts in pulmonary fibrosis; senescent T cells weaken vaccine responses. This is the cellular layer under [diseases of ageing](../../08-pathology/04-genetic-metabolic-and-environmental-disease/diseases-of-aging-and-degeneration.md) and [frailty](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/frailty-multimorbidity-and-polypharmacy.md).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Telomere | TTAGGG repeats at chromosome ends, bound by shelterin, that shorten with replication |
| End-replication problem | Inability of lagging-strand synthesis to copy the final segment of a linear molecule |
| Hayflick limit | The finite number of divisions (~50) of a normal somatic cell in culture |
| Telomerase | Reverse transcriptase with its own RNA template that extends telomeres |
| Oncogene-induced senescence | Arrest of a normal cell after an oncogene fires; a barrier to cancer |
| p16 / p21 | CDK inhibitors that enforce the senescent arrest through Rb and p53 |
| SASP | Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype: inflammatory and matrix-degrading secretions |
| Senolytic | Drug that selectively kills senescent cells |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Telomere shortening is the cause of ageing."** It is one of several interacting mechanisms; mice have long telomeres and short lives.
- **"Senescent cells are dead or dying."** They are alive and secretory for months or years; their persistence, not their death, causes harm.
- **"Activating telomerase would cure ageing."** It would also remove a major barrier to cancer; the trade-off is the point.
