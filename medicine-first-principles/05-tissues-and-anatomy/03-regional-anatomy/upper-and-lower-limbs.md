## Essence

The two limbs are built on one plan (a girdle, one proximal bone, two distal bones, then a hand or foot) tuned to opposite ends of a trade-off: the upper limb for reach and grip, the lower for carrying the body. Everything load-bearing about limb anatomy follows from where nerves and vessels lie relative to bone and fascia. Nerves are injured where they cross a bone or pass through a tunnel; arteries are torn where they are fixed beside a joint; bones die where their blood enters from one end; muscles die when they swell inside a fascial compartment. A limb is therefore read as a map of a few dozen such relations.

## Core tenets

1. **One plan, two settings.** The shoulder girdle hangs from the trunk by muscle and a single strut (the clavicle), the glenoid is shallow and the forearm bones rotate, which gives the hand a hemisphere of reach and makes the shoulder the joint most often dislocated. The pelvic girdle is fused to the sacrum, the acetabulum is deep, the tibia alone bears load and the foot is an arched spring. The lower limb therefore fractures and arthroses where the upper limb dislocates and sprains.

2. **Plexuses mix the roots, and the peripheral nerves are injured at fixed points.** The brachial (C5–T1) and lumbosacral (L1–S4) plexuses reshuffle segments into nerves, each with one or two places where it lies against bone or inside a tunnel. The axillary nerve wraps the surgical neck of the humerus and is stretched by shoulder dislocation; the radial nerve lies in the spiral groove and is cut by a mid-shaft fracture (wrist drop); the median nerve runs with the brachial artery at the elbow, where a child's supracondylar fracture injures both, and through the carpal tunnel, where any swelling compresses it; the ulnar nerve lies behind the medial epicondyle (clawed ring and little fingers). The common fibular nerve winds round the neck of the fibula under the skin, where a cast or crossed leg produces foot drop; the sciatic nerve is torn by posterior hip dislocation; the superior gluteal nerve's failure drops the pelvis in walking.

3. **Each limb segment is a set of osteofascial compartments, and swelling inside one is an emergency.** Deep fascia and bone divide arm, forearm, thigh and leg into compartments each holding a muscle group with its nerve and artery. Bleeding, post-fracture oedema, a tight cast or a crush raises the pressure until the capillaries close while the main artery outside still pulses; the anterior leg after a tibial fracture and the flexor forearm after a supracondylar fracture are the commonest sites. The sign is pain out of proportion and on passive stretch; the treatment is to open the fascia, and hours of delay cost the muscle and nerve.

4. **Arteries run on the flexor side, are fixed at a few joints, and enter some bones from the wrong end.** The brachial artery, medial to the biceps tendon, is kinked by a supracondylar fracture; its radial and ulnar branches join in the palmar arches, so the hand survives loss of one and the radial artery can be cannulated or borrowed for a graft. The femoral artery emerges under the inguinal ligament at the mid-inguinal point with the vein medial and nerve lateral, the main site of vascular access; the popliteal artery is fixed above and below the knee, so knee dislocation tears it. The femoral head is fed by vessels running up the neck inside the capsule, so an intracapsular neck fracture kills the head; the scaphoid is fed from its distal end, so a waist fracture starves the proximal fragment.

5. **Veins and lymph follow two layers, each with a disease of its own.** Deep veins travel with the arteries inside the compartments and depend on the calf pump and valves; the soleal sinuses, where flow is slowest, are where thrombi start in the immobile, and a clot reaching the femoral vein is the one that embolises. Superficial veins run in the fat: the cephalic and basilic in the arm, the great saphenous in front of the medial malleolus, used for cannulas and grafts; when the saphenofemoral valve fails the superficial system carries deep pressure and becomes varicose. Arm lymph drains to the axilla and leg lymph to the groin, so a hand or foot infection shows as a tender node there, and clearance of those nodes leaves lymphoedema.

6. **Each joint's design predicts its injury.** The shoulder dislocates anteriorly, tearing the labrum, and depends on the rotator cuff pressing the head into the socket. Nine flexor tendons and the median nerve share the carpal tunnel. The knee is almost entirely ligament-stabilised: cruciates control front-to-back translation, collaterals resist sideways bending, menisci spread load, and a valgus blow with a twist tears medial collateral, medial meniscus and anterior cruciate together. The ankle is a mortise whose lateral ligaments sprain on inversion and whose fractures are classified by their level relative to the tibiofibular ligament. In the hand, flexor tendon sheaths communicate with the palmar spaces, so an infected sheath destroys tendon gliding within days.

7. **Growth, rotation and segmental supply are visible in the adult limb.** Long bones grow mostly at the knee and away from the elbow, so growth-plate injury and limb-length discrepancy are problems of the knee and shoulder. Limb-bud rotation explains why the dermatomes spiral (C6 to the thumb, L5 to the great toe) and why knee and elbow bend opposite ways. The landmarks for every nerve block, joint injection and fracture reduction are the bony points to which fascia, nerves and vessels are fixed.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Girdle | Ring of bone attaching a limb to the trunk: scapula and clavicle, or the pelvis |
| Brachial / lumbosacral plexus | Networks redistributing roots C5–T1 and L1–S4 into the named limb nerves |
| Spiral groove | Channel on the humeral shaft where the radial nerve lies against bone |
| Carpal tunnel | Space under the flexor retinaculum containing nine flexor tendons and the median nerve |
| Osteofascial compartment | Muscle group with its nerve and artery enclosed by deep fascia and bone |
| Compartment syndrome | Pressure in a closed compartment high enough to stop capillary perfusion of muscle and nerve |
| Femoral triangle | Region below the inguinal ligament where femoral nerve, artery and vein lie lateral to medial |
| Retrograde blood supply | Bone perfused from its distal end (femoral head, scaphoid, talus), vulnerable to necrosis after fracture |
| Calf muscle pump | Compression of the deep veins by the calf muscles that returns blood against gravity |
| Saphenofemoral junction | Where the great saphenous joins the femoral vein; the valve whose failure causes varicose veins |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A fracture is a problem of bone."** The dangerous fractures injure what lies against the bone: the brachial artery and median nerve at the elbow, the radial nerve in the humeral shaft, the popliteal artery at the knee, the compartments of the leg, the femoral head's own blood supply.
- **"Pulses present means the limb is well perfused."** Compartment syndrome stops capillary flow while the main artery still pulses; perfusion is judged at the tissue, by colour, capillary refill, pain and function.
- **"Varicose veins are the disease."** They are the superficial system carrying deep pressure after a valve fails; the disease is the incompetent valve.
