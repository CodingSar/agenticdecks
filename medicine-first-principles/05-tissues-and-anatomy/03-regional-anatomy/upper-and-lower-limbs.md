## Essence

The two limbs are built on one plan (a girdle, one proximal bone, two distal bones, then a hand or foot) tuned to opposite ends of a trade-off: the upper limb for reach and grip, the lower for carrying the body. Everything load-bearing about limb anatomy follows from where nerves and vessels lie relative to bone and fascia. Nerves are injured where they cross a bone or pass through a tunnel; arteries are torn where they are fixed next to a joint; bones die where their blood enters from one end; muscles die when they swell inside a fascial compartment. A limb is therefore read as a map of a few dozen such relations, and the orthopaedic, neurological and vascular leaves above are their consequences.

## Core tenets

1. **One plan, two settings.** The shoulder girdle hangs from the trunk by muscle and a single strut (the clavicle), the glenoid is shallow and the forearm bones rotate, which gives the hand a hemisphere of reach and makes the shoulder the joint most often dislocated. The pelvic girdle is fused to the sacrum, the acetabulum is deep, the tibia alone bears the load (the fibula is a muscle anchor and the lateral wall of the ankle), and the foot is an arched spring. The lower limb therefore fractures and arthroses where the upper limb dislocates and sprains, and the hip, knee and ankle carry forces of several times body weight that the shoulder and elbow never see.

2. **Plexuses mix the roots, and the peripheral nerves are injured at fixed points.** The brachial plexus (C5–T1) and lumbosacral plexus (L1–S4) reshuffle segments into nerves, each of which runs a predictable course and has one or two places where it lies against bone or inside a tunnel. The axillary nerve wraps the surgical neck of the humerus and is stretched by a shoulder dislocation (weak abduction, numb patch over the deltoid). The radial nerve lies in the spiral groove of the humeral shaft and is cut by a mid-shaft fracture (wrist drop). The median nerve runs with the brachial artery in front of the elbow, where a child's supracondylar fracture injures both, and through the carpal tunnel under the flexor retinaculum, where any swelling compresses it (numb thumb, index and middle fingers, wasted thenar muscles). The ulnar nerve lies behind the medial epicondyle, exposed to pressure and fracture (clawed ring and little fingers, weak intrinsic hand muscles). In the leg, the common fibular nerve winds round the neck of the fibula just under the skin, where a cast, a crossed leg or a fracture produces foot drop; the sciatic nerve leaves the pelvis behind the hip and is torn by a posterior dislocation or a badly placed injection; the femoral nerve is damaged by pelvic haematoma and retroperitoneal surgery (a knee that gives way); the superior gluteal nerve supplies the abductors whose failure drops the pelvis in walking.

3. **Each limb segment is a set of osteofascial compartments, and swelling inside one is a surgical emergency.** The deep fascia and the bones divide the arm, forearm, thigh and leg into compartments that each contain a muscle group with its nerve and artery: the forearm into flexor and extensor compartments, the leg into anterior, lateral, superficial posterior and deep posterior. Bleeding, oedema after a fracture or reperfusion, a tight cast or a crush raises the pressure in a compartment until the capillaries close, while the main artery outside it still pulses; the anterior leg after a tibial fracture and the flexor forearm after a supracondylar or forearm fracture are the commonest sites. The sign is pain out of proportion and pain on passive stretch of the muscles inside; the treatment is to cut the fascia open, and a delay of hours costs the muscle and the nerve.

4. **Arteries run on the flexor side, are fixed at a few joints, and enter some bones from the wrong end.** The brachial artery, medial to the biceps tendon at the elbow, is the pulse taken for blood pressure and the vessel kinked by a supracondylar fracture; it divides into radial and ulnar arteries that join in the palmar arches, which is why the hand survives occlusion of one and why the radial artery can be cannulated or borrowed for a graft. The femoral artery emerges under the inguinal ligament at the mid-inguinal point with the vein medial and the nerve lateral, the most used site for arterial and venous access; it becomes the popliteal artery behind the knee, fixed above and below so that a knee dislocation tears it and its collaterals are poor; below the knee the anterior and posterior tibial arteries give the pulses at the ankle whose pressure, compared with the arm, measures peripheral arterial disease. The femoral head is supplied by vessels that run up the neck inside the capsule, so an intracapsular fracture of the neck cuts them and the head dies; the scaphoid is fed from its distal end, so a fracture across its waist starves the proximal fragment; the talus is similar. These are the bones whose fractures are treated by replacement or by prolonged, careful fixation.

5. **Veins and lymph follow two layers, and each has a disease of its own.** Deep veins travel with the arteries inside the compartments and depend on the calf muscle pump and their valves to return blood against gravity; the soleal sinuses, where flow is slowest, are where deep venous thrombi start in the immobile, and a clot that reaches the femoral or iliac vein is the one that embolises to the lung. Superficial veins run in the subcutaneous fat: the cephalic and basilic veins of the arm and the great saphenous vein (in front of the medial malleolus, with its nerve beside it, then up the medial leg to the groin) are where cannulas go and where grafts are harvested; when the valve at the saphenofemoral junction fails, the superficial system carries deep pressure and becomes varicose. Lymph from the arm drains to the axillary nodes and from the leg to the inguinal nodes, so a hand or foot infection presents as red streaks up the limb and a tender node in the axilla or groin, and clearance or irradiation of those nodes leaves lymphoedema of the limb.

6. **Each joint's design predicts its injury.** The shoulder dislocates anteriorly, tearing the labrum, and depends on the rotator cuff pressing the head into the socket, so cuff tears cause weakness and impingement. The elbow is a hinge plus a pivot; the radial head is pulled from its ligament in small children. At the wrist, nine flexor tendons and the median nerve share the carpal tunnel. The hip's deep socket and strong capsule make it stable until the neck weakens with age. The knee is the largest joint and almost entirely ligament-stabilised: the cruciates control front-to-back translation, the collaterals resist sideways bending, the menisci spread load, and a valgus blow with a twist tears medial collateral, medial meniscus and anterior cruciate together. The ankle is a mortise whose lateral ligaments are sprained by inversion and whose fractures are classified by their level relative to the ligament binding tibia to fibula. In the hand, the flexor tendons run in synovial sheaths that communicate with the palmar spaces, so an infected sheath spreads and destroys tendon gliding within days.

7. **Growth, rotation and segmental supply are visible in the adult limb.** Long bones grow mostly at the knee and away from the elbow, so growth-plate injury and limb-length discrepancy are problems of the knee and shoulder. The embryonic rotation of the limb buds explains why the dermatomes spiral (C6 to the thumb, L5 to the great toe), why the upper limb's flexors face forward and the lower limb's backward, and why the knee and elbow bend in opposite directions. The surface landmarks for every nerve block, joint injection and fracture reduction are the bony points that the fascia, nerves and vessels are fixed to.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Girdle | The ring of bone attaching a limb to the trunk: scapula and clavicle, or the pelvis |
| Brachial / lumbosacral plexus | Networks redistributing roots C5–T1 and L1–S4 into the named limb nerves |
| Spiral groove | Channel on the humeral shaft where the radial nerve lies against bone |
| Carpal tunnel | Space under the flexor retinaculum containing nine flexor tendons and the median nerve |
| Osteofascial compartment | Muscle group with its nerve and artery enclosed by deep fascia and bone |
| Compartment syndrome | Pressure in a closed compartment high enough to stop capillary perfusion of muscle and nerve |
| Femoral triangle | Region below the inguinal ligament where the femoral nerve, artery and vein lie lateral to medial |
| Retrograde blood supply | Bone perfused from its distal end (femoral head, scaphoid, talus), vulnerable to fracture-induced necrosis |
| Calf muscle pump | Compression of the deep veins by the calf muscles that returns blood against gravity |
| Saphenofemoral junction | Where the great saphenous vein joins the femoral vein; the valve whose failure causes varicose veins |
| Mortise | The tibiofibular socket that grips the talus at the ankle |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A fracture is a problem of bone."** The dangerous fractures are those that injure what lies against the bone: the brachial artery and median nerve at the elbow, the radial nerve in the humeral shaft, the popliteal artery at the knee, the compartments of the leg, and the femoral head's own blood supply.
- **"Pulses present means the limb is well perfused."** Compartment syndrome and small-vessel disease stop capillary flow while the main artery still pulses; perfusion is judged at the tissue, by colour, capillary refill, pain and function.
- **"Varicose veins are the disease."** They are the superficial system carrying deep venous pressure after a valve fails; the disease is the incompetent valve, and the superficial veins are its symptom.
