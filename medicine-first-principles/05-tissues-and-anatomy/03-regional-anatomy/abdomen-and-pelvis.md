## Essence

The abdomen and pelvis hold the gut, its glands, the kidneys and the organs of reproduction inside one peritoneal sac and a muscular wall that has to be both strong and openable. The region's anatomy is the embryo's anatomy made permanent: the gut is divided into three territories by the three arteries it was given before it rotated, every organ keeps the nerve supply and the lymph drainage of the level it came from, and the holes through which the testis descended and the umbilical vessels ran remain as the weak points through which hernias emerge. The single most consequential relation is the portal vein: all the blood from the gut passes through the liver before reaching the heart, so what the gut absorbs the liver sees first, and when the liver scars the gut's veins burst at the points where they meet the systemic circulation.

## Core tenets

1. **Three arteries divide the gut into three territories, each with its own nerve, pain and watershed.** The coeliac trunk (T12) supplies the foregut, from the lower oesophagus to the middle of the duodenum together with the liver, pancreas and spleen; the superior mesenteric artery (L1) supplies the midgut, from there to two thirds along the transverse colon, including the appendix; the inferior mesenteric artery (L3) supplies the hindgut to the upper rectum. Each territory sends its pain to one place (epigastrium, periumbilical, suprapubic), and the joins between them, at the splenic flexure and the rectosigmoid junction, are the watersheds that infarct when pressure falls. Sudden occlusion of the superior mesenteric artery kills the whole midgut, with pain out of proportion to the signs because the parietal peritoneum is not yet involved. During development the midgut rotated 270 degrees anticlockwise around that artery, which placed the caecum in the right iliac fossa and left the transverse colon in front of the duodenum; incomplete rotation leaves a narrow mesenteric root around which the bowel can twist.

2. **The portal vein makes the liver the gatekeeper of everything absorbed, and its pressure problems appear at the borders.** The superior mesenteric and splenic veins join behind the pancreas to form the portal vein, which supplies about three quarters of the liver's blood at a pressure of 5–10 mmHg before it drains by the hepatic veins into the inferior vena cava. Drugs and nutrients therefore pass the liver before the body (first-pass metabolism), and the liver can be seeded by tumour cells from any gut cancer. When cirrhosis raises the resistance of the sinusoids, portal blood finds the systemic veins at four junctions: the lower oesophagus (varices that bleed torrentially), the umbilicus (dilated veins radiating from it), the rectum and the retroperitoneum. Fluid filters into the peritoneum as ascites, the spleen enlarges and destroys platelets, and gut-derived ammonia bypasses the liver to reach the brain. The liver's eight segments each have their own portal inflow and biliary drainage, so a surgeon can remove any of them along a plane; the common bile duct and pancreatic duct open together at the ampulla, so one gallstone there causes jaundice and pancreatitis at once.

3. **The peritoneal spaces decide where blood and pus go and how a perforation presents.** Supine, the most dependent space in the upper abdomen is between the liver and the right kidney, which is why a few hundred millilitres of free blood are first seen there on ultrasound; upright, fluid runs down the paracolic gutters into the pelvis. The lesser sac behind the stomach collects pancreatic fluid as a pseudocyst. A perforated anterior duodenal ulcer or diverticulum releases gas that rises under the diaphragm and is seen on an erect chest radiograph; a posterior duodenal ulcer erodes instead into the gastroduodenal artery and bleeds. Perforations of retroperitoneal segments (posterior duodenum, ascending or descending colon, rectum below the peritoneal reflection) spare the peritoneal signs and are missed for that reason. The greater omentum migrates to and walls off inflammation, so an appendix or diverticulum can perforate into a contained abscess.

4. **The anterior wall is strongest where it is thickest and fails where things pass through.** Three flat muscles and the rectus run in sheaths that fuse at the midline linea alba, which is where surgeons enter and where incisional hernias form. The inguinal canal is the tunnel the testis took through the wall, running from the deep ring just lateral to the inferior epigastric artery to the superficial ring above the pubic tubercle. An indirect hernia follows that path through the deep ring, often in a congenitally patent sac, and is commonest in young males; a direct hernia pushes through the weakened posterior wall medial to the artery in older men. The femoral canal, below the inguinal ligament and medial to the femoral vein, admits a hernia with a narrow rigid neck that strangulates readily and is commoner in women. The umbilicus is the third weak point.

5. **The kidneys and ureters are retroperitoneal and their course crosses the surgeon's path.** The kidneys lie against the posterior wall from T12 to L3 inside a fascial envelope, move with the diaphragm, and are supplied by end arteries, so a renal embolus infarcts a wedge. The ureters descend on the psoas, cross the pelvic brim at the division of the common iliac artery and run under the uterine artery or the vas deferens to enter the bladder obliquely; they are narrowest at the renal pelvis, the pelvic brim and the bladder wall, where stones lodge, and their pain travels along T10–L1 from the loin to the groin and the testis or labium. The ureter is tied or cut most often in hysterectomy, where it lies a centimetre from the uterine artery, and in colectomy, where it lies behind the mesentery; the left gonadal vein drains into the left renal vein rather than the cava, which is why varicoceles are mostly left-sided.

6. **The pelvis is a ring of bone with a muscular floor, and its nerves come from two segmental levels.** A ring breaks in two places, and the veins on its inner surface bleed into the retroperitoneum without a wall to stop them. The pelvic floor (levator ani) holds the bladder, uterus and rectum against the pressure of standing, coughing and lifting; stretched or torn in childbirth, it lets them prolapse and the sphincters leak. Parasympathetic fibres from S2–S4 empty the bladder and rectum and produce erection, while sympathetic fibres from T11–L2 close the bladder neck and drive ejaculation, and the pudendal nerve (S2–S4) supplies perineal sensation and the voluntary sphincters, so the level of a spinal injury predicts bladder, bowel and sexual function. In the female pelvis the uterus lies between bladder and rectum, the ovarian vessels enter over the pelvic brim, and a ruptured ectopic pregnancy bleeds into the peritoneum and is felt at the shoulder tip; in the male the prostate encircles the urethra below the bladder and is examined through the rectal wall, and its enlargement obstructs the outflow.

7. **Lymph follows the arteries and the embryo, so node spread is predictable.** Lymph from the gut follows the three arteries to nodes in front of the aorta and then to the cisterna chyli and thoracic duct; a cancer of the stomach can therefore present as a node at the root of the left neck where the duct ends. The testis drains to para-aortic nodes at L2, the level from which it descended, while the scrotum drains to the groin, so a testicular tumour spreads to the back and a scrotal infection to the inguinal nodes. The rectum above the anal canal's pectinate line drains inward to the inferior mesenteric and internal iliac nodes; below it, the anal canal drains to the groin. The uterus and cervix drain to the iliac nodes and, along the round ligament, to the groin.

8. **The great vessels lie against the spine and their relations explain a handful of dangerous syndromes.** The aorta enters at T12, gives the three gut arteries and the renal arteries, and divides at L4 (the umbilicus) into the common iliacs; aneurysms form mostly below the renal arteries and rupture into the retroperitoneum, which may tamponade the bleed for long enough to reach an operating theatre. The inferior vena cava lies to the right; the left renal vein crosses in front of the aorta beneath the superior mesenteric artery and can be pinched there, and the third part of the duodenum passes through the same angle and can be compressed by it in the very thin.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Foregut / midgut / hindgut | The three embryonic gut territories, supplied by the coeliac, superior mesenteric and inferior mesenteric arteries |
| Portal vein | Vessel carrying all venous blood from the gut and spleen through the liver before it reaches the heart |
| Portosystemic anastomosis | Junction of portal and systemic veins (oesophagus, umbilicus, rectum, retroperitoneum) that dilates in portal hypertension |
| Hepatic segment | One of eight units of liver with its own portal inflow and bile drainage |
| Hepatorenal recess | Space between liver and right kidney; the most dependent part of the upper abdomen when supine |
| Retroperitoneal perforation | Rupture of a gut segment behind the peritoneum, sparing peritoneal signs |
| Inguinal canal | Oblique passage through the lower abdominal wall taken by the spermatic cord or round ligament |
| Indirect / direct / femoral hernia | Hernia through the deep ring, through the posterior canal wall, or under the inguinal ligament respectively |
| Ureteric constrictions | Renal pelvis, pelvic brim and bladder wall: where stones lodge |
| Pelvic floor | Levator ani and associated muscles supporting the pelvic organs against intra-abdominal pressure |
| Pectinate line | Junction in the anal canal above which lymph drains inward and below which it drains to the groin |

- **Below:** the peritoneal geography is the [serous cavity and hernia](../02-body-organization/body-cavities-fascia-and-compartments.md) rules in one region; the three gut arteries, their watersheds and the portal system are the clearest cases in [vascular architecture](../02-body-organization/vascular-and-lymphatic-architecture.md); the three pain sites and the pelvic outflows are the [segmental map](../02-body-organization/segmental-innervation-dermatomes-and-referred-pain.md); the gut lining is [epithelium](../01-tissues/epithelium.md) and the whole arrangement was fixed by the rotation and descent described in [organogenesis](../../04-genetics-evolution-and-development/03-development/germ-layers-and-organogenesis.md).
- **Above:** the surgical [acute abdomen](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/the-acute-abdomen.md) is this leaf's spaces, territories and pains read at the bedside; the [liver as metabolic hub](../../06-physiology/06-gastrointestinal-system/the-liver-as-metabolic-hub.md) and the complications of [liver disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/04-gastroenterology-and-hepatology/liver-disease.md) follow from the portal vein; [labour and delivery](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/labour-delivery-and-the-puerperium.md) is the pelvic ring and floor under load; abdominal [ultrasound](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/03-imaging-and-physiological-measurement/ultrasound.md) looks first in the dependent spaces named here.
- **Siblings:** the diaphragm's openings and the liver and spleen under the ribs are shared with the [thorax](thorax.md); the femoral vessels and nerve pass from here into the [lower limb](upper-and-lower-limbs.md), and the groin nodes receive lymph from both.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Abdominal pain is felt where the organ is."** Visceral pain is felt in the midline at the level of the organ's embryonic territory; only when the parietal peritoneum is inflamed does pain move to the organ's actual position.
- **"The liver's blood comes from the hepatic artery."** About three quarters comes from the portal vein, at low pressure and low oxygen; the liver is the one large organ perfused mostly by venous blood.
- **"A small hernia is a safe hernia."** The femoral hernia is small, often unnoticed and the most likely to strangulate, because its neck is narrow and rigid.
