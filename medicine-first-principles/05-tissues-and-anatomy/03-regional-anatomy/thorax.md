## Essence

The thorax is a bellows with a pump inside it. The rib cage and diaphragm change the volume of two sealed pleural sacs, and the lungs follow because a film of fluid couples them to the wall; between the sacs, the mediastinum carries the heart in its own inextensible sac together with every tube that connects head to abdomen. The region's medicine follows from three structural facts: the pleural coupling can be broken, the pericardium and the mediastinum have no spare room, and the heart's own arteries and the lungs' bronchi branch into territories that fail one at a time. Knowing which structure lies against which is what turns a chest radiograph, a stab wound or an electrocardiogram into a diagnosis.

## Core tenets

1. **Breathing is a volume change transmitted across a sealed space.** The diaphragm does most of the work of quiet breathing, descending like a piston; the ribs rotate at their vertebral joints to lift the sternum and widen the chest. Between the lung and the wall, the pleural space holds only a film of fluid at a pressure a few centimetres of water below atmospheric, so when the wall expands the lung must follow. Air entering that space breaks the coupling: the lung collapses toward its hilum and the wall springs out, and if the leak acts as a one-way valve (tension pneumothorax) pressure rises until the mediastinum is pushed across and the great veins are kinked. Multiple rib fractures that free a segment of wall (flail chest) let it move inward on inspiration. The parietal pleura is supplied by the intercostal nerves and the phrenic nerve, so its inflammation hurts sharply with each breath, at the chest wall or, from the diaphragmatic pleura, at the shoulder tip; the visceral pleura and the lung itself feel nothing.

2. **The mediastinum is full, and a mass announces itself by what it presses on.** Behind the sternum lie, from front to back, the thymus remnant, the great veins and the heart, the trachea and the aortic arch, the oesophagus and descending aorta, and the thoracic duct and azygos veins against the spine. The trachea divides at the sternal angle (T4–T5). A right-sided tumour or node compresses the superior vena cava and swells the face and arms; a left hilar tumour or an aortic aneurysm paralyses the left recurrent laryngeal nerve and the voice; an apical lung tumour invades the lower brachial plexus and the sympathetic chain, wasting the hand and dropping the eyelid; an enlarged left atrium presses the oesophagus and causes dysphagia; anything irritating the phrenic nerve paralyses the diaphragm on that side. Infection reaching the mediastinum from the neck or a torn oesophagus spreads through loose connective tissue with no barrier.

3. **The heart's position and its sac explain its injuries and its signs.** The right ventricle lies directly behind the sternum and is the chamber a knife reaches; the left ventricle forms the apex, felt in the fifth left intercostal space in the midclavicular line, and most of the left border; the left atrium is the most posterior chamber, resting on the oesophagus, which is why an ultrasound probe in the oesophagus sees it best. The fibrous pericardium does not stretch acutely, so 150–200 mL of blood from a stab or a ruptured infarct stops the heart filling (tamponade) with a rising venous pressure, a falling arterial pressure and quiet heart sounds, while a slow effusion of a litre may be tolerated. A fibrous skeleton at the atrioventricular junction insulates the atria from the ventricles electrically, so the conduction system's bundle is the only path and any extra muscular bridge across the ring is a short circuit.

4. **The coronary arteries are end arteries with fixed territories, so infarcts map onto the electrocardiogram.** The left main artery divides into the left anterior descending (anterior wall, the anterior two thirds of the septum and the apex) and the circumflex (lateral wall); the right coronary supplies the right ventricle, the inferior wall and, in about 85% of people, the posterior septum and the atrioventricular node, and in most people the sinoatrial node. Collaterals between them are scant unless a stenosis has grown slowly, so an acute occlusion kills a wedge of ventricle whose position is read from which leads show injury: anterior leads for the left anterior descending, inferior for the right coronary, lateral for the circumflex. Because the left ventricle compresses its own vessels during systole, coronary flow to it occurs in diastole, and a fast heart rate shortens diastole and starves the muscle that is working hardest.

5. **The bronchial tree branches into territories that can be removed, infected and aspirated into one at a time.** The right lung has three lobes and the left two, each divided into bronchopulmonary segments with their own bronchus and artery, so a surgeon can take out a segment or a lobe along a plane with little bleeding, and pneumonia often fills one segment or lobe sharply. The right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical than the left, so inhaled objects, aspirated vomit and an endotracheal tube pushed too far go right. Gravity decides which segments aspirate: the posterior segments of the upper lobes and the apical segments of the lower lobes in someone lying supine, the lower lobes in someone upright. The lungs have two arterial supplies, the pulmonary arteries carrying venous blood for gas exchange and the bronchial arteries carrying systemic blood to the airways, so a pulmonary embolus often does not infarct the lung it blocks.

6. **The chest wall has a predictable layout, used at every needle and every breast operation.** Each intercostal nerve, artery and vein run in the groove under the rib above, so a needle or drain is placed on the upper border of the rib below, and intercostal pain and herpes zoster follow a rib's dermatome round the chest (T4 at the nipple, T10 at the umbilicus). Around three quarters of the lymph from the breast drains to the axillary nodes and most of the rest to nodes along the internal thoracic artery, which is why the axilla is staged in breast cancer and why the long thoracic nerve (serratus anterior, a winged scapula if cut) and the thoracodorsal nerve are at risk there. The diaphragm's domes rise to the fifth rib on the right over the liver and slightly lower on the left, so a penetrating wound below the nipple line may have entered the abdomen, and the spleen and liver lie under ribs 9–11 where a fractured rib can lacerate them.

7. **The diaphragm is a wall with three doors and one nerve supply from far away.** The inferior vena cava passes through the central tendon at T8, the oesophagus with the vagal trunks through the muscular hiatus at T10, and the aorta with the thoracic duct behind the crura at T12. The oesophageal hiatus is the weak point: the stomach slides or rolls through it in a hiatus hernia, and the lower oesophageal sphincter depends partly on the pinch of the crura around it. The muscle is supplied only by the phrenic nerves (C3–C5), which descend the whole length of the mediastinum on the pericardium, so a lesion in the neck, the mediastinum or the cord above C3 paralyses it and the dome rises on the chest radiograph.

8. **The aortic arch is tethered at one point, and that is where it tears.** The arch gives off the brachiocephalic, left common carotid and left subclavian arteries and is fixed just beyond the last of these by the ligamentum arteriosum (the closed ductus) at the aortic isthmus. In a deceleration injury the mobile heart and arch swing against the fixed descending aorta and the wall tears at the isthmus; the same point is where congenital coarctation narrows the aorta. The subclavian artery and the brachial plexus leave the chest over the first rib between the scalene muscles, and an extra cervical rib or a tight scalene can compress them there.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Pleural coupling | The negative-pressure fluid film that makes the lung follow the chest wall |
| Tension pneumothorax | One-way leak of air into the pleural space that shifts the mediastinum and stops venous return |
| Mediastinum | The space between the pleural sacs containing the heart, great vessels, trachea, oesophagus and thoracic duct |
| Sternal angle | Manubriosternal joint at the second rib: the level (T4–T5) of the tracheal bifurcation and the top of the heart |
| Cardiac tamponade | Pericardial fluid under pressure preventing the heart from filling |
| Coronary dominance | Which coronary artery supplies the posterior septum and atrioventricular node; the right in about 85% |
| Bronchopulmonary segment | Unit of lung with its own bronchus and artery; the basis of segmental resection and segmental pneumonia |
| Dual pulmonary blood supply | Pulmonary arteries for gas exchange and bronchial arteries for the airways |
| Costal groove | Channel under each rib's inferior border carrying the intercostal vein, artery and nerve |
| Diaphragmatic openings | Caval (T8), oesophageal (T10) and aortic (T12) |
| Aortic isthmus | Narrow tethered segment just beyond the left subclavian artery; the site of traumatic rupture and coarctation |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A chest drain goes in low, where the fluid is."** The liver and spleen rise to the fifth rib under the domes; drains go in the safe triangle on the lateral chest, above the rib below, where there is only pleura behind the wall.
- **"Pulmonary embolism causes lung infarction."** Usually not, because the bronchial arteries keep the airway tissue alive; the harm is to gas exchange and to the right ventricle, which faces a sudden rise in resistance.
- **"The heart is on the left."** Two thirds of it lies left of the midline, but the right atrium forms the right border and the right ventricle lies behind the sternum; a central stab wound hits the right heart.
