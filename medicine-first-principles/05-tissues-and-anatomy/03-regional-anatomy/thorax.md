## Essence

The thorax is a bellows with a pump inside it. The rib cage and diaphragm change the volume of two sealed pleural sacs and the lungs follow because a film of fluid couples them to the wall; between the sacs the mediastinum carries the heart in its own inextensible sac and every tube connecting head to abdomen. The region's medicine follows from three structural facts: the pleural coupling can be broken, the pericardium and mediastinum have no spare room, and the heart's arteries and the lungs' bronchi branch into territories that fail one at a time. Knowing what lies against what turns a chest radiograph, a stab wound or an electrocardiogram into a diagnosis.

## Core tenets

1. **Breathing is a volume change transmitted across a sealed space.** The diaphragm does most of the work of quiet breathing, descending like a piston; the ribs rotate to lift the sternum and widen the chest. The pleural space holds only a film of fluid a few centimetres of water below atmospheric pressure, so the lung must follow the wall. Air entering it breaks the coupling: the lung collapses toward its hilum, and if the leak is a one-way valve (tension pneumothorax) the mediastinum is pushed across and the great veins kinked. Parietal pleura is supplied by intercostal and phrenic nerves, so its inflammation hurts sharply with each breath at the chest wall or, from the diaphragmatic pleura, at the shoulder tip; the lung itself feels nothing.

2. **The mediastinum is full, and a mass announces itself by what it presses on.** From front to back lie the thymus remnant, the great veins and heart, the trachea and aortic arch, the oesophagus and descending aorta, and the thoracic duct and azygos veins against the spine; the trachea divides at the sternal angle (T4–T5). A right-sided tumour compresses the superior vena cava and swells face and arms; a left hilar tumour or aortic aneurysm paralyses the left recurrent laryngeal nerve and the voice; an apical tumour invades the lower brachial plexus and sympathetic chain, wasting the hand and dropping the eyelid; an enlarged left atrium presses the oesophagus; anything irritating a phrenic nerve paralyses half the diaphragm.

3. **The heart's position and its sac explain its injuries and signs.** The right ventricle lies behind the sternum and is the chamber a knife reaches; the left ventricle forms the apex, felt in the fifth left intercostal space in the midclavicular line; the left atrium is most posterior, resting on the oesophagus, which is why a probe there images it best. The fibrous pericardium does not stretch acutely, so 150–200 mL of blood from a stab or ruptured infarct stops filling (tamponade): rising venous pressure, falling arterial pressure, quiet heart sounds. The fibrous ring at the atrioventricular junction insulates atria from ventricles electrically, so the conducting bundle is the only path and any extra muscular bridge is a short circuit.

4. **The coronaries are end arteries with fixed territories, so infarcts map onto the electrocardiogram.** The left main divides into the left anterior descending (anterior wall, anterior septum, apex) and the circumflex (lateral wall); the right coronary supplies the right ventricle, the inferior wall and, in about 85% of people, the posterior septum and the atrioventricular node. Collaterals are scant unless a stenosis grew slowly, so acute occlusion kills a wedge whose position is read from which leads show injury: anterior leads for the left anterior descending, inferior for the right coronary, lateral for the circumflex. The left ventricle compresses its own vessels in systole, so its coronary flow is diastolic, and a fast rate starves the muscle working hardest.

5. **The bronchial tree branches into territories that are resected, infected and aspirated into one at a time.** Each lobe (three right, two left) is divided into bronchopulmonary segments with their own bronchus and artery, so a surgeon can remove one along a bloodless plane and pneumonia often fills one sharply. The right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical, so inhaled objects and over-advanced endotracheal tubes go right; supine, aspirate falls into the posterior upper-lobe and apical lower-lobe segments, upright into the lower lobes. The lungs have two supplies, pulmonary arteries for gas exchange and bronchial arteries for the airways, so a pulmonary embolus usually does not infarct the lung it blocks.

6. **The chest wall has a layout used at every needle and every breast operation.** Each intercostal nerve, artery and vein run in the groove under the rib above, so a drain is placed on the upper border of the rib below; intercostal pain and shingles follow a rib's dermatome (T4 nipple, T10 umbilicus). About three quarters of breast lymph drains to the axillary nodes and most of the rest to the internal thoracic chain, so the axilla is staged in breast cancer and the long thoracic nerve (serratus anterior; winged scapula) is at risk there. The diaphragm's domes rise to the fifth rib, so a wound below the nipple line may have entered the abdomen, and the spleen and liver under ribs 9–11 are lacerated by their fractures.

7. **The diaphragm is a wall with three doors and a nerve from far away.** The inferior vena cava passes at T8, the oesophagus with the vagal trunks at T10, the aorta with the thoracic duct at T12. The oesophageal hiatus is the weak point through which the stomach herniates. The muscle is supplied only by the phrenic nerves (C3–C5), which descend the whole mediastinum on the pericardium, so a lesion in the neck, mediastinum or cord above C3 paralyses it and the dome rises on the radiograph.

8. **The aortic arch is tethered at one point, and that is where it tears.** The arch gives off the brachiocephalic, left common carotid and left subclavian arteries and is fixed just beyond the last by the ligamentum arteriosum at the isthmus. In deceleration the mobile heart and arch swing against the fixed descending aorta and the wall tears there; the same point is where coarctation narrows. The subclavian artery and brachial plexus leave the chest over the first rib between the scalenes, where a cervical rib can compress them.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Pleural coupling | The negative-pressure fluid film that makes the lung follow the chest wall |
| Tension pneumothorax | One-way leak of air into the pleural space that shifts the mediastinum and stops venous return |
| Mediastinum | Space between the pleural sacs holding heart, great vessels, trachea, oesophagus and thoracic duct |
| Sternal angle | Manubriosternal joint at the second rib: the level (T4–T5) of the tracheal bifurcation |
| Cardiac tamponade | Pericardial fluid under pressure preventing the heart from filling |
| Coronary dominance | Which artery supplies the posterior septum and atrioventricular node; the right in about 85% |
| Bronchopulmonary segment | Unit of lung with its own bronchus and artery; basis of segmental resection and pneumonia |
| Costal groove | Channel under each rib carrying the intercostal vein, artery and nerve |
| Diaphragmatic openings | Caval (T8), oesophageal (T10) and aortic (T12) |
| Aortic isthmus | Tethered segment just beyond the left subclavian; site of traumatic rupture and coarctation |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A chest drain goes in low, where the fluid is."** The liver and spleen rise to the fifth rib; drains go in the safe triangle on the lateral chest, where only pleura lies behind the wall.
- **"Pulmonary embolism causes lung infarction."** Usually not, because the bronchial arteries keep the tissue alive; the harm is to gas exchange and to the right ventricle facing a sudden rise in resistance.
- **"The heart is on the left."** Two thirds lies left of the midline, but the right ventricle lies behind the sternum; a central stab wound hits the right heart.
