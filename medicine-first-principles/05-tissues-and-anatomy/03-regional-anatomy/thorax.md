## Essence

The thorax is a bellows with a pump inside it. The rib cage and diaphragm change the volume of two sealed pleural sacs and the lungs follow because a film of fluid couples them to the wall; between the sacs the mediastinum carries the heart in its own inextensible sac and every tube connecting head to abdomen. Three structural facts drive the region's medicine: the pleural coupling can be broken, the pericardium and mediastinum have no spare room, and the coronary arteries and bronchi branch into territories that fail one at a time.

## Core tenets

1. **Breathing is a volume change transmitted across a sealed space.** The diaphragm does most of the work of quiet breathing; the ribs rotate to widen the chest. The pleural space holds a film of fluid a few centimetres of water below atmospheric pressure, so the lung must follow the wall. Air entering it collapses the lung; if the leak is a one-way valve (tension pneumothorax) the mediastinum is pushed across and the great veins kinked. Parietal pleura has somatic innervation, so its inflammation hurts with each breath, at the chest wall or (from the diaphragm) the shoulder tip; the lung itself feels nothing.

2. **The mediastinum is full, and a mass announces itself by what it presses on.** The trachea divides at the sternal angle (T4–T5) behind the great vessels and in front of the oesophagus. A right-sided tumour compresses the superior vena cava and swells face and arms; a left hilar tumour or aortic aneurysm paralyses the left recurrent laryngeal nerve and the voice; an apical tumour invades the lower brachial plexus and sympathetic chain, wasting the hand and dropping the eyelid.

3. **The heart's position and its sac explain its injuries and signs.** The right ventricle lies behind the sternum and is the chamber a knife reaches; the left ventricle forms the apex, felt in the fifth left intercostal space; the left atrium is most posterior, on the oesophagus. The fibrous pericardium does not stretch acutely, so 150–200 mL of blood from a stab or ruptured infarct stops filling (tamponade): rising venous pressure, falling arterial pressure, quiet heart sounds. The fibrous atrioventricular ring insulates atria from ventricles electrically, so the conducting bundle is the only path and any extra muscular bridge is a short circuit.

4. **The coronaries are end arteries with fixed territories, so infarcts map onto the electrocardiogram.** The left anterior descending supplies the anterior wall, septum and apex, the circumflex the lateral wall, and the right coronary the right ventricle, inferior wall and, in about 85% of people, the atrioventricular node. Collaterals are scant unless a stenosis grew slowly, so acute occlusion kills a wedge whose position is read from which leads show injury. The left ventricle compresses its own vessels in systole, so its coronary flow is diastolic and a fast rate starves the muscle working hardest.

5. **The bronchial tree branches into territories resected, infected and aspirated into one at a time.** Each lobe is divided into bronchopulmonary segments with their own bronchus and artery, so a surgeon can remove one along a bloodless plane and pneumonia often fills one sharply. The right main bronchus is wider and more vertical, so inhaled objects and over-advanced tubes go right. The lungs have two supplies, pulmonary arteries for gas exchange and bronchial arteries for the airways, so a pulmonary embolus usually does not infarct the lung it blocks.

6. **The chest wall has a layout used at every needle and every breast operation.** Each intercostal nerve, artery and vein run in the groove under the rib above, so a drain is placed on the upper border of the rib below; intercostal pain and shingles follow a rib's dermatome (T4 nipple, T10 umbilicus). About three quarters of breast lymph drains to the axillary nodes, so the axilla is staged in breast cancer and the long thoracic nerve is at risk there. The diaphragm's domes rise to the fifth rib, so a wound below the nipple line may have entered the abdomen, and the spleen and liver under ribs 9–11 are lacerated by their fractures.

7. **The diaphragm has three doors and a nerve from far away, and the arch is tethered at one point.** The inferior vena cava passes at T8, the oesophagus with the vagi at T10 (the weak point through which the stomach herniates), the aorta with the thoracic duct at T12. The muscle is supplied only by the phrenic nerves (C3–C5), so a lesion in neck, mediastinum or high cord paralyses it. The aortic arch is fixed just beyond the left subclavian by the ligamentum arteriosum; in deceleration the mobile heart swings against the fixed descending aorta and the wall tears there, where coarctation also narrows.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Pleural coupling | The negative-pressure fluid film that makes the lung follow the chest wall |
| Tension pneumothorax | One-way leak into the pleural space that shifts the mediastinum and stops venous return |
| Mediastinum | Space between the pleural sacs holding heart, great vessels, trachea, oesophagus and thoracic duct |
| Cardiac tamponade | Pericardial fluid under pressure preventing the heart from filling |
| Coronary dominance | Which artery supplies the posterior septum and atrioventricular node; the right in about 85% |
| Aortic isthmus | Tethered segment just beyond the left subclavian; site of traumatic rupture and coarctation |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A chest drain goes in low, where the fluid is."** The liver and spleen rise to the fifth rib; drains go in the safe triangle on the lateral chest, where only pleura lies behind the wall.
- **"Pulmonary embolism causes lung infarction."** Usually not, because the bronchial arteries keep the tissue alive; the harm is to gas exchange and to the right ventricle.
- **"The heart is on the left."** Two thirds lies left of the midline, but the right ventricle lies behind the sternum; a central stab wound hits the right heart.
