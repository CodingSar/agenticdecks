## Essence

The head, neck and spine hold the central nervous system in a rigid case and route everything that enters or leaves it, with the airway, gullet, great vessels and glands, through a space a few centimetres wide. Three facts explain most of the region's medicine. The skull and spinal canal are closed compartments, so bleeding or swelling inside them is paid for in pressure and herniation. Nerves and vessels pass through bony foramina and tight fascial planes, so they are compressed at predictable points. And the neck is a stack of embryonic pharyngeal arches whose nerves kept their original relations while the structures migrated, which is why a nerve to the voice box loops under the aorta.

## Core tenets

1. **The skull is a rigid box divided by dural partitions, and pressure moves brain across them.** Once venous blood and cerebrospinal fluid have been squeezed out, added volume raises intracranial pressure, cuts cerebral perfusion pressure (mean arterial minus intracranial) and herniates brain from the high-pressure compartment: the medial temporal lobe through the tentorial notch, compressing the oculomotor nerve (a fixed dilated pupil on the side of the mass), or the cerebellar tonsils through the foramen magnum, stopping breathing. The meningeal spaces bleed differently: extradural from the middle meningeal artery under a temporal fracture, lens-shaped, often after a lucid interval; subdural from bridging veins torn by shear, crescentic, in the elderly and anticoagulated; subarachnoid from a circle-of-Willis aneurysm.

2. **The brain has two inflows, and the territory map is the stroke map.** Two internal carotids feed the anterior circulation (anterior cerebral to the medial hemisphere and leg; middle cerebral to the lateral hemisphere, face, arm, language and, via lenticulostriate branches, the internal capsule); two vertebrals join into the basilar for brainstem, cerebellum and vision. The circle of Willis is a complete collateral in fewer than half of people and penetrating arteries are end arteries, so small deep infarcts disconnect large territories. The valveless cavernous sinus, holding the internal carotid and eye-movement nerves, communicates with the face.

3. **Cranial nerves are vulnerable where they are long or pass through bone.** The abducens nerve has the longest intracranial course and is stretched by any rise in pressure, so a sixth-nerve palsy can be a false localising sign. The facial nerve runs through the temporal bone and parotid, so middle-ear disease and parotid tumours damage it; because the upper face has bilateral cortical input, a stroke spares the forehead and a nerve lesion does not. The optic chiasm sits above the pituitary, so its tumours give bitemporal hemianopia.

4. **The pharyngeal arches explain the neck's wiring, including its strangest loop.** Each arch has a nerve, an artery and a cartilage that keep their relations as they descend. The recurrent laryngeal nerves (vagal, sixth arch) hook under the sixth-arch arteries: on the right that artery disappears and the nerve rides under the subclavian, but on the left it persists as the ductus arteriosus, so the left nerve loops under the aortic arch and climbs back in the tracheo-oesophageal groove. It is paralysed, giving a hoarse voice, by aortic aneurysm, an enlarged left atrium, a hilar lung cancer or thyroid surgery. The thyroid descends from the tongue base (a midline thyroglossal cyst rises on tongue protrusion); the inferior parathyroids descend with the thymus and may lie in the mediastinum.

5. **The fascial compartments of the neck direct spread and deny room.** The deep cervical fascia forms investing, pretracheal and prevertebral layers and the carotid sheath. The retropharyngeal space runs into the posterior mediastinum, so a deep tonsillar or dental infection can descend into the chest. After thyroid or carotid surgery a haematoma inside the unyielding layers compresses the airway and the wound must be opened. Lymph from the whole head and neck passes the deep cervical chain, and a hard node there is a tumour until proved otherwise.

6. **The airway and the gullet cross, and the airway's narrow points are its emergencies.** Air and food share the pharynx; the epiglottis and closing larynx keep food out of the trachea, protection the unconscious lose as the tongue also falls back, which a jaw thrust corrects. The adult larynx (C3–C6) is narrowest at the vocal cords, a young child's at the cricoid. The cricothyroid membrane lies subcutaneous in the midline between thyroid and cricoid cartilages, crossed by no major vessel, which makes it the site of emergency surgical access.

7. **The spine protects a cord shorter than its canal, fed by few arteries, hung on a peg.** The adult cord ends at L1–L2, so the lower roots descend as the cauda equina and a lumbar puncture at L3/4 or L4/5 is safe. Cervical roots leave above the vertebra of the same number (C8 below C7), lower roots below, so a posterolateral disc at L4/5 compresses the L5 root crossing to the next level. A single anterior spinal artery supplies the anterior two thirds of the cord, fed by few radicular arteries, the largest (Adamkiewicz) usually left-sided at T9–T12; the mid-thoracic cord is a watershed that infarcts when the aorta is clamped. The atlas pivots on the dens, held by the transverse ligament whose rupture (rheumatoid arthritis, trauma) lets the dens enter the cord. The phrenic nerve arises from C3–C5, so injury above C3 stops the diaphragm and injury at C5–C6 spares it: the level is the difference between a wheelchair and a ventilator.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cerebral perfusion pressure | Mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure |
| Herniation syndromes | Brain displaced across dural partitions or through the foramen magnum under pressure |
| Extradural / subdural / subarachnoid | Meningeal spaces bled into by the middle meningeal artery, bridging veins and aneurysms respectively |
| Anterior / posterior circulation | Internal carotid territory (hemispheres) versus vertebrobasilar (brainstem, cerebellum, occipital cortex) |
| False localising sign | A sign caused by raised pressure, not by a lesion of the nerve's nucleus |
| Pharyngeal arches | Embryonic segments of the neck, each with its own nerve, artery and cartilage |
| Recurrent laryngeal nerve | Vagal branch to the larynx looping under the subclavian (right) or aortic arch (left) |
| Cricothyroid membrane | Midline membrane between thyroid and cricoid cartilages; site of emergency airway access |
| Cauda equina | Lumbar and sacral roots descending below the end of the cord at L1–L2 |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A dilated pupil after head injury means the eye is damaged."** It usually means the oculomotor nerve is compressed by the herniating temporal lobe on the side of a mass.
- **"Hoarseness is a throat problem."** A paralysed cord is a nerve problem, and the left nerve's path through the chest means the cause may be in the aorta, heart or lung.
- **"A lumbar puncture risks the spinal cord."** Below L2 the adult canal holds only roots in fluid; the risk at that level comes from raised pressure above.
