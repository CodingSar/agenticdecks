## Essence

The head, neck and spine hold the central nervous system in a rigid case and route everything that enters or leaves it, together with the airway, the gullet, the great vessels of the brain and the glands of the neck, through a space a few centimetres wide. Three facts explain most of the region's medicine. The skull and spinal canal are closed compartments, so swelling or bleeding inside them is paid for in pressure and herniation. Nerves and vessels pass through bony foramina and tight fascial planes, so they are compressed and cut at predictable points. And the neck is a stack of embryonic pharyngeal arches whose nerves and arteries kept their original relations while the structures migrated, which is why a nerve to the voice box loops under the aorta and a thyroid remnant can sit at the base of the tongue.

## Core tenets

1. **The skull is a rigid box divided by dural partitions, and pressure moves brain across them.** Once the reserve of venous blood and cerebrospinal fluid is squeezed out, any added volume raises intracranial pressure and reduces cerebral perfusion pressure (mean arterial minus intracranial). The dural folds (falx between the hemispheres, tentorium between cerebrum and cerebellum) create compartments, and brain herniates from the high-pressure one: the medial temporal lobe through the tentorial notch, compressing the oculomotor nerve (a fixed dilated pupil on the side of the mass) and the brainstem; the cerebellar tonsils through the foramen magnum, compressing the medulla and stopping breathing. The three meningeal spaces bleed differently. An extradural haematoma comes from the middle meningeal artery under a temporal fracture at the pterion, is held by the sutures into a lens shape and can follow a lucid interval; a subdural haematoma comes from bridging veins torn by shear, spreads as a crescent, and is commonest in the elderly and the anticoagulated whose atrophied brains stretch the veins; subarachnoid blood comes from an aneurysm at a branch point of the circle of Willis and mixes with the cerebrospinal fluid.

2. **The brain has two arterial inflows and the territory map is the stroke map.** Two internal carotids feed the anterior circulation (anterior cerebral artery to the medial hemisphere and the leg area, middle cerebral to the lateral hemisphere, face, arm and language cortex, and through its lenticulostriate branches the internal capsule); two vertebrals join into the basilar and feed the posterior circulation (brainstem, cerebellum, and through the posterior cerebral arteries the occipital cortex and vision). The circle of Willis joins them and is a complete, effective collateral in fewer than half of people. Penetrating arteries are end arteries, so small deep infarcts (lacunes) disconnect large territories. Venous blood drains through valveless dural sinuses; the cavernous sinus, with the internal carotid and the nerves to the eye muscles inside it, communicates with the veins of the face and orbit, so facial infection can thrombose it.

3. **Cranial nerves are vulnerable where they are long or pass through bone.** The abducens nerve has the longest intracranial course and is stretched by any rise in intracranial pressure, so a sixth-nerve palsy can be a false localising sign. The facial nerve runs through a bony canal in the temporal bone and the parotid gland, so it is damaged by middle-ear disease, skull-base fracture and parotid tumours; because the upper face receives cortical input from both hemispheres, a stroke spares the forehead and a nerve lesion does not. The optic chiasm lies just above the pituitary, so a pituitary tumour cuts the crossing nasal fibres and produces bitemporal hemianopia. The brainstem packs the cranial nerve nuclei with the long motor and sensory tracts, so a brainstem lesion gives cranial nerve signs on one side and limb signs on the other, which localises it to within a centimetre.

4. **The pharyngeal arches explain the neck's wiring, including its strangest loop.** Each arch has a nerve, an artery, a cartilage and muscles, and they keep their relations as they descend. The recurrent laryngeal nerves (branches of the vagus, from the sixth arch) hook under the sixth-arch arteries: on the right that artery disappears and the nerve rides up under the subclavian artery, but on the left it persists as the ductus arteriosus, so the left nerve loops under the aortic arch, far down in the chest, and climbs back up in the groove between trachea and oesophagus. It can therefore be paralysed, giving a hoarse voice, by an aortic aneurysm, an enlarged left atrium, a lung cancer at the hilum or a mediastinal node, as well as by thyroid surgery; bilateral injury closes the airway. The thyroid starts at the base of the tongue and descends, leaving the thyroglossal duct, so a midline neck cyst that moves on tongue protrusion is thyroid tissue; the inferior parathyroids descend with the thymus from the third pouch and can be found in the mediastinum.

5. **The fascial compartments of the neck direct spread and deny room.** The deep cervical fascia forms an investing layer, a pretracheal (visceral) layer around the thyroid, trachea and oesophagus, a prevertebral layer and the carotid sheath. The retropharyngeal space runs behind the pharynx down into the posterior mediastinum, so a deep infection from the tonsil or a tooth can descend into the chest; infection under the floor of the mouth pushes the tongue up and back. After thyroid or carotid surgery a haematoma inside the unyielding pretracheal and investing layers compresses the airway, and the stitch that closes the wound must be cut to open the compartment. The carotid sheath binds artery, internal jugular vein and vagus together, so a stab that reaches one usually reaches the others.

6. **The airway and the gullet cross, and the airway's narrow points are its emergencies.** Air and food share the pharynx and cross at the laryngopharynx, where the epiglottis and the closing larynx keep food out of the trachea; an unconscious person loses that protection and also lets the tongue fall back against the posterior pharyngeal wall, which is why a jaw thrust opens the airway. The larynx sits at C3–C6 in the adult, the narrowest point of the adult airway is the vocal cords, and in a young child it is the cricoid ring. The cricothyroid membrane lies in the midline just under the skin between the thyroid and cricoid cartilages, crossed by no major vessel, which makes it the site of emergency surgical access when the airway cannot be secured from above; the thyroid isthmus lies lower, over the second to fourth tracheal rings, and a tracheostomy goes through or below it. Lymph from the whole head and neck drains through the deep cervical chain along the internal jugular vein, and a hard node there is a tumour until proved otherwise.

7. **The spine protects a cord that is shorter than its canal.** Thirty-three vertebrae (7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 fused sacral, 4 coccygeal) stack around a canal that is widest in the neck and narrowest in the thorax. The cord ends at L1–L2 in adults, so the lumbar and sacral roots descend as the cauda equina and a lumbar puncture at L3/4 or L4/5 is safe. Cervical roots leave above the vertebra of the same number (C8 below C7) and thoracic and lumbar roots below, so a posterolateral disc herniation at L4/5 compresses the L5 root crossing to the next level, while a far-lateral one compresses L4 leaving at that level. The cord's blood supply is a single anterior spinal artery for the anterior two thirds (motor and pain pathways) and two posterior arteries, fed by few radicular arteries, the largest (the artery of Adamkiewicz) usually from the left at T9–T12; the mid-thoracic cord is a watershed and infarcts when the aorta is clamped or dissects, leaving paraplegia with spared position sense.

8. **The upper cervical spine holds the head on a peg, and the level of injury sets whether the patient breathes.** The skull rocks on the atlas and the atlas pivots on the dens of the axis, held by the transverse ligament; rupture of that ligament in rheumatoid arthritis, Down syndrome or trauma lets the dens move into the cord. The phrenic nerve arises from C3–C5, so a cord injury above C3 stops the diaphragm and the patient cannot breathe, one at C5–C6 leaves the diaphragm but takes the arms and intercostals, and the fine distinctions of level in the neck are the difference between a wheelchair and a ventilator. Spinal cord compression, from any cause, is an emergency because the cord's capillaries fail under pressure exactly as those of a limb compartment do.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cerebral perfusion pressure | Mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure; the gradient that drives flow to the brain |
| Herniation syndromes | Displacement of brain across dural partitions or through the foramen magnum under pressure |
| Extradural / subdural / subarachnoid | The three meningeal spaces, bled into by the middle meningeal artery, bridging veins and circle-of-Willis aneurysms respectively |
| Anterior / posterior circulation | Internal carotid territory (cerebral hemispheres) versus vertebrobasilar territory (brainstem, cerebellum, occipital cortex) |
| Circle of Willis | Arterial ring joining the two circulations; a complete collateral in a minority |
| False localising sign | A sign (classically a sixth-nerve palsy) caused by raised pressure rather than a lesion of the nerve's nucleus |
| Pharyngeal arches | Embryonic segments of the neck, each with its own nerve, artery and cartilage |
| Recurrent laryngeal nerve | Vagal branch to the larynx that loops under the subclavian (right) or aortic arch (left) |
| Cricothyroid membrane | Midline membrane between thyroid and cricoid cartilages; the site of emergency airway access |
| Cauda equina | Lumbar and sacral roots descending below the end of the cord at L1–L2 |
| Artery of Adamkiewicz | Dominant radicular artery to the lower cord, usually left-sided, T9–T12 |

- **Above:** neurology's [stroke](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/stroke.md) is this arterial map read from the deficit; anaesthesia's [airway management](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/airway-and-ventilation-management.md) and surgery of the [nose, throat and neck](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/05-ophthalmology-and-ent/nose-throat-and-head-and-neck.md) work in the planes described here; [thyroid disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/05-endocrinology/thyroid-disease.md) and its surgery turn on the recurrent laryngeal nerve and the parathyroids; [magnetic resonance imaging](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/03-imaging-and-physiological-measurement/magnetic-resonance-imaging.md) is the way this anatomy is now seen in the living.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A dilated pupil after head injury means the eye is damaged."** It usually means the oculomotor nerve is being compressed by the medial temporal lobe herniating on the side of a mass; it is a sign of the brain, not the eye.
- **"Hoarseness is a throat problem."** A paralysed vocal cord is a nerve problem, and the left nerve's path through the chest means the cause may be in the aorta, the heart or the lung.
- **"A lumbar puncture risks the spinal cord."** Below L2 in an adult the canal holds only roots in fluid; the risk at that level is from raised intracranial pressure above, not from the needle hitting cord.
