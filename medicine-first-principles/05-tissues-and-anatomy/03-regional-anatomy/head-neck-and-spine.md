## Essence

The head, neck and spine hold the central nervous system in a rigid case and route everything that enters or leaves it, with the airway, the gullet, the brain's great vessels and the glands of the neck, through a space a few centimetres wide. Three facts explain most of the region's medicine. The skull and spinal canal are closed compartments, so swelling or bleeding inside them is paid for in pressure and herniation. Nerves and vessels pass through bony foramina and tight fascial planes, so they are compressed at predictable points. And the neck is a stack of embryonic pharyngeal arches whose nerves kept their original relations while the structures migrated, which is why a nerve to the voice box loops under the aorta.

## Core tenets

1. **The skull is a rigid box divided by dural partitions, and pressure moves brain across them.** Once venous blood and cerebrospinal fluid have been squeezed out, any added volume raises intracranial pressure and cuts cerebral perfusion pressure (mean arterial minus intracranial). The falx and tentorium create compartments, and brain herniates from the high-pressure one: the medial temporal lobe through the tentorial notch, compressing the oculomotor nerve (a fixed dilated pupil on the side of the mass) and brainstem; the cerebellar tonsils through the foramen magnum, stopping breathing. The three meningeal spaces bleed differently: an extradural haematoma from the middle meningeal artery under a temporal fracture, lens-shaped and bounded by sutures, often after a lucid interval; a subdural from bridging veins torn by shear, crescentic, commonest in the elderly and anticoagulated; subarachnoid blood from a circle-of-Willis aneurysm mixing with the cerebrospinal fluid.

2. **The brain has two inflows, and the territory map is the stroke map.** Two internal carotids feed the anterior circulation (anterior cerebral to the medial hemisphere and leg area; middle cerebral to the lateral hemisphere, face, arm and language, and through its lenticulostriate branches the internal capsule); two vertebrals join into the basilar for brainstem, cerebellum and, via the posterior cerebrals, vision. The circle of Willis joins them but is a complete collateral in fewer than half of people, and penetrating arteries are end arteries, so small deep infarcts disconnect large territories. Venous blood drains through valveless dural sinuses; the cavernous sinus, holding the internal carotid and the eye-movement nerves, communicates with the face, so facial infection can thrombose it.

3. **Cranial nerves are vulnerable where they are long or pass through bone.** The abducens nerve has the longest intracranial course and is stretched by any rise in pressure, so a sixth-nerve palsy can be a false localising sign. The facial nerve runs through the temporal bone and parotid, so middle-ear disease, skull-base fracture and parotid tumours damage it; because the upper face has bilateral cortical input, a stroke spares the forehead and a nerve lesion does not. The optic chiasm sits above the pituitary, so a pituitary tumour cuts the crossing fibres and gives bitemporal hemianopia. The brainstem packs cranial nerve nuclei beside the long tracts, so its lesions give cranial nerve signs on one side and limb signs on the other.

4. **The pharyngeal arches explain the neck's wiring, including its strangest loop.** Each arch has a nerve, an artery and a cartilage that keep their relations as they descend. The recurrent laryngeal nerves (vagal, sixth arch) hook under the sixth-arch arteries: on the right that artery disappears and the nerve rides under the subclavian, but on the left it persists as the ductus arteriosus, so the left nerve loops under the aortic arch and climbs back in the tracheo-oesophageal groove. It is therefore paralysed, giving a hoarse voice, by aortic aneurysm, an enlarged left atrium, a hilar lung cancer or thyroid surgery; bilateral injury closes the airway. The thyroid descends from the tongue base, leaving the thyroglossal duct (a midline cyst that rises on tongue protrusion); the inferior parathyroids descend with the thymus and may lie in the mediastinum.

5. **The fascial compartments of the neck direct spread and deny room.** The deep cervical fascia forms an investing layer, a pretracheal layer around thyroid, trachea and oesophagus, a prevertebral layer and the carotid sheath binding artery, internal jugular vein and vagus. The retropharyngeal space runs into the posterior mediastinum, so a deep tonsillar or dental infection can descend into the chest. After thyroid or carotid surgery a haematoma inside the unyielding layers compresses the airway, and the wound must be opened. Lymph from the whole head and neck passes the deep cervical chain along the internal jugular, and a hard node there is a tumour until proved otherwise.

6. **The airway and the gullet cross, and the airway's narrow points are its emergencies.** Air and food share the pharynx; the epiglottis and closing larynx keep food out of the trachea, protection the unconscious lose as the tongue also falls back against the pharynx, which a jaw thrust corrects. The adult larynx lies at C3–C6 and is narrowest at the vocal cords; a young child's airway is narrowest at the cricoid. The cricothyroid membrane lies subcutaneous in the midline between thyroid and cricoid cartilages, crossed by no major vessel, which makes it the site of emergency surgical access; the thyroid isthmus covers tracheal rings two to four, and a tracheostomy goes through or below it.

7. **The spine protects a cord shorter than its canal, fed by few arteries.** Thirty-three vertebrae (7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 fused sacral, 4 coccygeal) surround a canal widest in the neck. The adult cord ends at L1–L2, so the lower roots descend as the cauda equina and a lumbar puncture at L3/4 or L4/5 is safe. Cervical roots leave above the vertebra of the same number (C8 below C7), thoracic and lumbar roots below, so a posterolateral disc at L4/5 compresses the L5 root crossing to the next level, a far-lateral one the exiting L4. A single anterior spinal artery supplies the anterior two thirds of the cord (motor and pain pathways), fed by few radicular arteries, the largest (Adamkiewicz) usually left-sided at T9–T12; the mid-thoracic cord is a watershed and infarcts when the aorta is clamped or dissects, leaving paraplegia with spared position sense.

8. **The upper cervical spine holds the head on a peg, and the level of injury sets whether the patient breathes.** The skull rocks on the atlas and the atlas pivots on the dens, held by the transverse ligament; its rupture in rheumatoid arthritis, Down syndrome or trauma lets the dens move into the cord. The phrenic nerve arises from C3–C5, so a cord injury above C3 stops the diaphragm, one at C5–C6 spares it but takes arms and intercostals; fine distinctions of level are the difference between a wheelchair and a ventilator. Cord compression from any cause is an emergency because its capillaries fail under pressure exactly as a limb compartment's do.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cerebral perfusion pressure | Mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure |
| Herniation syndromes | Displacement of brain across dural partitions or through the foramen magnum under pressure |
| Extradural / subdural / subarachnoid | The three meningeal spaces, bled into by the middle meningeal artery, bridging veins and aneurysms respectively |
| Anterior / posterior circulation | Internal carotid territory (hemispheres) versus vertebrobasilar territory (brainstem, cerebellum, occipital cortex) |
| False localising sign | A sign (classically a sixth-nerve palsy) caused by raised pressure rather than a lesion of the nerve's nucleus |
| Pharyngeal arches | Embryonic segments of the neck, each with its own nerve, artery and cartilage |
| Recurrent laryngeal nerve | Vagal branch to the larynx looping under the subclavian (right) or aortic arch (left) |
| Cricothyroid membrane | Midline membrane between thyroid and cricoid cartilages; site of emergency airway access |
| Cauda equina | Lumbar and sacral roots descending below the end of the cord at L1–L2 |
| Artery of Adamkiewicz | Dominant radicular artery to the lower cord, usually left-sided, T9–T12 |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A dilated pupil after head injury means the eye is damaged."** It usually means the oculomotor nerve is compressed by the herniating temporal lobe on the side of a mass; it is a sign of the brain.
- **"Hoarseness is a throat problem."** A paralysed cord is a nerve problem, and the left nerve's path through the chest means the cause may be in the aorta, heart or lung.
- **"A lumbar puncture risks the spinal cord."** Below L2 the adult canal holds only roots in fluid; the risk at that level comes from raised intracranial pressure above.
