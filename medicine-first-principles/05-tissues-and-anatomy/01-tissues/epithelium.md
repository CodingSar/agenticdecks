## Essence

An epithelium is a sheet of cells, sealed to one another and standing on a basement membrane, that separates two compartments and controls what passes between them. Every surface facing the outside world or a lumen (skin, gut, airway, urinary tract, glands, vessels) is epithelial. The idea that explains the tissue is **polarity**: each cell has an apical face toward the lumen and a basolateral face toward the body, with different transporters on each, so a sheet can move salt, water, nutrients or secretions in one direction while keeping the two sides apart. Because epithelia divide constantly and meet every insult first, they are also where most cancers arise.

## Core tenets

1. **An epithelium is a sealed sheet on a basement membrane, with no vessels of its own.** Cells are packed with almost no matrix between them and rest on a thin layer of type IV collagen and laminin. Every cell is fed by diffusion from capillaries beneath that membrane, which caps epithelial thickness at a few hundred micrometres and means scarring of the underlying tissue starves the surface.

2. **Junctions set what the sheet lets through.** Tight junctions near the apical surface are the gate for the paracellular route; their claudin composition makes some epithelia leaky (the proximal renal tubule, with a transepithelial resistance of tens of ohm-square-centimetres) and others nearly impermeable (the bladder, thousands). Desmosomes bind cells against shear, hemidesmosomes bind them to the basement membrane. Autoantibodies against desmosomal cadherins split the epidermis into the blisters of pemphigus; loss of E-cadherin is one of the first steps by which a carcinoma cell leaves its sheet.

3. **Polarity turns a sheet into a pump.** The tight junction is also a fence that keeps apical and basolateral proteins apart. The sodium–potassium pump sits basolaterally in nearly every transporting epithelium; apical channels and cotransporters admit sodium, glucose or chloride down the gradient it makes, the solute leaves on the far side and water follows osmotically. With different apical proteins, this one arrangement is intestinal absorption, renal reabsorption, sweat, tears and gastric acid. Cholera toxin locks open apical chloride channels in the small intestine, so machinery that absorbs several litres a day secretes them instead.

4. **Shape and layering match the job, and change when the job changes.** Simple squamous epithelium is thin enough for diffusion (alveoli, endothelium); cuboidal and columnar cells have room for the mitochondria and membrane that transport and secretion need (tubules, gut, glands); stratified epithelium survives abrasion by shedding, keratinised where it must also resist drying; glands are the same sheet grown inward, keeping a duct (exocrine) or losing it and secreting into capillaries (endocrine). When the stress on a surface changes for long enough the stem cells switch programme: a smoker's bronchial epithelium becomes squamous, an acid-bathed lower oesophagus becomes columnar (Barrett oesophagus). This metaplasia is adaptive, loses the original function, and is a known step toward cancer.

5. **Epithelia renew throughout life from a basal stem compartment.** The gut lining is replaced every 3–5 days from crypt stem cells, the epidermis about every four weeks. Constant replication is why epithelia heal well and also why most mutations accumulate here: carcinomas are the large majority of human cancers. It is also why cytotoxic chemotherapy causes mouth ulcers, diarrhoea and hair loss first.

6. **The basement membrane is the line that defines invasion.** A carcinoma whose cells have not crossed it (carcinoma in situ) cannot reach lymphatics or vessels and cannot metastasise; once it crosses, staging and prognosis change. The same membrane, specialised, is the filter of the glomerulus, and antibodies against its collagen attack lung and kidney together because both share the antigen.

7. **Vessel and cavity linings are epithelia by form though they come from mesoderm.** Endothelium, one cell thick, is the body's largest epithelium and the one through which all exchange with blood occurs; its tight junctions in the brain make the blood–brain barrier, and its fenestrations in glomerulus and endocrine glands allow bulk filtration.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Basement membrane | Type IV collagen and laminin sheet under every epithelium; the boundary crossed by invasive cancer |
| Apical / basolateral | Lumen-facing and body-facing membrane domains with different proteins |
| Tight junction | Claudin seal setting paracellular permeability and separating membrane domains |
| Desmosome | Cadherin spot weld resisting shear; target of pemphigus antibodies |
| Transcellular / paracellular | Transport through cells versus between them |
| Simple / stratified | One layer versus several; squamous, cuboidal, columnar describe cell shape |
| Metaplasia | Reversible replacement of one mature epithelium by another under chronic stress |
| Carcinoma | Malignant epithelial tumour; adenocarcinoma from glands, squamous cell carcinoma from squamous epithelium |
| Endothelium | The simple squamous epithelium lining all blood and lymph vessels |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Epithelium is just a covering."** The kidney reabsorbs about 180 L of filtrate a day and the gut absorbs about 9 L, all through polarised epithelial cells.
- **"Cancer spreads once a tumour is large."** Size matters less than whether the basement membrane has been crossed; a small invasive carcinoma can metastasise, a large in-situ one cannot.
- **"Metaplasia is cancer."** It is an orderly, reversible change of cell type; it matters because the new tissue is at higher risk of the disordered growth (dysplasia) that precedes cancer.
