## Essence

An epithelium is a sheet of cells, sealed to one another and standing on a basement membrane, that separates two compartments and controls everything that passes between them. Every surface of the body that faces the outside world or a lumen (skin, gut, airway, urinary tract, glands, blood vessels) is epithelial. The one idea that explains the tissue is **polarity**: each cell has an apical face toward the lumen and a basolateral face toward the body, with different transporters on each, so a sheet can move salt, water, nutrients or secretions in one chosen direction while keeping the two sides apart. Because epithelia divide constantly and meet every chemical and microbial insult first, they are also the tissue from which most cancers arise.

## Core tenets

1. **An epithelium is a sealed sheet on a basement membrane, and it has no blood vessels of its own.** Cells are packed with almost no matrix between them and rest on a thin layer of type IV collagen and laminin that they secrete together with the connective tissue beneath. Oxygen and nutrients reach every epithelial cell by diffusion from the capillaries under that membrane, which limits how thick an epithelium can be (a stratified epithelium is at most a few hundred micrometres) and means that inflammation or scarring of the underlying tissue starves the surface above it.

2. **Junctions set what the sheet lets through.** Tight junctions near the apical surface close the gap between cells and are the gate for the paracellular route; their protein composition (claudins) makes some epithelia leaky (the proximal renal tubule, with a transepithelial resistance of tens of ohm-square-centimetres) and others nearly impermeable (the urinary bladder, thousands). Adherens junctions and desmosomes bind cells to each other against shear, and hemidesmosomes bind them to the basement membrane. Autoantibodies against desmosomal cadherins split the epidermis and produce the blisters of pemphigus; loss of E-cadherin is one of the first steps by which a carcinoma cell leaves its sheet.

3. **Polarity turns a sheet into a pump.** The tight junction is also a fence that keeps apical and basolateral membrane proteins apart. The sodium–potassium pump sits basolaterally in nearly every transporting epithelium; apical channels and cotransporters admit sodium, glucose or chloride down the gradient the pump makes, and the solute leaves on the other side. Water follows osmotically. This single arrangement, with different apical proteins, is intestinal absorption, renal reabsorption, sweat, tears, gastric acid and airway surface liquid. Cholera toxin does its damage by locking open apical chloride channels in the small intestine, so the same machinery that absorbs several litres a day secretes them instead.

4. **Shape and layering are matched to the job, and they change when the job changes.** Simple squamous epithelium is thin enough for diffusion (alveoli, capillary endothelium, mesothelium). Cuboidal and columnar cells have room for the mitochondria and membrane needed for transport and secretion (tubules, gut, glands). Stratified epithelium survives abrasion by shedding its top layer, keratinised where it must also resist drying (skin). Ciliated pseudostratified epithelium with goblet cells sweeps mucus out of the airway. When the stress on a surface changes for long enough, the stem cells switch programme: the bronchial epithelium of a smoker becomes squamous, the lower oesophagus of someone with chronic acid reflux becomes columnar (Barrett oesophagus). This metaplasia is adaptive, but the new tissue has lost the original function and is a known step toward cancer.

5. **Glands are epithelium that has grown inward.** An exocrine gland keeps its duct and delivers its product to a surface (sweat, saliva, pancreatic enzymes, milk); an endocrine gland loses its connection and releases hormones into the capillaries that grow around it (thyroid, adrenal cortex, pancreatic islets). The mode of secretion, merocrine (exocytosis), apocrine (shedding of apical cytoplasm) or holocrine (whole-cell disintegration, as in sebaceous glands), is visible in the cell's structure.

6. **Epithelia renew throughout life from a basal stem compartment.** The intestinal lining is replaced every 3–5 days from crypt stem cells, the epidermis roughly every four weeks, the airway more slowly. Constant replication is the reason epithelia heal well, and also why they are where most mutations accumulate: carcinomas (epithelial cancers) make up the large majority of human cancers. It is also why cytotoxic chemotherapy, which kills dividing cells, produces mouth ulcers, diarrhoea and hair loss before it damages anything else.

7. **The basement membrane is the line that defines invasion.** A carcinoma whose cells have not crossed the basement membrane (carcinoma in situ) cannot reach lymphatics or blood vessels and cannot metastasise; the moment it does cross, staging and prognosis change. The same membrane, thickened and specialised, is the filter of the renal glomerulus, and autoantibodies against its collagen (anti-glomerular basement membrane disease) attack lung and kidney together because both share the antigen.

8. **The linings of vessels and body cavities are epithelia by form, even though they come from mesoderm.** Endothelium, one cell thick, is the largest epithelium in the body and the one through which all exchange with blood happens; its tight junctions in the brain make the blood–brain barrier, and its fenestrations in the glomerulus and endocrine glands allow bulk filtration. Mesothelium lines the pleura, pericardium and peritoneum. When these cells become tumours they are named differently (angiosarcoma, mesothelioma), a reminder that histological classification follows embryology as much as shape.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Basement membrane | Thin sheet of type IV collagen and laminin under every epithelium; the boundary crossed by invasive cancer |
| Apical / basolateral | The lumen-facing and body-facing membrane domains, with different proteins |
| Tight junction | Claudin-based seal between cells that sets paracellular permeability and keeps membrane domains apart |
| Desmosome | Cadherin-based spot weld that resists shear; target of pemphigus antibodies |
| Transcellular / paracellular | Transport through the cells versus between them |
| Simple / stratified | One cell layer versus several; squamous, cuboidal, columnar describe cell shape |
| Metaplasia | Reversible replacement of one mature epithelium by another in response to chronic stress |
| Exocrine / endocrine | Gland that secretes through a duct to a surface versus into blood |
| Carcinoma | Malignant tumour of epithelium; adenocarcinoma from glandular epithelium, squamous cell carcinoma from squamous |
| Endothelium | The simple squamous epithelium lining all blood and lymph vessels |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Epithelium is just a covering."** It is the body's main transport tissue; the kidney reabsorbs about 180 L of filtrate a day and the gut absorbs about 9 L, all through polarised epithelial cells.
- **"Cancer spreads once a tumour is large."** Size matters less than whether the basement membrane has been crossed; a small invasive carcinoma can metastasise, a large in-situ one cannot.
- **"Metaplasia is cancer."** It is an orderly change of cell type and is reversible if the stimulus stops; it matters because the new tissue is at higher risk of the disordered growth (dysplasia) that precedes cancer.
