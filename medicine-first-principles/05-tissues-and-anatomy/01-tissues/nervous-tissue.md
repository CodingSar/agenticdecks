## Essence

Nervous tissue is built to carry information fast and far, and every one of its structural features is a consequence of that demand. A neuron is a cell stretched into a receiving tree, an integrating body and a transmitting cable that may be a metre long; glial cells insulate, feed and police it; and the whole tissue is sealed behind a barrier that keeps its ionic environment constant. The governing constraint is that a neuron is an obligate aerobe with no fuel store, no capacity to divide, and a geometry so extreme that it depends on active transport along its axon for everything it needs. That is why nervous tissue is the fastest tissue in the body to die, the slowest to recover, and the one whose diseases are explained by anatomy more than any other.

## Core tenets

1. **A neuron is polarised in shape and in information flow.** Dendrites receive synapses and sum them; the cell body integrates and holds the nucleus and almost all protein synthesis; the axon arises at a hillock where the decision to fire is made, conducts all-or-none action potentials, and ends in terminals that release transmitter. Signals travel one way. The shape of the dendritic tree determines what the cell computes, and the diameter and insulation of the axon determine how fast it reports the result.

2. **The axon is a supply line, and length is a liability.** A motor neuron to the foot has a cell body ~50 micrometres across and an axon about a metre long; its terminals are kept alive only by motor proteins hauling vesicles, mitochondria and enzymes along microtubules at a few hundred millimetres a day (fast transport) or about a millimetre a day (slow transport). Anything that impairs the cell's metabolism or its transport (diabetes, alcohol, vitamin B12 deficiency, many drugs) fails the longest axons first, which is why peripheral neuropathy begins in the feet and spreads in a stocking, then glove, distribution.

3. **Glia are half the tissue and do the housekeeping.** Human brain contains roughly 86 billion neurons and a similar number of glia. Astrocytes buffer extracellular potassium, take up glutamate, store glycogen, wrap capillaries and form the scar after injury. Oligodendrocytes make central myelin, each cell wrapping segments of many axons; Schwann cells make peripheral myelin, one cell per segment. Microglia are the resident macrophages, of mesodermal origin, and ependymal cells line the ventricles. Most primary brain tumours are gliomas, because glia keep dividing and neurons do not.

4. **Myelin buys speed and economy at the cost of a new vulnerability.** Wrapping an axon in layers of lipid membrane raises its resistance and lowers its capacitance, so the action potential jumps between the bare nodes of Ranvier instead of regenerating along every micrometre. Conduction speed rises from about 1 m/s in a thin unmyelinated fibre to around 100 m/s in a large myelinated one, roughly 6 m/s per micrometre of fibre diameter, with far less ion pumping per metre. When the myelin is stripped (multiple sclerosis centrally, Guillain–Barré syndrome peripherally) the axon survives but conducts slowly or not at all, and conduction block worsens with warmth; function can return if the sheath is remade.

5. **Grey matter computes, white matter connects, and the arrangement predicts the lesion.** Cell bodies, dendrites and synapses cluster into grey matter (cortex, nuclei, the central butterfly of the spinal cord); myelinated axons bundle into white matter tracts. The cerebrum has grey outside and white inside; the spinal cord is the reverse. A small lesion in white matter can disconnect large regions (a lacunar stroke in the internal capsule paralyses half the body), while a lesion of the same size in cortex produces a focal deficit.

6. **Peripheral nerves regrow; central axons do not.** A cut peripheral axon dies back distally (Wallerian degeneration) but the Schwann cells and their basement-membrane tubes persist and guide sprouts from the stump at about 1 mm a day, so a nerve injury at the wrist can recover hand function over months. In the brain and spinal cord, the oligodendrocyte myelin debris is inhibitory, astrocytes wall off the injury, no tubes guide regrowth, and the neurons themselves are post-mitotic. Spinal cord injury and stroke are therefore permanent at the level of the lost axons, and recovery is reorganisation of what remains.

7. **Nervous tissue has no reserve of oxygen or fuel.** The brain is about 2% of body mass and uses about 20% of resting oxygen and glucose, nearly all of it to pump ions back across membranes. Consciousness is lost within about ten seconds of stopped cerebral blood flow and neurons begin to die within minutes; the most active cells (hippocampal CA1 pyramidal cells, cerebellar Purkinje cells, cortical layers 3 and 5) go first. This single fact drives the time limits of resuscitation and stroke treatment and the pattern of damage after cardiac arrest.

8. **The tissue is sealed, and its support structures organise its diseases.** Brain capillaries have continuous tight junctions, backed by astrocyte end-feet and pericytes, forming the blood–brain barrier that keeps ions and transmitters constant and excludes most water-soluble drugs and antibodies; it also prevents the white cells and antibodies of ordinary inflammation from entering, which is why the Central Nervous System (CNS) is immunologically privileged and why meningitis and encephalitis are so dangerous once the barrier is breached. Peripheral nerves are wrapped in their own barriers: endoneurium around fibres, perineurium forming a tight sleeve around each fascicle, epineurium around the nerve. Fibres are classed by diameter, and diameter decides which fibres a pressure lesion (large first) or a local anaesthetic (small, unmyelinated pain fibres among the first) silences.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Dendrite / axon | Receiving processes that sum inputs; the single output cable that conducts action potentials |
| Axon hillock | Where the axon leaves the cell body and the decision to fire is made |
| Axonal transport | Motor-protein movement of cargo along microtubules in both directions |
| Myelin | Multilayered glial membrane wrapping an axon; produced by oligodendrocytes (CNS) and Schwann cells (PNS) |
| Node of Ranvier | Gap in the myelin where the action potential is regenerated; the basis of saltatory conduction |
| Astrocyte | Glial cell that buffers ions and transmitters, feeds neurons and forms the glial scar |
| Microglia | Resident macrophages of the CNS |
| Grey / white matter | Regions of cell bodies and synapses versus regions of myelinated tracts |
| Wallerian degeneration | Breakdown of the distal segment of a cut axon |
| Blood–brain barrier | Tight-junctioned endothelium with astrocyte support that controls what enters the CNS |
| Peripheral Nervous System (PNS) | Nerves and ganglia outside brain and spinal cord; the only part that regenerates |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Nerves carry electricity like wires."** An axon is a poor conductor; it carries a self-regenerating ionic wave, which is why conduction is slow (metres per second, not light speed) and why myelin, which makes the axon more wire-like between nodes, speeds it up so much.
- **"Glia are packing material."** They set the extracellular environment, make myelin, defend the tissue and form its scars; most brain tumours and the commonest neurological disease of young adults (multiple sclerosis) are diseases of glia.
- **"Brain cells cannot be replaced, so the brain cannot recover."** Neurons are not replaced, but surviving circuits reorganise; rehabilitation after stroke works on that plasticity, not on regrowth.
