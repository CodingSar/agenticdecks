## Essence

Muscle is the tissue that turns the chemical energy of Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) into force by letting myosin heads pull on actin filaments. The body builds this one motor into three tissues, skeletal, cardiac and smooth, and the differences between them are not decoration: how the filaments are arranged, how calcium is delivered, and whether the cells are electrically coupled decide whether a muscle is fast or slow, graded or all-or-none, voluntary or automatic, tireless or quick to fatigue, and whether it can heal. Muscle is also the body's largest organ by mass (about 40% of body weight), its main heat source and its biggest reservoir of protein, so what happens to it matters far beyond movement.

## Core tenets

1. **One motor, three packagings.** In every muscle cell, myosin heads bind actin, flex, release on binding ATP, and re-cock, and the cycle is switched on by a rise in cytoplasmic calcium. Skeletal and cardiac muscle line the filaments up in repeating sarcomeres, which gives their striated appearance and a sharp relationship between length and force. Smooth muscle anchors oblique bundles of filaments to dense bodies, which costs speed but lets the cell shorten to a fraction of its length and hold tension for hours at almost no energy cost.

2. **Skeletal muscle is a syncytium built for speed and voluntary gradation.** Each fibre is a single giant cell formed by fusion of hundreds of myoblasts, up to several centimetres long, with nuclei at the periphery. Each is driven by exactly one motor neuron at one neuromuscular junction, and no fibre is electrically coupled to its neighbours, so force is graded by how many motor units the nervous system recruits and how fast it fires them. Transverse tubules carry the action potential into the fibre and open sarcoplasmic-reticulum calcium stores within milliseconds, so the whole fibre contracts at once. The reserve of calcium is internal; skeletal muscle keeps contracting in a calcium-free bath.

3. **Fibre types are a trade between endurance and power.** Type I fibres are packed with mitochondria, myoglobin and capillaries, burn fat and glucose aerobically, and resist fatigue; postural muscles are mostly type I. Type II fibres have more myosin ATPase, larger glycogen stores and fewer mitochondria, so they produce more force faster but exhaust their fuel in seconds to minutes. The mix in a given muscle is largely set by its nerve, and the fibre type of a motor unit can be changed by re-innervation or by training, which is why endurance training shifts metabolism without changing the number of fibres.

4. **Cardiac muscle is coupled so that the heart behaves as one cell.** Cardiomyocytes are short, branched and joined end to end by intercalated discs that combine desmosomes (mechanical strength) with gap junctions (electrical continuity), so a depolarisation that starts anywhere spreads to the whole chamber. Its action potential lasts hundreds of milliseconds, giving a refractory period longer than the contraction, so the heart cannot be tetanised and must relax to fill. Unlike skeletal muscle, its contraction depends on calcium entering from outside during the plateau, which then triggers release from the stores; this is the handle through which calcium-channel blockers and extracellular calcium change contractility. Cardiomyocytes barely divide after birth, so a dead region of heart becomes fibrous scar that neither contracts nor conducts normally.

5. **Smooth muscle is slow, economical and responds to almost everything.** It has no sarcomeres, no transverse tubules and no neuromuscular junctions; nerves release transmitter from swellings along their length, and the cell also responds to stretch, hormones, local metabolites and temperature. Contraction is switched on by phosphorylation of myosin by a calcium-calmodulin-dependent kinase, which is slow but lets the muscle maintain tension with a fraction of the ATP a striated muscle would use (the latch state). Single-unit smooth muscle (gut, ureter, uterus, small vessels) is coupled by gap junctions and contracts in waves driven by pacemaker cells; multi-unit smooth muscle (iris, airway, large arteries, piloerector) is controlled fibre by fibre. Smooth muscle also divides, so the uterus grows by hyperplasia in pregnancy and arterial walls thicken under hypertension.

6. **Length sets force.** Because force depends on how many myosin heads overlap actin, each sarcomere has an optimum length of about 2.0–2.2 micrometres, with less force when stretched past overlap or compressed until filaments collide. Skeletal muscles are attached so that they work near the optimum across the joint's range; the heart works on the rising limb, so that filling it more makes it contract harder, which is the cellular basis of the Frank–Starling relationship that physiology builds on.

7. **Muscle mass is plastic, and the direction depends on load and nerve.** Mechanical load and amino acids drive protein synthesis; disuse, denervation, starvation, cortisol and inflammation drive breakdown. A limb in a cast or a patient in bed loses strength within days and a substantial share of muscle mass within weeks; a denervated muscle wastes and fibrillates. Skeletal muscle regenerates from satellite cells on the fibre surface, so a torn muscle heals, but a muscle whose fibres are replaced by fat and fibrous tissue (muscular dystrophy, long-standing denervation) does not. In Duchenne muscular dystrophy the missing protein, dystrophin, is the link between the contractile apparatus and the membrane; each contraction tears the membrane, and the muscle dies by a thousand small injuries.

8. **Muscle is dangerous when it breaks down.** Fibres contain the body's largest pools of potassium, creatine kinase, myoglobin and phosphate. Crush injury, prolonged immobility, extreme exertion, some drugs and some infections rupture enough fibres to release them (rhabdomyolysis): potassium stops the heart, myoglobin blocks the renal tubules, and the swollen muscle inside its fascial compartment cuts off its own blood supply. Muscle is also the main site of insulin-stimulated glucose uptake and of shivering heat production, so losing it worsens glucose control and cold tolerance.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sarcomere | Repeating unit of actin and myosin between two Z-discs; the source of striation and of the length–force relation |
| Cross-bridge cycle | Myosin binds actin, pulls, releases on ATP binding, re-cocks on ATP hydrolysis |
| Motor unit | One motor neuron and every fibre it innervates; the unit of force gradation in skeletal muscle |
| Transverse (T) tubule | Membrane invagination that carries the action potential into a striated fibre |
| Sarcoplasmic reticulum | Internal calcium store released by the action potential |
| Intercalated disc | Cardiac junction combining desmosomes and gap junctions; makes the heart a functional syncytium |
| Type I / type II fibres | Slow oxidative, fatigue-resistant fibres versus fast glycolytic, powerful, fatiguable fibres |
| Latch state | Smooth-muscle mechanism for holding tension with little ATP |
| Satellite cell | Stem cell on the surface of a skeletal fibre that repairs it |
| Rhabdomyolysis | Breakdown of skeletal muscle releasing potassium, myoglobin and enzymes into blood |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Muscle gets stronger by growing new fibres."** Adult skeletal muscle grows almost entirely by thickening existing fibres; fibre number is fixed early in life. The uterus and arterial smooth muscle are the exceptions that do add cells.
- **"The heart is a fast skeletal muscle that never rests."** It rests after every beat, for longer than it contracts; its long refractory period is what forbids tetanus and allows filling.
- **"Smooth muscle is weak."** Per cross-sectional area it generates force comparable to skeletal muscle, more slowly and far more cheaply; the uterus in labour and the bladder emptying against obstruction are not weak.
