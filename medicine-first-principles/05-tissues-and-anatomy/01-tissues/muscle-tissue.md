## Essence

Muscle turns the chemical energy of Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) into force by letting myosin heads pull on actin filaments. The body builds this one motor into three tissues, skeletal, cardiac and smooth, and the differences are not decoration: how the filaments are arranged, how calcium is delivered and whether the cells are electrically coupled decide whether a muscle is fast or slow, graded or all-or-none, voluntary or automatic, and whether it can heal. Muscle is also about 40% of body mass, the main heat source and the largest store of protein and potassium, so what happens to it matters far beyond movement.

## Core tenets

1. **One motor, three packagings.** In every muscle cell myosin binds actin, flexes, releases on binding ATP and re-cocks, and the cycle is switched on by a rise in cytoplasmic calcium. Skeletal and cardiac muscle line the filaments up in sarcomeres, giving striations and a force that peaks at an optimum sarcomere length of about 2.0–2.2 micrometres; the heart works on the rising limb of that curve, so filling it more makes it contract harder (the Frank–Starling relation). Smooth muscle anchors oblique bundles to dense bodies, which costs speed but lets the cell shorten to a fraction of its length and hold tension for hours at little energy cost.

2. **Skeletal muscle is a syncytium built for speed and voluntary gradation.** Each fibre is one giant multinucleate cell formed by fusion of myoblasts, driven by exactly one motor neuron at one neuromuscular junction and coupled to no neighbour, so force is graded by how many motor units are recruited and how fast they fire. Transverse tubules carry the action potential inward and open sarcoplasmic-reticulum calcium stores within milliseconds. Type I fibres (mitochondria, myoglobin, capillaries) resist fatigue; type II fibres produce more force faster from glycogen and exhaust in minutes. The nerve sets the type.

3. **Cardiac muscle is coupled so the heart behaves as one cell.** Cardiomyocytes are short, branched and joined by intercalated discs combining desmosomes (strength) with gap junctions (electrical continuity), so a depolarisation anywhere spreads to the whole chamber. The action potential lasts hundreds of milliseconds, so the refractory period outlasts contraction, the heart cannot be tetanised and must relax to fill. Contraction depends on calcium entering from outside during the plateau and triggering release from stores, the handle by which calcium-channel blockers alter contractility. Cardiomyocytes barely divide after birth, so dead heart becomes scar that neither contracts nor conducts normally.

4. **Smooth muscle is slow, economical and answers to almost everything.** It has no sarcomeres, transverse tubules or neuromuscular junctions; nerves release transmitter from swellings along their length, and the cell also responds to stretch, hormones and local metabolites. Contraction is switched on by calcium-calmodulin-dependent phosphorylation of myosin, slow but able to hold tension at a fraction of a striated muscle's ATP cost (the latch state). Single-unit smooth muscle (gut, ureter, uterus, small vessels) is gap-junction coupled and driven by pacemakers; multi-unit (iris, airway, large arteries) is controlled fibre by fibre. Smooth muscle divides, so the uterus grows by hyperplasia in pregnancy and arteries thicken under hypertension.

5. **Muscle mass is plastic, and the direction depends on load and nerve.** Load and amino acids drive protein synthesis; disuse, denervation, starvation, cortisol and inflammation drive breakdown, so a limb in a cast loses strength within days and a denervated muscle wastes. Skeletal muscle regenerates from satellite cells on the fibre surface, so a torn muscle heals, but fibres replaced by fat and fibrous tissue (muscular dystrophy, chronic denervation) do not return. In Duchenne dystrophy the missing dystrophin is the link between contractile apparatus and membrane; each contraction tears the membrane and the muscle dies by a thousand small injuries.

6. **Muscle is dangerous when it breaks down.** Fibres hold the body's largest pools of potassium, creatine kinase, myoglobin and phosphate. Crush, prolonged immobility, extreme exertion and some drugs rupture enough fibres to release them (rhabdomyolysis): potassium stops the heart, myoglobin blocks renal tubules, and swollen muscle inside its fascial compartment cuts off its own blood supply.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sarcomere | Repeating actin–myosin unit between Z-discs; source of striation and the length–force relation |
| Cross-bridge cycle | Myosin binds actin, pulls, releases on ATP binding, re-cocks on hydrolysis |
| Motor unit | One motor neuron and every fibre it innervates; the unit of force gradation |
| Transverse (T) tubule | Membrane invagination carrying the action potential into a striated fibre |
| Sarcoplasmic reticulum | Internal calcium store released by the action potential |
| Intercalated disc | Cardiac junction of desmosomes and gap junctions; makes the heart a functional syncytium |
| Type I / type II fibres | Slow oxidative, fatigue-resistant versus fast glycolytic, powerful, fatiguable |
| Satellite cell | Stem cell on a skeletal fibre that repairs it |
| Rhabdomyolysis | Breakdown of skeletal muscle releasing potassium, myoglobin and enzymes |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Muscle gets stronger by growing new fibres."** Adult skeletal muscle grows by thickening existing fibres; fibre number is fixed early. Uterine and arterial smooth muscle are the exceptions that add cells.
- **"The heart is a fast skeletal muscle that never rests."** It rests after every beat, for longer than it contracts; the long refractory period forbids tetanus and allows filling.
- **"Smooth muscle is weak."** Per cross-sectional area it generates force comparable to skeletal muscle, more slowly and far more cheaply.
