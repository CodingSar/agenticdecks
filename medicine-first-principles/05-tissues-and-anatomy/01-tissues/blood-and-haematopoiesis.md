## Essence

Blood is a connective tissue whose matrix is liquid, which lets it be pumped through every other tissue carrying oxygen, fuel, heat, hormones, waste and the cells of defence and repair. An adult has about 5 L, roughly 7% of body mass: a little under half cells, the rest plasma. All the cells descend from haematopoietic stem cells in the marrow and each lineage has a fixed lifespan in circulation, so a blood count is a snapshot of a production line, not a stock. The idea that makes blood intelligible is **flux**: circulating number equals production rate times lifespan, and nearly every abnormality changes one of those terms.

## Core tenets

1. **Plasma is water, salt and about 70 g/L of protein, and the protein keeps water in the vessels.** Albumin (about 40 g/L) provides most of the colloid osmotic pressure that draws filtered fluid back into capillaries, binds drugs, hormones and bilirubin, and is made only by the liver; globulins include antibodies and carriers; fibrinogen and the clotting factors circulate inactive. Low albumin from liver failure, renal leak or starvation causes oedema by removing the pull that opposes capillary pressure.

2. **The red cell is a bag of haemoglobin shaped for diffusion and deformation.** A biconcave disc 7–8 micrometres across has a large surface for its volume and no nucleus or mitochondria, so nearly all its content is haemoglobin and it can fold through capillaries narrower than itself. About 5 × 10^12 per litre each live ~120 days before splenic macrophages remove them, so the marrow makes about 2 × 10^11 a day. Abnormal shapes fail at deformability: spherocytes and sickled cells are trapped and destroyed in the spleen, hence haemolytic anaemia.

3. **The haematocrit is a compromise between carrying oxygen and flowing.** Oxygen content rises with red-cell concentration, but viscosity rises exponentially with haematocrit and flow falls with viscosity. The normal 0.40–0.50 is near the optimum for delivery; polycythaemia above about 0.55 slows flow enough to provoke thrombosis. In the smallest vessels red cells stream centrally and effective viscosity falls (the Fåhræus–Lindqvist effect), so the microcirculation tolerates high haematocrits better than large vessels do.

4. **All blood cells come from one stem-cell hierarchy in a factory with large idle capacity.** A haematopoietic stem cell renews itself and yields progenitors committed to the myeloid line (red cells, platelets, granulocytes, monocytes) or the lymphoid line (B, T and natural killer cells). Output is set by cytokines that measure demand: erythropoietin from the kidney when oxygen is low, thrombopoietin from the liver, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor from inflamed tissue. The marrow makes on the order of 10^11 cells a day and can raise red-cell output five- to eight-fold given iron. By adulthood only the axial skeleton and the ends of long bones hold red marrow; the fetal sites, liver and spleen, resume the job if it fails.

5. **Lifespan determines how fast each count responds.** Neutrophils live 6–8 hours in blood with a marrow reserve many times the circulating number, so a count doubles within hours of infection and the roughly one-week marrow transit time sets the neutrophil nadir after chemotherapy. Platelets live 7–10 days. Red cells live 120 days, so anaemia of failed production develops over months while anaemia of destruction or bleeding appears in a day and is betrayed by a rising reticulocyte count.

6. **Blood is a transit compartment for white cells, not their home.** Most lymphocytes are in nodes, spleen and tissues and recirculate through blood for hours; monocytes leave to become tissue macrophages; about half the neutrophils in vessels roll along the endothelium rather than flow (the marginated pool). Adrenaline or corticosteroids release that pool and raise the count without new production, so a white count measures traffic and is read with the clinical state. The same caution applies to every blood value: blood samples every tissue, which makes it the most used diagnostic material, and every number in it is a rate in a flow, not a quantity in a tank.

7. **Platelets respond to the one thing blood must never meet: tissue beneath the endothelium.** Megakaryocytes shed thousands of anucleate platelets each; at 150–400 × 10^9/L they circulate inert until exposed collagen or von Willebrand factor activates them to adhere, change shape and aggregate into the plug that plasma coagulation then reinforces. Bleeding from too few platelets (mucosal, petechial) differs from bleeding from missing factors (deep, into joints), so the two are tested separately.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Plasma / serum | The liquid matrix; serum is plasma minus clotting factors |
| Haematocrit | Fraction of blood volume occupied by red cells, about 0.40–0.50 |
| Albumin | Main plasma protein; source of colloid osmotic pressure and chief carrier of drugs |
| Haematopoietic stem cell | Self-renewing marrow cell from which all blood lineages descend |
| Myeloid / lymphoid | Red cells, platelets, granulocytes, monocytes versus B, T and natural killer cells |
| Erythropoietin | Kidney hormone raising red-cell production when oxygen delivery falls |
| Reticulocyte | Red cell released within a day or two; its count measures marrow output |
| Marginated pool | Neutrophils adherent to vessel walls, released by adrenaline and steroids |
| Fåhræus–Lindqvist effect | Fall in apparent viscosity in vessels below about 300 micrometres |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A normal blood count means a normal marrow."** The marrow compensates many-fold; counts fall only when production is exhausted or outrun.
- **"More red cells always mean more oxygen delivery."** Beyond a haematocrit of roughly 0.5, rising viscosity cuts flow faster than oxygen content rises.
- **"A high white-cell count means infection."** Stress, steroids, smoking, pregnancy and leukaemia raise it too; the differential and the clinical picture say why.
