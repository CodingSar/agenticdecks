## Essence

Blood is a connective tissue whose matrix is liquid, which lets it be pumped through every other tissue to carry oxygen, fuel, heat, hormones, waste and the cells of defence and repair. An adult has about 5 L of it, roughly 7% of body mass: a little under half is cells, the rest plasma. All of the cells descend from a small population of haematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow, and each lineage has a fixed lifespan in the circulation, so a blood count is a snapshot of a production line, not a stock. The idea that makes blood intelligible is **flux**: circulating number equals production rate times lifespan, and nearly every abnormality is a change in one of those two terms.

## Core tenets

1. **Plasma is water, salt and about 70 g/L of protein, and the protein is what keeps water in the vessels.** Albumin (about 40 g/L) provides most of the colloid osmotic pressure that draws filtered fluid back into capillaries, binds drugs, hormones and bilirubin, and is made only by the liver; globulins include antibodies and transport proteins; fibrinogen and the clotting factors are dissolved and inactive until needed. Serum is plasma with the clot removed. Low albumin from liver failure, kidney leak or starvation produces oedema by removing the pull that opposes capillary pressure.

2. **The red cell is a bag of haemoglobin shaped for diffusion and deformation.** A biconcave disc 7–8 micrometres across has a large surface for its volume and no nucleus or mitochondria, so almost its entire content (haemoglobin at about 330 g/L inside the cell) is available for gas transport, and it can fold to pass capillaries narrower than itself. There are about 5 × 10^12 red cells per litre, each living about 120 days before macrophages in the spleen remove it; the marrow must make about 2 × 10^11 a day to keep up. Abnormal shapes fail at deformability: spherocytes and sickled cells are trapped and destroyed in the spleen, which is why those conditions cause haemolytic anaemia.

3. **The haematocrit is a compromise between carrying oxygen and flowing.** Oxygen content rises with red-cell concentration, but blood viscosity rises exponentially with haematocrit, and flow through a vessel falls with viscosity. The normal haematocrit of about 0.40–0.50 is near the optimum for oxygen delivery; polycythaemia above about 0.55 raises viscosity enough to slow flow and provoke thrombosis, while in the smallest vessels red cells stream centrally and effective viscosity falls (the Fåhræus–Lindqvist effect), which is why the microcirculation tolerates high haematocrits better than large vessels do.

4. **All blood cells come from one stem-cell hierarchy, and the marrow is a factory with a large idle capacity.** A haematopoietic stem cell renews itself and gives rise to progenitors committed to the myeloid line (red cells, platelets, granulocytes, monocytes) or the lymphoid line (B and T lymphocytes, natural killer cells). Lineage choice and output are set by cytokines that measure demand: erythropoietin from the kidney when oxygen is low, thrombopoietin from the liver, and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor from inflamed tissue. In health the marrow produces on the order of 10^11 cells a day and can increase red-cell output five- to eight-fold if given iron. In the fetus the yolk sac, liver and spleen make blood; by adulthood only the axial skeleton and the ends of the long bones hold red marrow, and the liver and spleen can resume the job if the marrow is replaced or fails.

5. **Lifespan determines how fast each cell count responds.** Neutrophils live about 6–8 hours in blood and are backed by a marrow reserve many times the circulating number, so a count can double within hours of infection and the marrow transit time of about a week sets the timing of the neutrophil nadir after chemotherapy. Platelets live 7–10 days. Red cells live 120 days, so an anaemia of failed production develops over months, while an anaemia of destruction or bleeding can appear in a day and is betrayed by a rising reticulocyte count, the marrow's export of freshly made cells. Thrombocytopenia with a marrow full of megakaryocytes means platelets are being destroyed; with an empty marrow, not made.

6. **Blood is a transit compartment for white cells, not their home.** Most lymphocytes are in nodes, spleen and tissues at any moment and recirculate through blood for hours; monocytes leave blood to become tissue macrophages; neutrophils are destined for tissues and about half of those in the vessels are rolling along the endothelium rather than flowing (the marginated pool). Adrenaline or corticosteroids release that pool and raise the count without any new production, so a white-cell count measures traffic and must be read with the clinical state.

7. **Platelets are cell fragments that respond to the one thing blood must never meet: tissue beneath the endothelium.** Megakaryocytes shed thousands of anucleate platelets each; at 150–400 × 10^9/L they circulate inert until exposed collagen or von Willebrand factor at a breach activates them to adhere, change shape and aggregate, forming the plug that the plasma coagulation cascade then reinforces. Their number and function, and the plasma factors, are tested separately because bleeding from too few platelets (mucosal, petechial) looks different from bleeding from missing factors (deep, into joints and muscle).

8. **Blood carries the body's buffers, heat and signals, and its composition is read as a window on every organ.** Bicarbonate and haemoglobin buffer acid produced by metabolism; the flow of warm blood from core to skin is the main lever of temperature control; every hormone and most drugs reach their target in plasma, usually bound to albumin. Because blood samples every tissue, its chemistry and cells are the most widely used diagnostic material in medicine, but every value must be interpreted as a rate in a flow, not a quantity in a tank.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Plasma / serum | The liquid matrix of blood; plasma minus clotting factors after clotting |
| Haematocrit | Fraction of blood volume occupied by red cells, about 0.40–0.50 |
| Albumin | Main plasma protein; the source of colloid osmotic pressure and the chief carrier of drugs and hormones |
| Haematopoietic stem cell | Self-renewing marrow cell from which all blood lineages descend |
| Myeloid / lymphoid | The two branches: red cells, platelets, granulocytes, monocytes versus B, T and natural killer cells |
| Erythropoietin | Kidney hormone that raises red-cell production when oxygen delivery falls |
| Reticulocyte | Red cell released within the last day or two; its count measures marrow output |
| Marginated pool | Neutrophils adherent to vessel walls, released by adrenaline and steroids |
| Megakaryocyte | Large marrow cell that fragments into platelets |
| Fåhræus–Lindqvist effect | Fall in apparent blood viscosity in vessels below about 300 micrometres as red cells stream centrally |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A normal blood count means a normal marrow."** The marrow can compensate for destruction or loss many-fold; counts fall only when production is exhausted or the loss outruns it. The reticulocyte count and the marrow itself tell the two apart.
- **"More red cells always mean more oxygen delivery."** Beyond a haematocrit of roughly 0.5, rising viscosity cuts flow faster than oxygen content rises, and delivery falls.
- **"A high white-cell count means infection."** Stress, steroids, smoking, pregnancy and leukaemia raise it too; a count is traffic, and the differential and the clinical picture say why.
