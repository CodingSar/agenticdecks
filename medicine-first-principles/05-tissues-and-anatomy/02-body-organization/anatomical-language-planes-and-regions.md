## Essence

Anatomy needs a coordinate system before it can say anything, because "above", "in front of" and "left" change meaning as soon as a patient lies down, turns over or is viewed on a screen. The anatomical position (standing, facing forward, arms at the sides, palms forward) fixes a frame on the body itself, and three planes and a handful of paired directional terms describe every structure relative to that frame, not to the room. The vocabulary is a convention, but it is not arbitrary: the planes are the ones imaging reconstructs, the terms derive from the embryo's axes, and surface landmarks map the interior onto the skin so that examination and procedures can find organs they cannot see. Getting this language wrong is how operations are done on the wrong side.

## Core tenets

1. **The frame is attached to the body, not the observer.** In the anatomical position, superior and inferior run along the long axis, anterior (ventral) and posterior (dorsal) are front and back, medial and lateral are toward and away from the midline, and superficial and deep are relative to the surface. A supine patient's sternum is still anterior and their spine posterior. Left and right are always the patient's. Because the limbs can move, they use proximal and distal (nearer or farther from the trunk) rather than superior and inferior.

2. **Three orthogonal planes carry all imaging and most description.** The sagittal plane divides left from right (the median plane is the midline sagittal plane); the coronal plane divides front from back; the axial or transverse plane divides upper from lower. Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging acquire and display in these planes. By convention an axial image is shown as if viewed from the patient's feet, so the patient's left appears on the viewer's right, and a chest radiograph is shown as if you faced the patient, with the same effect. The whole of radiological interpretation assumes this convention, and surgical site marking and the surgical safety checklist exist because a reversed image, a mirrored label or a verbal "left" referred to the wrong frame has repeatedly led to wrong-side surgery.

3. **The terms come from the embryo, and the limbs rotated.** Dorsal and ventral, cranial and caudal are the axes laid down by the patterning genes of the early embryo, and they persist in the names of nerve roots (dorsal and ventral), the surfaces of the hand (dorsum and palm) and foot (dorsum and sole). During development the upper limb bud rotates laterally and the lower limb bud rotates medially, so the thumb ends up lateral, the great toe medial, the elbow points back and the knee points forward, and the flexor muscles face anterior in the arm but posterior in the leg. This rotation is why limb dermatomes spiral around the limb while trunk dermatomes are horizontal bands, and why forearm anatomy uses radial and ulnar instead of lateral and medial: pronation turns the radius across the ulna and would otherwise swap the words.

4. **Movements are defined by the plane they occur in.** Flexion and extension are movements in the sagittal plane that decrease or increase the angle between segments; abduction and adduction move a part away from or toward the midline in the coronal plane; rotation turns a part about its long axis. Special terms cover joints whose geometry does not fit: pronation and supination of the forearm, dorsiflexion and plantarflexion of the ankle, inversion and eversion of the foot, opposition of the thumb. Range of motion is measured in degrees from the anatomical position, which is what makes a joint examination reproducible between examiners.

5. **Surface landmarks let you find the inside from the outside.** The sternal angle (manubriosternal joint) marks the second costal cartilage, the level of the fourth thoracic vertebra, the bifurcation of the trachea and the arch of the aorta; it is where rib counting begins. The iliac crests lie at the fourth lumbar vertebra, and because the spinal cord ends at the first or second lumbar vertebra in adults, a needle placed at or below the crest line enters the cerebrospinal fluid without touching the cord. The transpyloric plane, halfway between the suprasternal notch and the pubic symphysis, passes through the first lumbar vertebra, the pylorus, the neck of the pancreas and the hila of the kidneys. The heart's apex beat is in the fifth left intercostal space in the midclavicular line, and the liver's edge normally rests under the right costal margin. Every physical sign and every needle, drain and incision is placed by landmarks like these, and they change in children, in pregnancy and with body shape.

6. **Regions divide the body for description and for diagnosis.** The abdomen is divided into nine regions by two vertical and two horizontal lines, or into four quadrants through the umbilicus, and the location of pain or a mass in those regions is the first filter in reasoning about it: right upper quadrant for liver and gallbladder, epigastrium for stomach and pancreas, right iliac fossa for appendix and caecum, suprapubic for bladder and uterus. The limbs are divided into compartments and the neck into triangles for the same reason: a region predicts the structures beneath it.

7. **The textbook body is a mode, not a rule.** Anatomy is a distribution. About a quarter to a third of kidneys have more than one renal artery; the coeliac trunk, hepatic arteries and circle of Willis branch in several common patterns; the palmaris longus tendon is absent in roughly one person in seven; mirror-image arrangement of all the organs (situs inversus) occurs in about 1 in 10,000 people. Variation is the reason that imaging precedes surgery, that a surgeon identifies a structure rather than assumes it, and that a "normal" value or position on examination is a range. Age, sex and body habitus shift the landmarks: the larynx and the diaphragm are higher in children, the heart lies more horizontally in a short wide chest, and pregnancy pushes the appendix upward.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Anatomical position | Standing, facing forward, arms at the sides, palms forward: the reference frame for all terms |
| Sagittal / coronal / axial | Planes dividing left–right, front–back and upper–lower |
| Anterior (ventral) / posterior (dorsal) | Toward the front or the back of the body in the anatomical position |
| Medial / lateral | Toward or away from the median plane |
| Proximal / distal | Nearer to or farther from the trunk along a limb |
| Superficial / deep | Nearer to or farther from the surface |
| Flexion / extension | Movements in the sagittal plane that decrease or increase the joint angle |
| Abduction / adduction | Movements away from or toward the midline in the coronal plane |
| Radiological convention | Axial images and radiographs are displayed as if facing the patient, so the patient's left is on the viewer's right |
| Transpyloric plane | Horizontal plane through the first lumbar vertebra, used to locate upper abdominal organs |
| Anatomical variant | A structure whose arrangement differs from the commonest pattern without being a disease |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Left on the image is the patient's left."** On a conventionally displayed radiograph or axial scan the patient's left is on the viewer's right; checking the side marker is the first step of reading any image.
- **"The anatomy in the atlas is what the patient has."** The atlas shows the commonest pattern; vessels, nerves and organ positions vary, and the variants are common enough that a surgeon or radiologist must expect them.
- **"Up and down are the same in a lying patient."** Superior and inferior refer to the head-to-foot axis whatever the posture; dependent regions (where fluid pools) change with position, and that distinction matters for examining effusions and reading erect versus supine films.
