## Essence

Anatomy needs a coordinate system before it can say anything, because "above", "in front of" and "left" change meaning as soon as a patient lies down or is viewed on a screen. The anatomical position (standing, facing forward, palms forward) fixes a frame on the body itself, and three planes plus a few paired terms describe every structure relative to that frame, not to the room. The vocabulary is a convention but not arbitrary: the planes are those imaging reconstructs, the terms derive from the embryo's axes, and surface landmarks map the interior onto the skin. Getting the language wrong is how operations are done on the wrong side.

## Core tenets

1. **The frame is attached to the body, not the observer.** Superior and inferior run along the long axis, anterior (ventral) and posterior (dorsal) are front and back, medial and lateral are toward and away from the midline, superficial and deep are relative to the surface. A supine patient's sternum is still anterior. Left and right are always the patient's. Limbs use proximal and distal. Movements are named by plane: flexion and extension in the sagittal plane, abduction and adduction in the coronal, rotation about the long axis, measured in degrees from the anatomical position so that a joint examination is reproducible.

2. **Three orthogonal planes carry all imaging.** Sagittal divides left from right, coronal front from back, axial (transverse) upper from lower; computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging display in these. By convention an axial image is viewed from the patient's feet and a radiograph as if facing the patient, so the patient's left appears on the viewer's right. Surgical site marking and the surgical safety checklist exist because a reversed image or a "left" in the wrong frame has repeatedly led to wrong-side surgery.

3. **The terms come from the embryo, and the limbs rotated.** Dorsal, ventral, cranial and caudal are the axes laid down by the patterning genes and persist in the names of nerve roots and the surfaces of hand and foot. The upper limb bud rotates laterally and the lower medially, so the thumb is lateral and the great toe medial, the elbow points back and the knee forward, and the flexors face anterior in the arm but posterior in the leg. This is why limb dermatomes spiral while trunk dermatomes are horizontal bands, and why the forearm uses radial and ulnar rather than lateral and medial: pronation turns the radius across the ulna and would swap the words.

4. **Surface landmarks let you find the inside from the outside.** The sternal angle marks the second costal cartilage, the fourth thoracic vertebra, the tracheal bifurcation and the aortic arch, and is where rib counting begins. The iliac crests lie at the fourth lumbar vertebra; because the adult cord ends at L1–L2, a needle at or below the crest line reaches cerebrospinal fluid without touching cord. The transpyloric plane (L1) passes through the pylorus, the pancreatic neck and the renal hila. Every needle, drain and incision is placed by landmarks like these, which shift in children, in pregnancy and with body shape.

5. **Regions divide the body for description and for diagnosis.** The abdomen is divided into nine regions or four quadrants, and the location of pain or a mass there is the first filter in reasoning: right upper quadrant for liver and gallbladder, epigastrium for stomach and pancreas, right iliac fossa for appendix and caecum, suprapubic for bladder and uterus. A region predicts the structures beneath it.

6. **The textbook body is a mode, not a rule.** About a quarter to a third of kidneys have more than one renal artery; the coeliac trunk and circle of Willis branch in several common patterns; the palmaris longus tendon is absent in roughly one person in seven; complete mirror-image arrangement (situs inversus) occurs in about 1 in 10,000. Variation is why imaging precedes surgery and why a surgeon identifies a structure rather than assumes it.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Anatomical position | Standing, facing forward, arms at the sides, palms forward: the reference frame |
| Sagittal / coronal / axial | Planes dividing left–right, front–back and upper–lower |
| Anterior (ventral) / posterior (dorsal) | Toward the front or back in the anatomical position |
| Medial / lateral | Toward or away from the median plane |
| Proximal / distal | Nearer to or farther from the trunk along a limb |
| Flexion / abduction / rotation | Movement in the sagittal plane, in the coronal plane, and about the long axis |
| Radiological convention | Images are displayed as if facing the patient, so the patient's left is on the viewer's right |
| Transpyloric plane | Horizontal plane through L1 used to locate upper abdominal organs |
| Anatomical variant | An arrangement differing from the commonest pattern without being a disease |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Left on the image is the patient's left."** On a conventionally displayed radiograph or axial scan the patient's left is on the viewer's right; checking the side marker is the first step of reading any image.
- **"The anatomy in the atlas is what the patient has."** The atlas shows the commonest pattern; variants are common enough that a surgeon or radiologist must expect them.
- **"Up and down are the same in a lying patient."** Superior and inferior follow the head-to-foot axis whatever the posture; dependent regions, where fluid pools, change with position.
