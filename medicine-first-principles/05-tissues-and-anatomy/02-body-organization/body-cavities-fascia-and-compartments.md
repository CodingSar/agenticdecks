## Essence

The body is not one bag but a nested set of compartments: the skull, the spinal canal, the thorax with its two pleural sacs and the pericardium, the abdomen and pelvis with the peritoneal sac, and in every limb a set of osteofascial compartments. Each is bounded by bone or by dense connective tissue that stretches little or not at all, and each contains organs that move, swell, bleed or fill with fluid. The one idea that explains most of what goes wrong is the **pressure–volume relation of a closed compartment**: while the wall can give, added volume costs little pressure; once it cannot, a small further volume sends pressure up steeply, and when pressure in the compartment exceeds the pressure driving blood into it, the contents die. Compartment syndrome, cardiac tamponade, tension pneumothorax, raised intracranial pressure and abdominal compartment syndrome are one physical fact in five places.

## Core tenets

1. **A compartment with an inextensible wall converts volume into pressure.** The skull is the extreme case: brain, blood and cerebrospinal fluid share a fixed volume, so a growing haematoma or swelling is first bought by squeezing out venous blood and fluid, and then, when that reserve is spent, by a steep rise in intracranial pressure that reduces cerebral perfusion and finally pushes brain through the dural openings (the Monro–Kellie doctrine). The fibrous pericardium behaves the same way: 150–200 mL of blood arriving fast fills the reserve and stops the heart filling (tamponade), whereas a litre accumulating over weeks stretches the sac and may be tolerated. A fascial limb compartment follows the same curve; once its pressure approaches the capillary perfusion pressure (within about 30 mmHg of diastolic pressure, or an absolute pressure above about 30 mmHg) the muscle and nerve inside become ischaemic while the arterial pulse distal to it is still present, because the artery carries a far higher pressure than the capillaries.

2. **Serous cavities are closed sacs into which organs are pushed, not boxes they sit in.** The pleural, pericardial and peritoneal cavities are derived from the embryonic coelom and lined by mesothelium. The organ invaginates the sac like a fist pushed into a balloon, so the layer on the organ (visceral) is continuous with the layer on the wall (parietal) at a reflection, and the cavity itself is a potential space holding only a film of lubricating fluid. This lets the lung, heart and gut slide freely against the wall with every breath, beat and peristaltic wave. The parietal layer is supplied by the somatic nerves of the body wall and signals sharp, well-localised pain; the visceral layer has only autonomic afferents and signals dull, midline, poorly localised pain. Pleuritic chest pain and the two phases of the acute abdomen are this distinction in action.

3. **The peritoneal cavity has a geography that decides where fluid goes.** Mesenteries suspend the intraperitoneal organs and carry their vessels and nerves; retroperitoneal organs (kidneys, pancreas, most of the duodenum, ascending and descending colon) lie behind the sac and leak or bleed into the retroperitoneum, where peritoneal signs are late or absent. The greater sac is divided by the transverse mesocolon into supracolic and infracolic parts and communicates with the lesser sac behind the stomach through the epiploic foramen. Blood, pus and ascites follow gravity along the paracolic gutters into the pelvis (the lowest point when upright) or into the right subhepatic space (the lowest point of the upper abdomen when supine), and collect under the diaphragm, where they irritate the diaphragmatic peritoneum supplied by the phrenic nerve and produce pain at the shoulder tip. Metastases that shed into the fluid implant along the same paths. Ascites is the Starling balance of the peritoneal capillaries pushed toward filtration by portal hypertension and low albumin.

4. **Fascia is the connective-tissue packaging, and it has two layers that behave differently.** Superficial fascia is the fatty layer under the skin: an energy store, an insulator and the reservoir in which oedema and cellulitis spread. Deep fascia is a dense, inelastic sheet that invests muscles, sends septa to bone to divide each limb segment into compartments, thickens into retinacula that hold tendons down at the wrist and ankle, and forms the sheaths and spaces of the neck. A compartment is a muscle group with its own nerve and artery, and the neurovascular bundles run in the septa between compartments. Fascial planes guide the spread of infection (necrotising fasciitis travels along them faster than through muscle), set the route of pus and haematoma, carry injected local anaesthetic to the nerves that lie in them (fascial plane blocks), and are where surgeons dissect, because they are bloodless.

5. **Pressure differences across compartment walls drive flow, and the diaphragm is the main wall.** The thorax is at negative pressure during inspiration and the abdomen at positive pressure, so each breath pulls venous blood and lymph from abdomen to chest; the pleural pressure of a few centimetres of water below atmospheric is what holds the lung against the chest wall. Open the pleural space and the lung collapses and the wall springs out; let air in through a valve that will not let it out (tension pneumothorax) and the rising pressure shifts the mediastinum and stops venous return. Raised abdominal pressure from distension, ascites, bleeding or over-resuscitation (abdominal compartment syndrome) splints the diaphragm, compresses the renal veins and inferior vena cava, and fails the kidney and the lung together.

6. **A hernia is a compartment breached at a weak point.** Wherever a structure passes through a wall (the spermatic cord through the inguinal canal, the femoral vessels under the inguinal ligament, the oesophagus through the diaphragm, the umbilical vessels through the abdominal wall, a surgical incision), the wall is weaker and pressure can push a viscus through it into the neighbouring compartment. The danger of a hernia is not the protrusion but the neck: a narrow opening compresses the veins of the herniated bowel before its artery, so it swells, becomes obstructed, then infarcts (strangulation). The same logic applies to the brain herniating through the tentorial notch and to the stomach through the oesophageal hiatus.

7. **Potential spaces exist to be filled.** The pleural, pericardial and peritoneal cavities, the subdural and extradural spaces around the brain, and the retropharyngeal and other spaces of the neck are empty in health but open to blood, pus, air, lymph or transudate. A filled space presses on its neighbours in proportion to the compliance of the compartment, and its contents can be drained by a needle placed with the surface landmarks of the cavity's margins. Much of acute medicine is recognising which space has filled with what.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Compliance | Volume change per unit pressure change; a compartment's compliance falls as its reserve is used |
| Monro–Kellie doctrine | Within the rigid skull, brain, blood and cerebrospinal fluid share a fixed volume |
| Compartment syndrome | Pressure within a closed fascial or body compartment high enough to stop capillary perfusion of its contents |
| Serous membrane | Mesothelium-lined sac with a parietal layer on the wall and a visceral layer on the organ |
| Potential space | A closed space that holds only a film of fluid in health but can fill in disease |
| Mesentery | Double fold of peritoneum suspending an organ and carrying its vessels and nerves |
| Retroperitoneal | Lying behind the peritoneal sac against the posterior wall |
| Superficial / deep fascia | The fatty subcutaneous layer versus the dense inelastic sheet that invests muscles and forms compartments |
| Fascial plane | The cleavage between two fascial surfaces along which fluid, infection, anaesthetic and dissection travel |
| Hernia | Protrusion of a viscus through a weak point in the wall of its compartment |
| Strangulation | Venous obstruction, then infarction, of herniated bowel by the constricting neck of the hernia |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A palpable pulse rules out compartment syndrome."** The artery carries systolic pressure of 120 mmHg or more; capillaries are shut by compartment pressures of 30–40 mmHg, so pulses persist until far too late. Pain out of proportion and pain on passive stretch are the early signs.
- **"The lungs are inside the pleural cavity."** Each lung is outside its pleural sac, covered by the visceral layer; the cavity is a film between two surfaces. This is why a pneumothorax surrounds the lung rather than entering it.
- **"A hernia is dangerous because it is large."** Large hernias with wide necks rarely strangulate; small ones with narrow necks (femoral) do.
