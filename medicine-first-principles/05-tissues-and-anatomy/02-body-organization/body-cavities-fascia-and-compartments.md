## Essence

The body is not one bag but a nested set of compartments: the skull, the spinal canal, the thorax with its two pleural sacs and pericardium, the abdomen and pelvis with the peritoneal sac, and in every limb a set of osteofascial compartments. Each is bounded by bone or by dense connective tissue that stretches little, and each holds organs that move, swell, bleed or fill with fluid. The idea that explains most of what goes wrong is the **pressure–volume relation of a closed compartment**: while the wall can give, added volume costs little pressure; once it cannot, pressure climbs steeply, and when it exceeds the pressure driving blood in, the contents die. Compartment syndrome, tamponade, tension pneumothorax and raised intracranial pressure are one physical fact in four places.

## Core tenets

1. **An inextensible wall converts volume into pressure.** The skull is the extreme case: brain, blood and cerebrospinal fluid share a fixed volume, so a growing haematoma is first paid for by squeezing out venous blood and fluid, then by a steep rise in intracranial pressure that cuts cerebral perfusion and finally pushes brain through the dural openings (the Monro–Kellie doctrine). The fibrous pericardium behaves the same way: 150–200 mL of blood arriving fast stops the heart filling (tamponade), whereas a litre over weeks is tolerated. A fascial limb compartment follows the same curve; once its pressure comes within about 30 mmHg of diastolic pressure the muscle and nerve inside are ischaemic while the distal pulse persists, because the artery carries far more pressure than the capillaries.

2. **Serous cavities are closed sacs into which organs are pushed, not boxes they sit in.** The pleural, pericardial and peritoneal cavities derive from the embryonic coelom and are lined by mesothelium. The organ invaginates the sac like a fist pushed into a balloon, so visceral and parietal layers are continuous and the cavity is a potential space holding a film of lubricant; in disease that space fills with blood, pus, air or transudate and presses on its neighbours. The parietal layer is supplied by somatic nerves of the body wall and signals sharp, localised pain; the visceral layer has only autonomic afferents and signals dull, midline pain. Pleuritic chest pain and the two phases of the acute abdomen are this distinction in action.

3. **The peritoneal cavity has a geography that decides where fluid goes.** Retroperitoneal organs (kidneys, pancreas, most of the duodenum, ascending and descending colon) leak or bleed behind the sac, where peritoneal signs are late or absent. Blood, pus and ascites follow gravity along the paracolic gutters into the pelvis when upright or into the right subhepatic space when supine, and collect under the diaphragm, whose peritoneum is supplied by the phrenic nerve, producing pain at the shoulder tip. Metastases shed into the fluid implant along the same paths.

4. **Fascia is the connective-tissue packaging, in two layers that behave differently.** Superficial fascia is the fatty layer in which oedema and cellulitis spread. Deep fascia is a dense, inelastic sheet that invests muscles, sends septa to bone to divide each limb segment into compartments, and forms the sheaths and spaces of the neck. A compartment is a muscle group with its own nerve and artery, and the neurovascular bundles run in the septa between compartments. Fascial planes guide the spread of infection (necrotising fasciitis), carry injected local anaesthetic to the nerves lying in them, and are where surgeons dissect, because they are bloodless.

5. **Pressure differences across compartment walls drive flow, and the diaphragm is the main wall.** The thorax is at negative pressure during inspiration and the abdomen at positive pressure, so each breath pulls venous blood and lymph into the chest; a pleural pressure a few centimetres of water below atmospheric holds the lung against the chest wall. Open the pleural space and the lung collapses; let air in through a valve that will not let it out (tension pneumothorax) and the rising pressure shifts the mediastinum and stops venous return. Raised abdominal pressure (abdominal compartment syndrome) splints the diaphragm and compresses the cava and renal veins.

6. **A hernia is a compartment breached at a weak point.** Wherever a structure passes through a wall (spermatic cord, femoral vessels, oesophagus, umbilical vessels, a surgical incision) the wall is weaker and pressure can push a viscus through into the next compartment. The danger is the neck, not the protrusion: a narrow opening compresses the veins of the herniated bowel before its artery, so it swells, obstructs and infarcts (strangulation).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Compliance | Volume change per unit pressure change; falls as a compartment's reserve is used |
| Monro–Kellie doctrine | Within the rigid skull, brain, blood and cerebrospinal fluid share a fixed volume |
| Compartment syndrome | Pressure within a closed compartment high enough to stop capillary perfusion of its contents |
| Serous membrane | Mesothelium-lined sac with a parietal layer on the wall and a visceral layer on the organ |
| Retroperitoneal | Lying behind the peritoneal sac against the posterior wall |
| Superficial / deep fascia | The fatty subcutaneous layer versus the dense sheet that invests muscles and forms compartments |
| Fascial plane | Cleavage between fascial surfaces along which fluid, infection, anaesthetic and dissection travel |
| Strangulation | Venous obstruction, then infarction, of herniated bowel by the constricting neck |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A palpable pulse rules out compartment syndrome."** The artery carries systolic pressure; capillaries are shut by compartment pressures of 30–40 mmHg, so pulses persist until far too late. Pain out of proportion and pain on passive stretch are the early signs.
- **"The lungs are inside the pleural cavity."** Each lung is outside its sac, covered by the visceral layer; the cavity is a film between two surfaces, which is why a pneumothorax surrounds the lung rather than entering it.
- **"A hernia is dangerous because it is large."** Large hernias with wide necks rarely strangulate; small ones with narrow necks (femoral) do.
