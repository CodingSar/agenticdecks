## Essence

The circulation is a closed loop of two pumps and two circuits in series, with the systemic organs in parallel between aorta and venae cavae. That layout, and the structure of each vessel's wall, decides how pressure is distributed, how flow is shared, what happens when one vessel blocks, and where a tumour or infection will travel. The lymphatics are the overflow drain of the same circuit, returning what capillaries filter and cannot reabsorb through nodes that inspect it on the way. The one idea is that **the topology of the vessels is the topology of disease**: a territory with one supply infarcts, a territory between two supplies fails first when pressure falls, and two beds in series share each other's pressure problems.

## Core tenets

1. **Two circuits in series, organs in parallel.** All right-ventricular output crosses the lungs and all left-ventricular output crosses the systemic organs, so the two must match beat for beat and a failing left heart backs blood into the lungs. Systemic organs in parallel each see the same arterial pressure and draw flow set by their own resistance, so a dilated gut or exercising muscle takes more without starving the brain while total output can rise. Portal systems are the exception: all gut and splenic venous blood crosses the liver sinusoids before the heart, and hypothalamic blood crosses a second bed in the pituitary. The second organ gets first use of what the first absorbs, and is hostage to its resistance.

2. **Each wall is built for its pressure and its job.** Elastic arteries near the heart stretch in systole and recoil in diastole, smoothing pulsatile flow; muscular arteries distribute it. Arterioles, thick muscle around a narrow lumen, are the resistance vessels where mean pressure falls from about 90 to 35 mmHg and the body sets each organ's share; since resistance varies with the fourth power of radius, a small calibre change is a large flow change. Capillaries are one endothelial cell thick with a combined area of several hundred square metres, in three permeabilities: continuous (muscle, brain), fenestrated (gut, glands, glomerulus) and sinusoidal (liver, spleen, marrow), where whole cells pass. Veins are thin, valved and hold about two thirds of the blood, returned by the muscle and respiratory pumps.

3. **Where arteries anastomose a block is survivable; where they do not, it is an infarct.** Networks around joints, the palmar arches, the marginal artery of the colon and the circle of Willis give a second route. End arteries, with no effective anastomosis, supply the retina, renal segments, spleen, the penetrating arteries of the brain and, in practice, the coronaries, and occlusion kills a wedge bounded by that artery's territory. Collaterals enlarge over weeks when a vessel narrows slowly, so a gradual coronary stenosis may be silent while the same vessel blocked suddenly kills its territory.

4. **The border between two territories fails first when pressure falls.** A watershed zone is fed by the distal ends of two arterial trees at the lowest pressure either can supply. When output or pressure falls these zones infarct before either territory: the cortex between anterior and middle cerebral arteries after cardiac arrest, the splenic flexure between superior and inferior mesenteric arteries in shock, the mid-thoracic cord during aortic surgery. A watershed infarct therefore says "low flow", not "blocked vessel".

5. **Venous drainage has its own map, and pressure in one system shows at the junctions with the other.** Portal and systemic veins meet at the lower oesophagus, umbilicus, rectum and retroperitoneum; when cirrhosis raises portal pressure, blood takes these routes and the thin veins there dilate into varices that bleed because they carry arterial-scale pressure. The valveless vertebral venous plexus connects pelvic and thoracic veins to spine and skull, so prostate and breast cancer reach the vertebrae by venous spread; the deep calf veins, where flow is slowest, are where thrombi form in the immobile.

6. **Lymphatics return the filtrate through checkpoints.** Capillaries filter on the order of 8 L of plasma a day and reabsorb most; the remaining 2–4 L, with every leaked protein and every cell or microbe in the tissue, is collected by blind-ended lymphatics, moved by muscle compression and valves through chains of nodes, and returned nearly all through the thoracic duct into the left subclavian vein. Each tissue drains to a predictable first node, so a tender node in infection and the sentinel node in cancer point at the source: groin for leg and perineum, axilla for arm and breast, pre-aortic for gut. Block the drain and protein-rich lymphoedema accumulates that does not pit and does not respond to diuretics; tear the thoracic duct and lymph fills the pleura.

7. **Vessels run in protected, predictable places, and that is used.** Arteries travel with veins and nerves in bundles on the flexor side of limbs, where flexion shortens rather than stretches them, and in the septa between fascial compartments. A pulse is palpable where an artery crosses bone near the skin (radial, brachial, carotid, femoral, popliteal, dorsalis pedis), which is also where it is compressed to stop bleeding and punctured for access. The endothelium is an organ in its own right, secreting nitric oxide, keeping the surface anticoagulant and controlling which white cells leave.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Series / parallel circuits | Pulmonary and systemic circuits in series; systemic organs in parallel at one arterial pressure |
| Portal system | Two capillary beds in series on one venous path (hepatic, hypophyseal) |
| Resistance vessel | Arterioles, where most of the pressure drop occurs and flow distribution is set |
| Capacitance vessel | Veins, holding most of the blood volume at low pressure |
| Fenestrated / sinusoidal endothelium | Capillary walls with pores or gaps allowing bulk filtration or passage of cells |
| Anastomosis / end artery | Alternative route of supply versus a territory with none, which infarcts on occlusion |
| Watershed zone | Border between two arterial territories, perfused at the lowest pressure, first to fail |
| Portosystemic anastomosis | Junction of portal and systemic veins; site of varices in portal hypertension |
| Sentinel node | First lymph node draining a tissue |
| Thoracic duct | Main lymph trunk, returning lymph from all but the right upper quadrant to the left subclavian vein |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Arteries carry oxygenated blood."** They carry blood away from the heart; pulmonary and umbilical arteries carry deoxygenated blood. Naming follows direction.
- **"Lymphoedema is swelling, so a diuretic will help."** The fluid is protein-rich and trapped by a blocked drain; it needs compression and drainage, not diuresis.
- **"A blocked artery always causes an infarct."** Only if the territory has no collateral or the block was too fast for collaterals to enlarge; a watershed infarct happens with no artery blocked at all.
