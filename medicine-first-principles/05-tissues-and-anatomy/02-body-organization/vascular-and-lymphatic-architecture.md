## Essence

The circulation is a closed loop of two pumps and two circuits in series, with the systemic organs arranged in parallel between the aorta and the venae cavae. That layout, and the structure of each vessel's wall, decides how pressure is distributed, how flow is shared between organs, what happens when one vessel blocks, and where a tumour or an infection will travel. The lymphatic system is the overflow drain of the same circuit, returning what the capillaries filter and could not reabsorb, and its nodes sit in series along fixed routes so that whatever leaves a tissue is inspected on its way back to the blood. The one idea is that **the topology of the vessels is the topology of disease**: a territory with one supply infarcts, a territory between two supplies fails first when pressure falls, and two beds in series share each other's pressure problems.

## Core tenets

1. **Two circuits in series, organs in parallel.** All the output of the right ventricle crosses the lungs and all of the left ventricle crosses the systemic organs, so the two outputs must match beat for beat and a left heart that fails backs blood up into the lungs. Within the systemic circuit, organs lie in parallel, each fed from the same arterial pressure and drawing a share of flow set by its own resistance; a vasodilated gut after a meal or exercising muscle takes more without starving the brain, as long as total output can rise. The exceptions are the portal systems, where two capillary beds sit in series: all the venous blood from the gut and spleen passes through the liver sinusoids before reaching the heart, and blood from the hypothalamus passes a second capillary bed in the pituitary. A portal system gives the second organ first use of what the first absorbs or secretes, and makes it hostage to the resistance of the first.

2. **Each vessel's wall is built for its pressure and its job.** Arteries near the heart have a media rich in elastin that stretches in systole and recoils in diastole, converting pulsatile output into steadier flow; distal arteries have a media of smooth muscle that distributes flow. Arterioles, with a thick muscular wall around a narrow lumen, are the resistance vessels where mean pressure falls from around 90 mmHg to about 35 mmHg and where the body controls both total resistance and the share each organ gets; because resistance varies with the fourth power of the radius, a small change in arteriolar calibre is a large change in flow. Capillaries are one endothelial cell thick with a combined surface area of several hundred square metres, and their endothelium comes in three permeabilities: continuous (muscle, brain, where the tight junctions make the blood–brain barrier), fenestrated (gut, endocrine glands, renal glomerulus, where bulk filtration is the point) and discontinuous sinusoids (liver, spleen, marrow, where whole cells and proteins must pass). Veins have thin walls, large lumens and valves; they hold about two thirds of the blood volume and return it with help from the muscle and respiratory pumps, which is why standing still pools blood and walking does not.

3. **Where arteries anastomose, a block is survivable; where they do not, it is an infarct.** Arterial networks around joints, the palmar and plantar arches, the marginal artery of the colon and the circle of Willis let flow reach a territory by a second route. End arteries, with no effective anastomosis, supply the retina, the renal segments, the spleen, the penetrating arteries of the brain and, in practice, the coronary arteries, and occlusion of one produces a wedge of dead tissue bounded by that artery's territory. Collaterals enlarge over weeks when a vessel narrows slowly, so a gradual coronary or iliac stenosis may be silent while the same vessel blocked suddenly kills its territory.

4. **The border between two territories is the first place to fail when pressure falls.** A watershed zone receives blood from the distal ends of two arterial trees, at the lowest pressure either can supply. When cardiac output or blood pressure falls, these zones infarct before either territory does: the strip of cortex between the anterior and middle cerebral arteries after cardiac arrest, the splenic flexure of the colon between the superior and inferior mesenteric arteries in shock (ischaemic colitis), the mid-thoracic spinal cord between its cervical and lumbar feeders during aortic surgery. A watershed infarct therefore says "low flow", not "blocked vessel".

5. **Venous drainage has its own map, and pressure in one system shows up at the junctions with the other.** The portal and systemic venous systems meet at the lower oesophagus, the umbilicus, the rectum and the retroperitoneum. When cirrhosis raises portal pressure, blood takes these routes and the thin-walled veins at the junctions dilate into oesophageal varices, a caput medusae and rectal varices; the varices bleed because they are veins carrying arterial-scale pressures. The vertebral venous plexus has no valves and connects pelvic, abdominal and thoracic veins to the spine and skull, so prostate and breast cancer reach the vertebrae and brain by venous spread; the deep veins of the calf, where flow is slowest and valves pocket the blood, are where thrombi form in the immobile.

6. **Lymphatics return the filtrate the capillaries lose, and they return it through checkpoints.** Capillaries filter on the order of 8 L of plasma a day and reabsorb most of it; the remaining 2–4 L, together with every protein that leaked and every cell or microbe that entered the tissue, is collected by blind-ended lymphatic capillaries, moved by muscle compression and valves through chains of lymph nodes, and returned to the venous system at the root of the neck, nearly all of it through the thoracic duct into the left subclavian vein. Each tissue drains to a predictable first node, so the enlarged, tender node in an infection and the sentinel node in a cancer point back at the source; the groin nodes drain the leg, the perineum and the skin of the lower trunk, the axillary nodes drain the arm and most of the breast, and the gut drains along its arteries to nodes in front of the aorta. Block the drain (surgery, radiotherapy, filarial worms, tumour) and protein-rich fluid accumulates as lymphoedema that does not pit and does not respond to diuretics; tear the thoracic duct and lymph fills the pleural cavity.

7. **Vessels run in protected, predictable places, and that predictability is used.** Arteries travel with their veins and nerves in neurovascular bundles along the flexor side of limbs, where flexion shortens rather than stretches them, and in the septa between fascial compartments. A pulse is palpable wherever an artery crosses bone close to the skin (radial, brachial, carotid, femoral, popliteal, dorsalis pedis, posterior tibial), which is where it is also compressed to stop bleeding and punctured for access. Deep veins accompany arteries and share their names; superficial veins run in the subcutaneous fat and are where cannulas go. The large vessels themselves have their own supply (the vasa vasorum) and an endothelium that is an organ in its own right, secreting nitric oxide, keeping the surface anticoagulant and controlling which white cells leave.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Series / parallel circuits | Pulmonary and systemic circuits in series; systemic organs in parallel, sharing one arterial pressure |
| Portal system | Two capillary beds in series on one venous path (hepatic portal, hypophyseal portal) |
| Resistance vessel | Arterioles, where most of the pressure drop occurs and flow distribution is controlled |
| Capacitance vessel | Veins, which hold most of the blood volume at low pressure |
| Fenestrated / sinusoidal endothelium | Capillary walls with pores or gaps, allowing bulk filtration or passage of cells |
| Anastomosis | Connection between two arteries allowing an alternative route of supply |
| End artery | Artery whose territory has no effective alternative supply; occlusion causes infarction |
| Watershed zone | Border between two arterial territories, perfused at the lowest pressure, first to fail when flow falls |
| Portosystemic anastomosis | Site where portal and systemic veins connect; site of varices in portal hypertension |
| Lymph node | Filter in series on a lymphatic route; the first node draining a tissue is its sentinel node |
| Thoracic duct | Main lymphatic trunk returning lymph from the whole body except the right upper quadrant to the left subclavian vein |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Arteries carry oxygenated blood."** They carry blood away from the heart; the pulmonary arteries and the umbilical arteries carry deoxygenated blood. Naming follows direction, not content.
- **"Lymphoedema is just swelling, so a diuretic will help."** The fluid is protein-rich and trapped by a blocked drain; removing water from the plasma does not remove it, and the swelling needs compression and drainage, not diuresis.
- **"A blocked artery always causes an infarct."** It causes one only if the territory has no collateral supply or the block was too fast for collaterals to enlarge; a watershed infarct, conversely, happens with no artery blocked at all.
