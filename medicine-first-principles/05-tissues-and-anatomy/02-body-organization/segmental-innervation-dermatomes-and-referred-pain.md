## Essence

The human body is built in segments, and the nervous system keeps the segmental plan long after the body has hidden it. Each of the 31 pairs of spinal nerves grew out to a block of embryonic tissue (a somite) that formed one strip of skin, one set of muscles and one slice of vertebra, and the nerve follows those tissues wherever they migrate. The result is a fixed map: every spinal segment owns a dermatome, a myotome and a set of viscera. The map is why a single damaged nerve root produces a predictable band of numbness, why a tendon reflex tests one level of the spinal cord, and why pain from the heart is felt in the arm and pain from the diaphragm in the shoulder. Referred pain is not an error; it is the map being read by a brain that learnt the skin before it learnt the organs.

## Core tenets

1. **Segmentation is embryological and permanent.** The paraxial mesoderm forms paired somites along the neural tube, each splitting into a dermatome (dermis), a myotome (skeletal muscle) and a sclerotome (vertebra and rib), and each receives the spinal nerve of its level, named C1–C8, T1–T12, L1–L5, S1–S5 and Co1. Muscles that migrate far (the diaphragm from cervical somites to the bottom of the thorax, the latissimus dorsi from the arm bud to the pelvis) drag their nerve with them, so the phrenic nerve arises from C3–C5 and runs the length of the chest. Wherever a tissue is, the segment that innervates it tells you where it came from.

2. **A dermatome is the skin of one root, and the bands are drawn by development.** On the trunk the dermatomes are horizontal strips: T4 crosses the nipple, T10 the umbilicus, L1 the groin. On the limbs they are stretched and twisted by the outgrowth and rotation of the limb buds, so C6 reaches the thumb, C7 the middle finger and C8 the little finger; L4 runs down the medial leg to the medial ankle, L5 across the dorsum of the foot to the great toe, and S1 along the lateral foot and sole; S2–S4 supply the perineum. Adjacent dermatomes overlap, so cutting one root blurs sensation rather than abolishing it, and complete anaesthesia of a strip needs about three roots. Reactivation of varicella-zoster virus in one dorsal root ganglion (shingles) paints its dermatome exactly, which is the most vivid demonstration that the map is real.

3. **A root pattern is not a nerve pattern, and telling them apart is the first task of localisation.** Spinal roots are reshuffled in the brachial and lumbosacral plexuses so that each peripheral nerve carries fibres from several roots and each root contributes to several nerves. A lesion of the C8 root numbs the little finger and the medial forearm and weakens the small hand muscles; a lesion of the ulnar nerve numbs the little finger but spares the medial forearm (which belongs to a different nerve of the same root) and weakens the same hand muscles while also affecting the ulnar forearm flexors. The sensory loss of a root lesion follows the dermatome; that of a nerve lesion follows the nerve's own territory. Knowing both maps is what lets a clinician say "root", "plexus" or "nerve" from an examination.

4. **Myotomes and reflexes let the examiner read the spinal cord level by level.** Each movement is driven mainly by one or two segments: shoulder abduction C5, elbow flexion C5–C6, elbow extension C7, finger flexion C8, finger abduction T1, hip flexion L2–L3, knee extension L3–L4, ankle dorsiflexion L4–L5, great toe extension L5, ankle plantarflexion S1–S2. The tendon reflexes are the same map with the cord included in the loop: biceps C5–C6, triceps C7, knee L3–L4, ankle S1. A reflex that is lost marks a break in that segment's loop (root, cord grey matter or nerve); reflexes that are brisk below a level with weakness mark damage to the descending pathways above it. The pattern of weak muscles, lost reflexes and the sensory band together give the cord segment.

5. **The autonomic outflow is segmental too, and it carries the pain fibres of the viscera.** Sympathetic neurons leave the cord only between T1 and L2, run to the sympathetic chain and then onward to every organ; parasympathetic fibres leave with cranial nerves III, VII, IX and X and from S2–S4. Visceral pain afferents travel back along the sympathetic routes to the T1–L2 segments and, for the pelvic organs, along the parasympathetic routes to S2–S4. Each organ therefore reports to a known set of cord segments: the heart to T1–T4, the foregut (stomach, liver, biliary tree, pancreas) to about T5–T9, the midgut (small bowel, appendix, right colon) to T10–T11, the hindgut to T12–L2, the kidney and ureter to T10–L1, the bladder and uterus partly to T11–L2 and partly to S2–S4.

6. **Referred pain is convergence read by a brain trained on skin.** Visceral afferents are sparse and end on the same dorsal-horn neurons as the far more numerous somatic afferents from the dermatomes of the same segments. The cortex has learnt that activity in those neurons means skin, so it places visceral pain in the dermatome: cardiac ischaemia in the left chest, inner arm and jaw (T1–T4 and the cervical segments of the vagus), gallbladder disease at the right scapula, renal colic from the loin to the groin along T10–L1. The diaphragm is the clearest case: its central part is supplied by C3–C5, so blood or pus under it, or a ruptured ectopic pregnancy, hurts at the tip of the shoulder (C4) and not in the abdomen at all. Appendicitis tells the whole story in sequence: the inflamed appendix first gives dull periumbilical pain (midgut, T10), and when the inflammation reaches the parietal peritoneum, which has somatic innervation, the pain moves to a sharp, localised point in the right iliac fossa. The migration is the inflammation crossing from visceral to somatic territory.

7. **The segment and the vertebra are not at the same level.** The spinal cord stops growing before the vertebral column, so in adults it ends at the first or second lumbar vertebra and the roots below descend inside the canal as the cauda equina before leaving at their own vertebral level. A cervical cord segment lies about one vertebra above its number, upper thoracic segments two above, lower thoracic about three, and the entire lumbar and sacral cord sits behind vertebrae T11–L1. Two consequences follow. A patient with a sensory level at the umbilicus (T10) has a cord lesion opposite about the T7–T8 vertebra, so imaging must be aimed there and not at T10. And a lumbar puncture below L2 enters a canal that contains only roots floating in cerebrospinal fluid, which move aside from the needle.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Somite | Block of paraxial mesoderm that forms one segment's dermis, muscle and vertebra |
| Spinal nerve | Mixed nerve formed from one dorsal (sensory) and one ventral (motor) root; 31 pairs |
| Dermatome | Strip of skin whose sensory fibres enter the cord through one dorsal root |
| Myotome | Muscles whose motor fibres leave the cord through one ventral root |
| Plexus | Network (brachial, lumbosacral) in which roots are redistributed into peripheral nerves |
| Radiculopathy / neuropathy | Lesion of a root versus lesion of a peripheral nerve; distinguished by the pattern of loss |
| Sensory level | The dermatome below which sensation is lost in a cord lesion |
| Thoracolumbar / craniosacral outflow | Sympathetic origin in T1–L2 versus parasympathetic origin in cranial nerves and S2–S4 |
| Visceral afferent | Sensory fibre from an organ, travelling with autonomic nerves to the cord |
| Convergence | Visceral and somatic afferents ending on the same dorsal-horn neurons; the mechanism of referred pain |
| Cauda equina | The lumbar and sacral roots descending below the end of the cord |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Referred pain means the patient is mistaken about where it hurts."** The pain is genuinely felt in the dermatome; the nervous system has no separate channel for the organ. Shoulder-tip pain after abdominal bleeding is a sign to be sought, not a misreport.
- **"Numbness in one finger means a nerve was cut."** The thumb, middle and little fingers each belong to a root and to a nerve; the forearm and hand muscles involved decide which, and a cervical disc is a commoner cause of a numb thumb than a cut nerve.
- **"The vertebra tells you the cord segment."** Below the neck the cord segment lies one to three vertebrae above the bone of the same number, and below L2 there is no cord at all.
