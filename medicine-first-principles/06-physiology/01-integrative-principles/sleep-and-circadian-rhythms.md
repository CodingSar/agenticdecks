## Essence

The body keeps time. A master clock in the hypothalamus, built from a transcriptional feedback loop with a period of about 24 hours and reset daily by light, schedules the set points of nearly every other loop: core temperature, cortisol, blood pressure, melatonin, appetite, kidney function and the readiness to sleep. Sleep itself is a distinct, regulated brain state, not an absence of activity, entered when a homeostatic pressure that accumulates during waking coincides with the circadian signal for night, and cycling every ~90 minutes between slow-wave and rapid-eye-movement sleep. Its functions, memory consolidation, synaptic renormalisation, metabolic and immune maintenance, and clearance of brain interstitial waste, are revealed by what fails when it is lost.

## Core tenets

1. **The circadian clock is a molecular negative feedback loop with a built-in delay, and that is why it oscillates.** In every cell the transcription factors CLOCK and BMAL1 drive expression of the *Period* and *Cryptochrome* genes; their proteins accumulate, enter the nucleus and inhibit CLOCK/BMAL1, then are degraded, and the cycle restarts. Transcription, translation, nuclear entry and degradation add up to a delay of ~24 hours, which [oscillation theory](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/03-systems-and-models/nonlinear-dynamics-oscillations-and-networks.md) predicts will ring at that period. The intrinsic human period is slightly over 24 hours (~24.2 h), so it must be reset every day.

2. **The suprachiasmatic nucleus is the master clock, and light through the eye is its main resetting signal.** The ~20,000 neurons of the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) in the hypothalamus synchronise each other and the peripheral clocks of liver, muscle, kidney and gut. They receive light not from rods and cones but from intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells containing melanopsin, most sensitive to blue light, via the retinohypothalamic tract. Light in the evening delays the clock, light in the morning advances it; a totally blind person with no melanopsin input free-runs on a ~24.2 h cycle. Meal timing resets peripheral clocks and can desynchronise them from the SCN, as in shift work.

3. **The clock schedules set points across the day, so "normal" has a time of day.** Core temperature is lowest at ~04:00–05:00 and ~1 °C higher in the early evening. Cortisol peaks just before waking and is lowest around midnight. Melatonin from the pineal gland rises at dusk under SCN control and is suppressed by light. Blood pressure dips ~10–20% in sleep and surges on waking, which is when myocardial infarction and stroke cluster. Growth hormone is released in early slow-wave sleep. Drug effects and laboratory values vary by time of day for the same reason.

4. **Sleep timing is set by two processes: a homeostatic pressure and the circadian signal.** In Borbély's two-process model, Process S, sleep pressure, builds during waking (adenosine accumulating in the basal forebrain is one substrate; caffeine blocks its receptors) and discharges during slow-wave sleep. Process C, the circadian alerting signal, rises through the day and falls in the evening. Sleep comes when S is high and C is low; the mid-afternoon dip and the evening "second wind" are the two curves crossing at different angles. Jet lag and shift work are misalignment of C with the sleep opportunity.

5. **Sleep is cyclic, and its two main states are physiologically opposite.** Non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep deepens from stage N1 through N2 (sleep spindles, K-complexes) to N3 slow-wave sleep (high-amplitude delta waves, synchronised cortical firing, lowest heart rate, pressure and metabolic rate). Rapid eye movement (REM) sleep has a desynchronised, waking-like electroencephalogram, dreaming, skeletal muscle atonia (via brainstem glycinergic inhibition of motor neurons), and unstable autonomic control: breathing and heart rate irregular, thermoregulation largely suspended. Cycles last ~90 min; slow-wave sleep dominates the first half of the night, REM the second.

6. **Wake and sleep are stabilised by a flip-flop switch, so the brain is rarely half-asleep.** Arousal is driven by brainstem and hypothalamic nuclei releasing noradrenaline (locus coeruleus), serotonin (raphe), histamine (tuberomammillary nucleus), acetylcholine and dopamine, projecting to thalamus and cortex. Sleep-active neurons in the ventrolateral preoptic area release γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and inhibit them; the arousal nuclei inhibit the preoptic area in turn. Mutual inhibition gives two stable states and fast transitions. Orexin (hypocretin) neurons in the lateral hypothalamus bias the switch toward waking; their loss causes narcolepsy, in which sleep and REM atonia intrude into waking. Most hypnotics act by potentiating GABA.

7. **Sleep changes the body's loops, and some diseases appear only then.** Chemoreceptor sensitivity to carbon dioxide falls and arterial carbon dioxide rises 3–5 mmHg; upper airway muscle tone falls, so a narrow pharynx collapses (obstructive sleep apnoea), and a weak diaphragm first fails in REM, when accessory muscles are paralysed ([control of breathing](../04-respiratory-system/control-of-breathing.md)). Vagal tone rises and sympathetic tone falls in NREM; REM brings sympathetic surges. Epileptic seizures, asthma and cluster headache each have characteristic sleep timing.

8. **Sleep loss is a systemic insult, not only a cognitive one.** A night without sleep impairs attention and reaction time as much as a blood alcohol level at the legal driving limit, and the deficit accumulates across nights of partial restriction while subjective sleepiness plateaus. Restriction to 4–5 hours for a week raises evening cortisol, lowers glucose tolerance toward a pre-diabetic range, lowers leptin and raises ghrelin (increasing appetite), raises blood pressure and blunts antibody responses to vaccination. Slow-wave sleep consolidates declarative memory and renormalises synapses strengthened by the day's learning; REM sleep is linked to procedural and emotional memory.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Circadian rhythm | An endogenous ~24 h oscillation that persists without external cues |
| Zeitgeber | An external signal that entrains the clock: light above all, then meals and activity |
| Suprachiasmatic nucleus | Hypothalamic master clock, light-entrained through melanopsin ganglion cells |
| Melatonin | Pineal hormone marking biological night; a signal of darkness, not a hypnotic of great power |
| Process S / Process C | Homeostatic sleep pressure / circadian alerting signal (Borbély) |
| Slow-wave sleep (N3) | Deepest NREM stage: delta waves, lowest metabolic rate, memory consolidation |
| REM sleep | Active brain, paralysed body, unstable autonomic control, dreaming |
| Sleep spindle | 11–16 Hz burst of N2 sleep generated in the thalamus; linked to memory consolidation |
| Orexin (hypocretin) | Lateral hypothalamic peptide that stabilises waking; lost in narcolepsy |
| Chronotype | An individual's habitual phase ("lark" or "owl"), partly genetic, shifting later in adolescence |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Sleep is the brain switched off."** Cortical activity in REM resembles waking, slow-wave sleep is highly organised, and the sleeping brain does work (consolidation, clearance) that cannot be done awake.
- **"You can adapt to short sleep."** Performance keeps falling across nights of restriction while the sense of sleepiness levels off; people adapt to feeling tired, not to being impaired.
- **"Melatonin is a sleeping pill."** It is a darkness signal that shifts the clock; its direct hypnotic effect is modest, and timing matters more than dose.
