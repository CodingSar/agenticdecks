## Essence

The body keeps time. A master clock in the hypothalamus, built from a transcriptional feedback loop with a period of about 24 hours and reset daily by light, schedules the set points of nearly every other loop: temperature, cortisol, blood pressure, melatonin, appetite and the readiness to sleep. Sleep is a distinct, regulated brain state, entered when a homeostatic pressure that builds during waking coincides with the circadian signal for night, and cycling every ~90 minutes between slow-wave and rapid-eye-movement sleep. Its functions, memory consolidation, synaptic renormalisation and metabolic and immune maintenance, are revealed by what fails when it is lost.

## Core tenets

1. **The circadian clock is a molecular negative feedback loop with a built-in delay, and that is why it oscillates.** In every cell the transcription factors CLOCK and BMAL1 drive the *Period* and *Cryptochrome* genes; their proteins accumulate, enter the nucleus and inhibit CLOCK/BMAL1, are degraded, and the cycle restarts. The steps add up to a delay of ~24 hours, at which [oscillation theory](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/03-systems-and-models/nonlinear-dynamics-oscillations-and-networks.md) predicts the loop will ring. The intrinsic human period is ~24.2 h, so it must be reset daily.

2. **The suprachiasmatic nucleus is the master clock, and light through the eye is its main resetting signal.** The ~20,000 neurons of the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) synchronise each other and the peripheral clocks of liver, muscle, kidney and gut. They receive light from melanopsin-containing retinal ganglion cells, most sensitive to blue, via the retinohypothalamic tract. Evening light delays the clock, morning light advances it; a person with no melanopsin input free-runs at ~24.2 h. Meal timing resets peripheral clocks and can desynchronise them from the SCN, as in shift work.

3. **The clock schedules set points, so "normal" has a time of day.** Core temperature is lowest at ~04:00–05:00 and ~1 °C higher in the early evening; cortisol peaks before waking; melatonin rises at dusk and is suppressed by light; blood pressure dips ~10–20% in sleep and surges on waking, when myocardial infarction and stroke cluster; growth hormone is released in early slow-wave sleep.

4. **Sleep timing is set by two processes: a homeostatic pressure and the circadian signal.** In Borbély's two-process model, Process S, sleep pressure, builds during waking (adenosine accumulating in the basal forebrain is one substrate; caffeine blocks its receptors) and discharges in slow-wave sleep. Process C, the circadian alerting signal, rises through the day and falls in the evening. Sleep comes when S is high and C low; the afternoon dip and the evening "second wind" are the two curves crossing. Jet lag and shift work are misalignment of C with the sleep opportunity.

5. **Sleep is cyclic, and its two main states are physiologically opposite.** Non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep deepens from N1 through N2 (sleep spindles) to N3 slow-wave sleep (high-amplitude delta waves, synchronised cortical firing, lowest heart rate and metabolic rate). Rapid eye movement (REM) sleep has a waking-like electroencephalogram, dreaming, skeletal muscle atonia (brainstem glycinergic inhibition of motor neurons) and unstable autonomic control, with thermoregulation largely suspended. Cycles last ~90 min; slow-wave sleep dominates the first half of the night, REM the second.

6. **Wake and sleep are stabilised by a flip-flop switch, so the brain is rarely half-asleep.** Arousal is driven by brainstem and hypothalamic nuclei releasing noradrenaline (locus coeruleus), serotonin (raphe), histamine, acetylcholine and dopamine to thalamus and cortex. Sleep-active neurons in the ventrolateral preoptic area release γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and inhibit them; the arousal nuclei inhibit the preoptic area in turn, so mutual inhibition gives two stable states and fast transitions. Orexin (hypocretin) neurons bias the switch toward waking; their loss causes narcolepsy, in which sleep and REM atonia intrude into waking. Most hypnotics potentiate GABA.

7. **Sleep changes the body's loops, and some diseases appear only then.** Chemosensitivity to carbon dioxide falls and arterial carbon dioxide rises 3–5 mmHg; upper airway tone falls, so a narrow pharynx collapses (obstructive sleep apnoea), and a weak diaphragm first fails in REM, when accessory muscles are paralysed ([control of breathing](../04-respiratory-system/control-of-breathing.md)). Vagal tone rises in NREM; REM brings sympathetic surges. Seizures, asthma and cluster headache each have characteristic sleep timing.

8. **Sleep loss is a systemic insult, not only a cognitive one.** A night without sleep impairs attention as much as a blood alcohol level at the legal driving limit, and the deficit accumulates across nights of restriction while subjective sleepiness plateaus. A week at 4–5 hours raises evening cortisol, lowers glucose tolerance, lowers leptin and raises ghrelin (increasing appetite), raises blood pressure and blunts vaccine responses. Slow-wave sleep consolidates declarative memory and renormalises synapses; REM sleep is linked to procedural and emotional memory.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Circadian rhythm | An endogenous ~24 h oscillation that persists without external cues |
| Suprachiasmatic nucleus | Hypothalamic master clock, light-entrained through melanopsin ganglion cells |
| Melatonin | Pineal hormone marking biological night; a darkness signal more than a hypnotic |
| Process S / Process C | Homeostatic sleep pressure / circadian alerting signal (Borbély) |
| Slow-wave sleep (N3) | Deepest NREM stage: delta waves, lowest metabolic rate, memory consolidation |
| REM sleep | Active brain, paralysed body, unstable autonomic control, dreaming |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Sleep is the brain switched off."** Cortical activity in REM resembles waking, slow-wave sleep is highly organised, and the sleeping brain does work (consolidation, clearance) that cannot be done awake.
- **"You can adapt to short sleep."** Performance keeps falling across nights of restriction while the sense of sleepiness levels off; people adapt to feeling tired, not to being impaired.
