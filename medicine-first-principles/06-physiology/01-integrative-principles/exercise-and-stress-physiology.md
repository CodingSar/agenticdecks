## Essence

Exercise raises whole-body oxygen consumption from ~250 mL/min at rest to 3–5 L/min, a twelve- to twentyfold increase that no single organ can supply alone. The heart pumps four to five times more, ventilation rises twenty times, blood is redirected from gut and kidney to muscle, and muscle extracts three times more oxygen from each litre. Exercise is therefore the best test of whether the circulation, lungs, blood and muscle have been understood as one supply chain, and the Fick principle (oxygen uptake = cardiac output × arteriovenous oxygen difference) is its accounting identity. The stress response is the same mobilisation triggered by threat rather than work: the sympatho-adrenal system in seconds and the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis in minutes to hours move fuel and blood toward survival, at a cost that accumulates when the response is chronic.

## Core tenets

1. **Oxygen uptake is cardiac output times extraction, and both terms scale.** At rest: 5 L/min × (200 − 150 mL/L) = 250 mL/min. At maximal exercise in an untrained adult: ~20 L/min × (200 − 50 mL/L) ≈ 3 L/min (~40 mL/kg/min; elite endurance athletes reach 80–90 mL/kg/min with outputs above 35 L/min). Cardiac output rises mainly through heart rate (to ~200 minus age) with stroke volume rising ~1.5-fold; extraction rises because muscle blood flow is diverted from tissues that extract little.

2. **Muscle blood flow is set locally and overrides the sympathetic nervous system in working muscle.** Metabolites of work (adenosine, potassium, hydrogen ions, carbon dioxide, inorganic phosphate) and nitric oxide dilate arterioles in proportion to metabolic rate, and muscle flow rises from ~1 L/min to 15–20 L/min, from ~20% of output to ~85%. The same sympathetic discharge constricts splanchnic, renal and inactive-muscle beds; in the working muscle it is overridden (functional sympatholysis). Total peripheral resistance therefore *falls* in dynamic exercise, so systolic pressure rises with output while diastolic pressure barely changes. Isometric exercise, which compresses vessels and raises resistance, raises both.

3. **Ventilation tracks carbon dioxide production so well that arterial gases stay normal in health.** Minute ventilation rises linearly with oxygen uptake to ~100–150 L/min, driven by feedforward from the motor cortex and muscle afferents as much as by chemoreceptor feedback, which is why it jumps at the first step. Above the lactate threshold (~50–70% of maximum), buffering of lactic acid releases extra carbon dioxide and ventilation rises out of proportion; this ventilatory threshold is measurable and marks the shift to unsustainable work. The lungs are not the limit in health; the pump is.

4. **Three energy systems hand over in sequence, by time.** Phosphocreatine regenerates ATP for the first ~10 s. Anaerobic glycolysis supplies the next ~1–2 min at the price of lactate and hydrogen ions. Oxidative phosphorylation, of glycogen and then increasingly of fat, supplies everything longer, limited by oxygen delivery and mitochondrial density ([oxidative phosphorylation](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/02-metabolism/glycolysis-tca-cycle-and-oxidative-phosphorylation.md)). Lactate is a fuel shuttled to heart, liver and other muscle, not a waste; the hydrogen ion that accompanies it is what limits glycolytic work.

5. **The limit of maximal aerobic capacity is central (delivery), and the limit of endurance is peripheral (fuel).** Maximal oxygen uptake rises with training mainly because stroke volume and blood volume rise; it falls with anaemia, heart failure and lung disease in proportion to delivery. Endurance at a given fraction of maximum depends on glycogen stores and the muscle's capacity to oxidise fat, which rise with training (more mitochondria, more capillaries per fibre). A cardiopulmonary exercise test separates the two by watching which system reaches its ceiling first.

6. **Exercise is a controlled disturbance of every homeostatic loop, and recovery is their relaxation.** Core temperature rises 1–2 °C and is held there by sweating; plasma volume falls 10–15% into the interstitium and sweat; potassium leaves contracting muscle; pH falls; glucose is held by glucagon and catecholamines against a tenfold rise in muscle uptake. Oxygen uptake stays raised after exercise stops (the "oxygen debt") while phosphocreatine, lactate, temperature and hormones return to baseline.

7. **Training is adaptation to repeated load, and it reverses with disuse.** Weeks of endurance work enlarge stroke volume and plasma volume, lower resting heart rate (through higher vagal tone), and raise capillary and mitochondrial density; resistance work enlarges fibres. The same tissues shrink within weeks of bed rest, which is why immobility is itself a cause of weakness, orthostatic intolerance and insulin resistance.

8. **The stress response is exercise physiology without the exercise, in two waves.** Threat activates the sympatho-adrenal system within seconds: noradrenaline from nerves and adrenaline from the adrenal medulla raise heart rate, contractility and blood pressure, redirect flow, dilate airways and pupils, and mobilise glucose and fatty acids. Within minutes the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis (corticotropin-releasing hormone → adrenocorticotropic hormone → cortisol) sustains the mobilisation, raises glucose, and suppresses digestion, reproduction, growth and, over time, immunity. In acute illness this is adaptive; chronic activation (allostatic load) raises blood pressure, glucose and visceral fat, and explains part of the link between social stress and cardiovascular disease.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fick principle | Oxygen uptake = cardiac output × (arterial − venous oxygen content) |
| Maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max) | The ceiling of aerobic metabolism, ~35–45 mL/kg/min untrained |
| Metabolic equivalent (MET) | Resting oxygen uptake, ~3.5 mL/kg/min; activities are rated in multiples |
| Functional sympatholysis | Local metabolites overriding sympathetic vasoconstriction in working muscle |
| Lactate (ventilatory) threshold | Intensity above which lactate accumulates and ventilation rises disproportionately |
| Phosphocreatine | Immediate ATP buffer, exhausted within ~10 s of maximal effort |
| Oxygen debt | Elevated oxygen uptake after exercise while stores and temperature are restored |
| Sympatho-adrenal response | Seconds-fast mobilisation by noradrenaline and adrenaline |
| Hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis | Minutes-to-hours response ending in cortisol |
| Allostatic load | Cumulative cost of a chronically activated stress response |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Lactic acid causes the burn and the fatigue."** Lactate itself is a fuel; the hydrogen ions, inorganic phosphate and potassium shifts of heavy work are the proximate causes of fatigue, and the next-day soreness is muscle damage, not lactate.
- **"Blood pressure goes up in exercise because resistance goes up."** Resistance falls; pressure rises because output rises more than resistance falls.
- **"Stress hormones are bad."** The acute response is what keeps a bleeding patient conscious; it is the chronic, unresolved response that does harm.
