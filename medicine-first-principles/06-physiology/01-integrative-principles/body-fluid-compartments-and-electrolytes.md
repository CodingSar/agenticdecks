## Essence

About 60% of an adult's mass is water, divided by two barriers into three compartments: the cell membrane separates intracellular from extracellular fluid, and the capillary wall splits the extracellular fluid into interstitium and plasma. Water crosses both barriers freely and distributes itself so that osmolality is the same everywhere; small ions cross the capillary wall but are pumped across cell membranes; proteins stay mostly in plasma. Three consequences explain most of fluid medicine: total body sodium sets the volume of the extracellular fluid, total body water relative to solute sets the osmolality (and so the plasma sodium concentration), and plasma proteins hold fluid in the vessels.

## Core tenets

1. **The compartments have fixed proportions.** In a 70 kg adult: total body water ~42 L (60%); intracellular ~28 L (two-thirds); extracellular ~14 L (one-third), of which interstitial ~10–11 L and plasma ~3 L (blood volume ~5 L with red cells). Fat holds little water, so the fraction is lower in women, the obese and the old, higher in infants (~75%).

   ```
   total body water 42 L
   |-- intracellular 28 L   [K+ 140, Na+ ~12, Mg2+, phosphate, protein]
   |        --- cell membrane: water crosses, ions pumped ---
   |-- extracellular 14 L   [Na+ 140, Cl- 100, HCO3- 24, K+ 4, Ca2+ 2.4]
         |-- interstitial 10.5 L   (protein low)
         |        --- capillary wall: water and ions cross, protein not ---
         |-- plasma 3 L            (protein ~70 g/L)
   ```

2. **Water distributes to equalise osmolality; solutes that cannot cross a membrane decide where it goes.** Osmolality is ~285–295 mOsm/kg in every compartment, set by sodium salts outside the cell and potassium salts inside. A solute that crosses the membrane freely (urea, ethanol) raises measured osmolality but moves no water: an ineffective osmole. Sodium, mannitol and glucose without insulin are effective osmoles. *Tonicity*, the effective osmolality, is what shrinks or swells cells: hypernatraemia dehydrates cells, a high urea does not.

3. **The sodium pump makes sodium the osmotic skeleton of the extracellular fluid.** Na+/K+-adenosine triphosphatase (Na+/K+-ATPase) exports three sodium ions for two potassium, keeping intracellular sodium ~12 mmol/L against 140 outside and potassium ~140 inside against 4 outside. Since sodium cannot accumulate inside cells, every millimole retained by the kidney holds ~7 mL of water in the extracellular space. **Total body sodium sets extracellular volume; total body water relative to sodium sets the sodium concentration.** Oedema is a sodium problem; hyponatraemia is almost always a water problem.

4. **Across the capillary wall, protein is the solute that matters, because it is the only one held back.** Small ions and glucose equilibrate between plasma and interstitium and exert no net pull across the capillary. Plasma proteins (~70 g/L, ~25 mmHg of oncotic pressure including the Gibbs–Donnan excess of ions they attract) do, and this pull against capillary hydrostatic pressure is the Starling balance of [microcirculation](../03-cardiovascular-system/microcirculation-and-capillary-exchange.md). Low albumin (liver failure, nephrotic syndrome) shifts fluid into the interstitium.

5. **An infused fluid distributes according to which barriers its solute can cross.** One litre of isotonic saline carries sodium that stays extracellular, so it spreads over ~14 L and leaves ~250 mL (plasma's quarter share) in the vessels. One litre of 5% glucose becomes free water once the glucose is metabolised, spreads over all 42 L and leaves ~80 mL in plasma: it treats water deficit, not haemorrhage. Blood or colloid stays largely in plasma for hours. Hypertonic saline pulls water out of cells, which is why it shrinks a swollen brain.

6. **Potassium's distribution, not its total, governs excitability.** About 98% of body potassium (~3,500 mmol) is intracellular; plasma holds ~60 mmol. The resting membrane potential depends on the ratio across the membrane, so a rise from 4 to 7 mmol/L (a 75% change in the ratio) depolarises cardiac muscle toward arrest while the intracellular side barely moves. Insulin, β2-adrenergic stimulation and alkalosis drive potassium into cells; acidosis, cell lysis and insulin deficiency drive it out, changing plasma potassium within minutes without any change in body content.

7. **Calcium is regulated as its free ionised fraction.** Total plasma calcium ~2.2–2.6 mmol/L; ~45% is ionised (~1.2 mmol/L), ~40% albumin-bound, the rest complexed. Alkalosis increases albumin binding and lowers ionised calcium (hyperventilation causes tetany); low albumin lowers the total while the ionised, regulated fraction is normal.

8. **The kidney is the only regulated outlet.** Intake ~2–2.5 L/day (drink, food, ~300 mL from metabolism); losses via urine (~1.5 L, variable), skin and breath (~0.7–0.9 L, fixed), faeces (~0.1 L). Sweat, vomiting and diarrhoea are unregulated losses of known composition (sweat hypotonic; gastric fluid rich in H+ and Cl−; diarrhoea rich in HCO3− and K+), so what is lost predicts the disturbance.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Osmolality | Solute particles per kilogram of water (~290 mOsm/kg); estimated as 2[Na+] + glucose + urea in mmol/L |
| Tonicity | Effective osmolality: solutes that cannot cross the cell membrane and so move water |
| Effective osmole | Sodium, glucose (without insulin), mannitol; moves water across cells |
| Ineffective osmole | Urea, ethanol; raise osmolality without moving water |
| Oncotic (colloid osmotic) pressure | Osmotic pull of plasma proteins across the capillary wall, ~25 mmHg |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Plasma sodium measures body sodium."** It measures water relative to sodium. A patient with gross oedema (sodium excess) can have a low sodium concentration (greater water excess).
- **"Dehydration and volume depletion are the same."** Dehydration is water loss (hypernatraemia, cells shrink); volume depletion is sodium-and-water loss (pressure falls). They need different fluids.
