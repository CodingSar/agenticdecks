## Essence

The heart times itself. Cells in the sinoatrial node depolarise spontaneously about a hundred times a minute; the impulse spreads across the atria, is delayed in the atrioventricular node so the atria can empty, then races down the His–Purkinje system to fire both ventricles within ~80 ms. The ventricular action potential is a neuronal one with a long calcium plateau, which keeps the muscle refractory for nearly the whole contraction and so prevents tetany and most re-entry. The electrocardiogram (ECG) is the sum of these currents seen from the body surface, and its deflections can be read back to the chamber, direction and channel involved. Arrhythmias arise from three mechanisms: abnormal automaticity, triggered activity and re-entry.

## Core tenets

1. **Pacemaker cells have no resting potential; they drift to threshold, and the autonomic nerves set the slope.** Sinoatrial (SA) node cells sit at ~−60 mV and depolarise steadily through the "funny" current of hyperpolarisation-activated cyclic-nucleotide-gated (HCN) channels, T-type calcium current and rhythmic calcium release; the upstroke is carried by L-type calcium channels, not sodium. Vagal acetylcholine (M2) opens potassium channels and reduces the funny current, flattening the slope (resting rate ~70 against an intrinsic ~100); sympathetic noradrenaline (β1, cyclic adenosine monophosphate) steepens it. Ivabradine blocks HCN and slows the heart without touching contractility.

2. **The ventricular action potential has a plateau, and the plateau is what protects the heart.** Phase 0: fast sodium influx (Nav1.5) depolarises from −90 to +30 mV in ~1 ms. Phase 2: a plateau of ~200 ms in which inward L-type calcium current (which triggers contraction) balances outward potassium current. Phase 3: delayed-rectifier potassium currents repolarise to a stable −90 mV (phase 4). Because sodium channels stay inactivated through the plateau, the cell is refractory for almost its entire contraction: no tetanus, and a wavefront cannot turn back into tissue it has just excited.

3. **Conduction has a hierarchy of speeds, and the slowest link is a safety device.** Atrial muscle conducts at ~1 m/s; the atrioventricular (AV) node at ~0.05 m/s, adding ~100 ms so atrial systole finishes first; the His–Purkinje system at 2–4 m/s so both ventricles fire together; ventricular muscle at ~0.5 m/s. The AV node also conducts *decrementally* (more slowly the faster it is stimulated), so in atrial fibrillation at 400–600 atrial impulses per minute it passes only 100–180 to the ventricles; digoxin, β-blockers and calcium-channel blockers slow it further, which is how ventricular rate is controlled.

4. **There is a hierarchy of pacemakers, and faster suppresses slower.** The SA node (~100/min), the AV junction (~40–60) and the Purkinje system (~20–40) can all pace, but the fastest resets the others before they reach threshold (overdrive suppression). If the sinus node fails or conduction is blocked, a lower pacemaker escapes at its own rate: complete heart block gives ~30–40/min, enough to live, rarely enough to exert.

5. **The electrocardiogram is the spatial sum of all cardiac currents projected onto lines between electrodes.** The P wave is atrial depolarisation; the PR interval (120–200 ms) is mostly AV delay; the QRS complex (<120 ms) is ventricular depolarisation, narrow because the Purkinje system fires both ventricles together and broad when conduction must spread through muscle; the T wave is repolarisation, upright like the QRS because the epicardium repolarises first. The QT interval (~400 ms, rate-corrected) is action potential duration. Each of the 12 leads views the heart from a direction, so the lead in which a change appears localises it (inferior II, III, aVF; anterior V1–V4; lateral I, aVL, V5–V6). Ischaemic tissue repolarises abnormally, producing ST-segment and T-wave change.

6. **Arrhythmias have three mechanisms, and each maps to a channel or a circuit.** *Abnormal automaticity*: a cell that should not pace does, as in ischaemia. *Triggered activity*: early afterdepolarisations when repolarisation is prolonged (long QT from potassium-channel loss or drugs) launch torsades de pointes; delayed afterdepolarisations from calcium overload (digoxin toxicity) fire extra beats. *Re-entry*, the commonest, needs a circuit with slow conduction and unidirectional block, so the wavefront returns to find recovered tissue: around a scar, through an accessory pathway, within the AV node, or as wandering wavelets in atrial fibrillation. It is sustained when the wavelength (conduction velocity × refractory period) is shorter than the path; drugs that prolong refractoriness (class III) or slow conduction (class I) break it, and an ablation catheter removes the path.

7. **Plasma potassium and calcium rewrite the action potential within minutes.** Hyperkalaemia depolarises the resting potential (Nernst), inactivating sodium channels: peaked T waves, then a wide QRS, then a sine wave and arrest. Hypokalaemia prolongs repolarisation (U waves, long QT) and predisposes to triggered arrhythmias. Hypocalcaemia lengthens the QT; hypercalcaemia shortens it. Acidosis, hypoxia and catecholamines lower the threshold for fibrillation, which is why an arrest is reversible when these are corrected and not otherwise.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Pacemaker (funny) current | HCN-channel inward current giving the SA node its slow diastolic depolarisation |
| Plateau (phase 2) | Calcium-mediated ~200 ms sustained depolarisation unique to cardiac muscle |
| Effective refractory period | Interval during which no propagating response can be evoked; nearly the whole action potential |
| Overdrive suppression | Faster pacemakers resetting slower ones so only one rhythm emerges |
| Re-entry | Self-sustaining circulation of a wavefront around an obstacle or through slow tissue |

## Common misconceptions

- **"The ECG measures the heart's pumping."** It records only electrical activity; a normal trace is compatible with no output at all (pulseless electrical activity) and a bizarre one with a good pulse.
