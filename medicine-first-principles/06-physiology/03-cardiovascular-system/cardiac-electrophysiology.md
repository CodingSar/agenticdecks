## Essence

The heart times itself. A few thousand cells in the sinoatrial node depolarise spontaneously about a hundred times a minute; their impulse spreads across the atria, is deliberately delayed in the atrioventricular node so the atria can empty, then races down the His–Purkinje system to fire both ventricles from apex to base within ~80 ms. The ventricular action potential is a neuronal one with a long calcium plateau, which makes the muscle refractory for nearly the whole contraction and so prevents both tetany and most re-entry. The electrocardiogram is the sum of these currents seen from the body surface, and because the sequence and the ion channels are known, its deflections can be read back to the chamber, the direction and the channel involved. Arrhythmias arise from three mechanisms only: abnormal automaticity, triggered activity and re-entry.

## Core tenets

1. **Pacemaker cells have no resting potential; they drift to threshold, and the autonomic nerves set the slope.** Sinoatrial (SA) node cells sit at ~−60 mV and depolarise steadily (the pacemaker potential) through the "funny" current carried by hyperpolarisation-activated cyclic-nucleotide-gated (HCN) channels, T-type calcium current and a rhythmic release of calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum; the upstroke is carried by L-type calcium channels, not sodium. Vagal acetylcholine (M2 receptors) opens potassium channels and reduces the funny current, flattening the slope: resting rate ~70 against an intrinsic ~100. Sympathetic noradrenaline (β1, cyclic AMP) steepens it. Ivabradine blocks HCN and slows the heart without touching contractility.

2. **The ventricular action potential has a plateau, and the plateau is what protects the heart.** Phase 0: fast sodium influx (Nav1.5) depolarises from −90 to +30 mV in ~1 ms. Phase 1: brief repolarisation. Phase 2: a plateau of ~200 ms in which inward L-type calcium current (which triggers contraction) balances outward potassium current. Phase 3: delayed-rectifier potassium currents repolarise. Phase 4: a stable −90 mV held by inward-rectifier potassium channels. Because sodium channels stay inactivated through the plateau, the cell is refractory for almost its entire contraction: no summation, no tetanus, and a travelling wavefront cannot turn back into tissue it has just excited.

3. **Conduction has a hierarchy of speeds, and the slowest link is a safety device.** Atrial muscle conducts at ~1 m/s; the atrioventricular (AV) node at ~0.05 m/s, adding ~100 ms so that atrial systole finishes before ventricular systole begins; the His bundle and Purkinje fibres at 2–4 m/s so that both ventricles fire nearly simultaneously; ventricular muscle at ~0.5 m/s from endocardium to epicardium. The AV node also conducts *decrementally* (more slowly the faster it is stimulated), so in atrial fibrillation at 400–600 atrial impulses per minute it passes only 100–180 to the ventricles. Digoxin, β-blockers and calcium-channel blockers slow it further, which is how ventricular rate is controlled.

4. **There is a hierarchy of pacemakers, and faster suppresses slower.** The SA node (~100/min intrinsic), the AV junction (~40–60) and the Purkinje system (~20–40) can all pace, but each discharge of the fastest resets the others before they reach threshold (overdrive suppression). If the sinus node fails or conduction is blocked, a lower pacemaker escapes after a pause at its own slower rate: complete heart block gives a ventricular rhythm of ~30–40/min, enough to live, rarely enough to exert.

5. **The electrocardiogram is the spatial sum of all cardiac currents projected onto lines between electrodes.** The P wave is atrial depolarisation; the PR interval (120–200 ms) is mostly AV nodal delay; the QRS complex (<120 ms) is ventricular depolarisation, narrow because the Purkinje system fires both ventricles together and broad when conduction must spread through muscle (bundle branch block, ventricular origin); the T wave is repolarisation, upright like the QRS because the epicardium repolarises first, reversing the direction of current. The QT interval (~400 ms, rate-corrected) is action potential duration. Each of the 12 leads views the heart from a direction, so the lead in which a change appears localises it: inferior (II, III, aVF), anterior (V1–V4), lateral (I, aVL, V5–V6). Ischaemic tissue repolarises abnormally, producing ST-segment shift and T-wave change.

6. **Arrhythmias have three mechanisms, and each maps to a channel or a circuit.** *Abnormal automaticity*: a cell that should not pace does, as in ischaemia or catecholamine excess. *Triggered activity*: early afterdepolarisations when repolarisation is prolonged (long QT from potassium-channel loss or many drugs) can launch torsades de pointes; delayed afterdepolarisations from calcium overload (digoxin toxicity) can fire extra beats. *Re-entry*, the commonest, needs a circuit with a region of slow conduction and unidirectional block, so that the wavefront returns to find tissue that has recovered: around a scar after infarction, through an accessory pathway in Wolff–Parkinson–White syndrome, within the AV node, or as many wandering wavelets in atrial fibrillation. Re-entry is sustained when the wavelength (conduction velocity × refractory period) is shorter than the path; drugs that prolong refractoriness (class III) or slow conduction in the circuit (class I) break it, and an ablation catheter removes the path.

7. **Plasma potassium and calcium rewrite the action potential within minutes.** Hyperkalaemia depolarises the resting potential (Nernst), inactivating sodium channels: tall peaked T waves, then widened QRS, then a sine wave and arrest. Hypokalaemia prolongs repolarisation (U waves, long QT) and predisposes to triggered arrhythmias, especially with digoxin. Hypocalcaemia lengthens the plateau and QT; hypercalcaemia shortens them. Acidosis, hypoxia and catecholamines all lower the threshold for fibrillation, which is why the same arrest is reversible when these are corrected and not otherwise.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Pacemaker (funny) current | HCN-channel inward current giving the SA node its slow diastolic depolarisation |
| Plateau (phase 2) | Calcium-mediated ~200 ms sustained depolarisation unique to cardiac muscle |
| Effective refractory period | Interval during which no propagating response can be evoked; nearly the whole action potential |
| Decremental conduction | Slower AV nodal conduction at faster input rates; the ventricles' filter |
| Overdrive suppression | Faster pacemakers resetting slower ones so only one rhythm emerges |
| Escape rhythm | A lower pacemaker emerging when the sinus impulse fails to arrive |
| PR / QRS / QT | AV delay (120–200 ms) / ventricular depolarisation (<120 ms) / action potential duration (~400 ms corrected) |
| Afterdepolarisation | Secondary depolarisation during (early) or after (delayed) repolarisation that can trigger beats |
| Re-entry | Self-sustaining circulation of a wavefront around an obstacle or through slow tissue |
| Wavelength | Conduction velocity × refractory period; re-entry needs a path longer than this |

## Common misconceptions

- **"The ECG measures the heart's pumping."** It records only electrical activity; a normal trace is compatible with no output at all (pulseless electrical activity) and a bizarre one with a good pulse.
- **"A slow AV node is a defect."** Its slowness is what lets the atria empty and what protects the ventricles from atrial tachyarrhythmias.
- **"Antiarrhythmic drugs make the heart safer."** Many prolong the QT or slow conduction and can create the re-entry they are meant to break; several increased mortality in trials.
