## Essence

Blood flows from aorta to right atrium because the heart creates a pressure difference of ~95 mmHg across a resistance set almost entirely by the arterioles. Mean arterial pressure equals cardiac output times total peripheral resistance, and the body regulates that product, not flow to any organ, because pressure is what its sensors measure and because a constant pressure head lets each organ take the flow it needs by adjusting its own arterioles. The baroreflex corrects pressure within a heartbeat but resets within days; the kidney sets the long-term level by excreting sodium and water until excretion balances intake. Poiseuille's fourth power of radius explains why the arterioles hold the power; venous compliance explains where the blood is; parallel resistances explain how organs with a hundredfold range of need share one pressure.

## Core tenets

1. **Flow equals pressure difference over resistance, and resistance is dominated by radius.** Q = ΔP / R, and for laminar flow Poiseuille gives R = 8ηL / (πr⁴): halving the radius raises resistance sixteenfold. The arterioles (10–100 µm, thick muscular walls) are therefore the resistance vessels, and mean pressure falls from ~95 mmHg at their entrance to ~35 at the capillaries, the largest drop in the circuit.

2. **Organ beds lie in parallel, so each sets its own flow without disturbing the pressure the others see.** For parallel resistances 1/R_total = Σ 1/R_i, so dilating one bed raises its flow while lowering total resistance only slightly. This is why the brain (~15% of output), kidneys (~20%), gut and liver (~25%), muscle (~20% at rest, >80% in exercise) and skin can each follow their own metabolism.

3. **Local control matches flow to metabolism; neural control defends pressure; when they conflict, the vital organs win.** Arterioles constrict when stretched (myogenic response) and dilate to metabolites (adenosine, carbon dioxide, hydrogen ions, potassium) and to shear-released endothelial nitric oxide, so brain, heart and kidney hold flow nearly constant over mean pressures of ~60–160 mmHg (autoregulation). Sympathetic α1 fibres constrict most beds tonically ([autonomic nervous system](../02-nervous-system/the-autonomic-nervous-system.md)). In working muscle metabolites override them; in brain and heart local control rules; in skin, gut and kidney sympathetic constriction dominates, so these are sacrificed in haemorrhage.

4. **The veins hold the blood, and their compliance makes them the reservoir.** About 65% of blood volume is in the veins, ~13% in arteries, ~5% in capillaries, ~10% in the lungs and ~7% in the heart. Venous compliance is ~20–30 times arterial, so sympathetic venoconstriction can return ~1 L to the central circulation after haemorrhage, an internal transfusion. Standing pools 500–800 mL in the legs within seconds; the baroreflex, muscle pump and venous valves counter it, and when they fail the result is fainting. Venous return and cardiac output are the same flow in steady state: the heart can only pump what returns.

5. **Pulse pressure reports stroke volume against arterial stiffness.** Each stroke volume (~70 mL) enters an aorta whose compliance converts it into a pressure rise of ~40 mmHg (120/80); recoil keeps flow going in diastole. Mean pressure ≈ diastolic + one-third of pulse pressure, ~93 mmHg. With age the aorta stiffens, so the same stroke volume gives a higher systolic and lower diastolic pressure. Flow becomes turbulent at high velocity or in narrowed vessels (Reynolds number), producing bruits and Korotkoff sounds; Bernoulli's principle converts the velocity across a stenosis into the pressure gradient echocardiography reports.

6. **The baroreflex corrects pressure in seconds, by one variable, and then resets.** Stretch receptors in the carotid sinus (glossopharyngeal nerve) and aortic arch (vagus) fire in proportion to pressure. A fall reduces their firing, disinhibiting sympathetic outflow and withdrawing vagal tone: heart rate, contractility, arteriolar resistance and venous tone rise within a beat or two. The gain is high (a 40 mmHg disturbance is corrected to within ~10 mmHg) but the receptors adapt to a sustained change within 1–2 days, so the reflex buffers minute-to-minute variation and cannot set the long-term level; chronic hypertension is defended, not corrected, by it.

7. **The kidney sets the long-term pressure through pressure natriuresis, and every sustained hypertension involves a kidney that needs a higher pressure to excrete the day's sodium.** Raising arterial pressure raises renal sodium and water excretion steeply; the pressure at which excretion equals intake is the only stable long-term level, and the kidney adjusts blood volume, and with it venous return and output, until it is reached. The renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system shifts the curve: low renal perfusion or β1 stimulation releases renin, angiotensin II constricts arterioles and stimulates thirst, vasopressin and aldosterone, and aldosterone retains sodium; natriuretic peptides from a stretched heart do the opposite. Drugs that lower pressure durably act on this axis or on the resistance it sets ([cardiovascular drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/autonomic-and-cardiovascular-drugs.md); [renal sodium handling](../05-renal-system/sodium-water-and-volume-regulation.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mean arterial pressure | Cardiac output × total peripheral resistance (+ central venous pressure); ≈ diastolic + pulse pressure / 3 |
| Total peripheral resistance | Sum of all parallel vascular resistances, set chiefly by arteriolar radius |
| Poiseuille's law | Resistance ∝ viscosity × length / radius⁴ for laminar flow |
| Baroreflex | Fast neural loop from carotid sinus and aortic arch that adjusts rate, contractility and tone |

## Common misconceptions

- **"The body regulates blood flow."** It regulates pressure centrally and lets each organ regulate its own flow; in shock it will constrict the kidney to death to hold pressure for the brain.
