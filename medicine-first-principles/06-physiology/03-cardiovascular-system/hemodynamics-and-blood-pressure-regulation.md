## Essence

Blood flows from the aorta to the right atrium because the heart creates a pressure difference of ~95 mmHg across a resistance set almost entirely by the arterioles. Mean arterial pressure equals cardiac output times total peripheral resistance, and the body regulates that product, not flow to any organ, because pressure is what its sensors measure and because a constant pressure head lets each organ take the flow it needs by adjusting its own arterioles. Two control systems share the task: the baroreflex corrects pressure within a heartbeat through the autonomic nerves but resets within days, and the kidney sets the long-term level by excreting sodium and water until the pressure at which excretion balances intake is reached. Poiseuille's law, with its fourth power of radius, explains why the arterioles hold the power; the compliance of the veins explains where the blood is; and the distribution of resistances in parallel explains how organs with a hundredfold range of need share one pressure.

## Core tenets

1. **Flow equals pressure difference over resistance, and resistance is dominated by radius.** Q = ΔP / R. For laminar flow in a tube, Poiseuille gives R = 8ηL / (πr⁴): halving the radius raises resistance sixteenfold, so a small change in arteriolar diameter moves total peripheral resistance far more than any change in length or viscosity. The arterioles (diameter 10–100 µm, thick muscular walls) are therefore the resistance vessels, and mean pressure falls from ~95 mmHg at their entrance to ~35 at the capillaries, the largest drop in the circuit. Viscosity (η) matters when haematocrit changes: polycythaemia raises resistance, anaemia lowers it.

2. **Organ beds lie in parallel, so each sets its own flow without disturbing the pressure the others see.** For parallel resistances 1/R_total = Σ 1/R_i: total resistance is lower than any single bed's, and dilating one bed raises its flow while lowering total resistance only slightly. This is why the brain (~15% of output), kidneys (~20%), gut and liver (~25%), muscle (~20% at rest, >80% in exercise) and skin (variable) can each follow their own metabolism. Within a bed, series resistance (artery, arteriole, capillary, venule) adds, so a proximal stenosis steals from everything downstream.

3. **Local control matches flow to metabolism; neural control defends pressure; when they conflict, the vital organs win.** Arterioles constrict when stretched (myogenic response) and dilate to metabolites (adenosine, carbon dioxide, hydrogen ions, potassium, low oxygen) and to endothelial nitric oxide released by shear stress, so brain, heart and kidney hold flow nearly constant over mean pressures of ~60–160 mmHg (autoregulation). Sympathetic α1 fibres constrict most beds tonically ([autonomic nervous system](../02-nervous-system/the-autonomic-nervous-system.md)). In working muscle, metabolites override sympathetic constriction; in brain and heart, sympathetic influence is weak and local control rules; in skin, gut and kidney, sympathetic constriction dominates, so these are sacrificed in haemorrhage.

4. **The veins hold the blood, and their compliance makes them the reservoir.** About 65% of blood volume is in the veins, ~13% in arteries, ~5% in capillaries, ~10% in the lungs and ~7% in the heart. Venous compliance is ~20–30 times arterial, so a litre can be lost from the venous side with a small fall in pressure, and sympathetic venoconstriction can return ~1 L to the central circulation: an internal transfusion that keeps cardiac output after haemorrhage. Standing pools 500–800 mL in the legs within seconds; the baroreflex, the muscle pump and venous valves counter it, and when they fail the result is a postural fall in pressure and fainting. Venous return and cardiac output are the same flow in steady state, which is Guyton's way of saying that the heart can only pump what returns.

5. **Pulse pressure reports stroke volume against arterial stiffness.** Each stroke volume (~70 mL) is pushed into an aorta whose compliance converts it into a pressure rise of ~40 mmHg (120/80); the elastic recoil then keeps flow going in diastole. Mean pressure ≈ diastolic + one-third of pulse pressure, ~93 mmHg. With age the aorta stiffens, so the same stroke volume produces a higher systolic and lower diastolic pressure (isolated systolic hypertension) and the heart's afterload rises. Flow becomes turbulent when velocity is high or vessels are narrowed (Reynolds number), producing the bruits of arterial stenosis and the Korotkoff sounds under a cuff; Bernoulli's principle converts the velocity across a stenosis into the pressure gradient echocardiography reports.

6. **The baroreflex corrects pressure in seconds, by one variable, and then resets.** Stretch receptors in the carotid sinus (glossopharyngeal nerve) and aortic arch (vagus) fire in proportion to pressure and its rate of change. A fall in pressure reduces their firing, disinhibiting sympathetic outflow and withdrawing vagal tone: heart rate, contractility, arteriolar resistance and venous tone all rise within one or two beats. The gain is high (a 40 mmHg disturbance is corrected to within ~10 mmHg) but the receptors adapt to a sustained change within 1–2 days, so the reflex buffers minute-to-minute variation and cannot set the long-term level; chronic hypertension is defended, not corrected, by it.

7. **The kidney sets the long-term pressure through pressure natriuresis, and every sustained hypertension involves a kidney that needs a higher pressure to excrete the day's sodium.** Raising arterial pressure raises renal sodium and water excretion steeply; the pressure at which excretion equals intake is the only stable long-term level, and the kidney will adjust blood volume, and with it venous return and output, until that pressure is reached. The renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system shifts the curve: low renal perfusion or sympathetic β1 stimulation releases renin, angiotensin II constricts arterioles (and the efferent arteriole), stimulates thirst, vasopressin and aldosterone, and aldosterone retains sodium. Natriuretic peptides from a stretched heart do the opposite. Drugs that lower pressure durably all act on this axis or on the resistance it sets: diuretics, angiotensin-converting-enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, calcium-channel blockers ([cardiovascular drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/autonomic-and-cardiovascular-drugs.md); [renal sodium handling](../05-renal-system/sodium-water-and-volume-regulation.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mean arterial pressure | Cardiac output × total peripheral resistance (+ central venous pressure); ≈ diastolic + pulse pressure / 3 |
| Total peripheral resistance | Sum of all parallel vascular resistances, set chiefly by arteriolar radius |
| Poiseuille's law | Resistance ∝ viscosity × length / radius⁴ for laminar flow |
| Autoregulation | Constant organ flow across a range of pressures, by myogenic and metabolic control |
| Compliance | ΔVolume / ΔPressure; veins ~20–30× arteries |
| Venous return | Flow back to the heart; equals cardiac output in steady state |
| Pulse pressure | Systolic − diastolic; stroke volume / arterial compliance |
| Baroreflex | Fast neural loop from carotid sinus and aortic arch that adjusts rate, contractility and tone |
| Pressure natriuresis | Rise in renal sodium excretion with arterial pressure; sets the long-term level |
| Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system | Hormonal cascade that constricts vessels and retains sodium when renal perfusion falls |

## Common misconceptions

- **"The body regulates blood flow."** It regulates pressure centrally and lets each organ regulate its own flow; in shock it will constrict the kidney to death to hold pressure for the brain.
- **"Hypertension is a disease of the arteries."** Stiff arteries raise systolic pressure, but a sustained rise in mean pressure requires a kidney that excretes sodium only at that higher pressure.
- **"Blood pressure falls on standing because blood falls to the feet."** It pools in compliant leg veins, lowering venous return and stroke volume; the baroreflex normally hides this within seconds.
