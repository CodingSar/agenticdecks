## Essence

The heart is two pumps in series, each filling at low pressure and ejecting at high pressure through valves moved only by pressure differences. Each beat the left ventricle takes in ~120 mL and ejects ~70 mL (ejection fraction ~60%) against ~120/80 mmHg; at ~70 beats per minute that is ~5 L/min. Stroke volume is set by preload (stretch before contraction), afterload (the pressure to overcome) and contractility (force at a given stretch). The Frank–Starling mechanism, by which a more stretched ventricle ejects more, lets the two pumps match outputs beat by beat with no controller. The heart's own oxygen supply is the weak point: it extracts most of the oxygen in its coronary blood even at rest, so any rise in demand must be met by more flow, during a diastole that shortens as it works harder.

## Core tenets

1. **The cycle is a sequence of pressure crossings, and the valves are passive.** Diastole: the ventricle fills through the open mitral valve, rapidly, then slowly, then with the atrial contraction that adds ~20% at rest. Systole: ventricular pressure exceeds atrial and the mitral valve shuts (first heart sound); pressure rises at constant volume until it exceeds aortic pressure, the aortic valve opens and blood is ejected; as the ventricle relaxes the aortic valve shuts (second heart sound) and pressure falls isovolumetrically until the mitral valve opens again. Left ventricular pressure swings from ~5–10 to ~120 mmHg; the right ventricle, pumping into a low-resistance lung, reaches ~25 mmHg. A murmur is turbulent flow through a valve that fails to open fully or to close.

2. **Diastole is two-thirds of the cycle at rest and shrinks first when heart rate rises, which costs filling and coronary flow.** At 70/min the cycle lasts ~0.85 s, systole ~0.3 s; at 180/min diastole is squeezed to ~0.1 s. The left ventricle is perfused almost entirely in diastole, because systolic wall tension compresses its own vessels. Tachycardia therefore raises oxygen demand while cutting both filling time and the time in which supply can arrive.

3. **Frank–Starling: the more the ventricle is filled, the more it ejects, so venous return and cardiac output are equal without any controller.** Stretching cardiac muscle increases its force, through myofilament overlap and calcium sensitivity, up to a plateau. If venous return rises, the ventricle fills more and ejects more on the next beat; if the right heart delivers more, the left heart matches it. A weakened ventricle has a flatter Starling curve, so it needs a higher filling pressure for the same output, and that pressure is transmitted back into the lungs as congestion.

4. **Afterload is wall stress, and Laplace's law says dilation raises it and hypertrophy lowers it.** Wall stress σ = P·r / 2h: it rises with the pressure generated and the radius, and falls with wall thickness. A dilated failing ventricle needs more tension per unit pressure; a pressure-loaded ventricle (hypertension, aortic stenosis) thickens to normalise stress, at the cost of stiffness and oxygen demand. Lowering arterial pressure reduces afterload and lets a weak ventricle eject more.

5. **Contractility is the force at a given length, and it is set by calcium.** Each action potential admits calcium through L-type channels, triggering much larger release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum; force depends on how much calcium reaches the filaments. Sympathetic β1 stimulation raises cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), which increases calcium entry and release (more force) and speeds its reuptake (faster relaxation, so filling survives tachycardia). Digoxin raises intracellular calcium indirectly; β-blockers, calcium-channel blockers, acidosis and ischaemia lower contractility. On a pressure–volume loop, contractility is the slope of the end-systolic line and stroke work the area inside.

6. **Myocardial oxygen demand rises with heart rate, wall tension and contractility, and coronary supply can only rise by increasing flow.** The heart at rest extracts ~70–80% of the oxygen from its ~250 mL/min of coronary blood, against ~25% for the body as a whole, so demand rising four- to fivefold in exercise is met by coronary vasodilation (adenosine, nitric oxide) raising flow in proportion. A narrowed coronary artery is therefore silent at rest and fails when demand rises; nitrates relieve angina mainly by lowering venous return and wall tension, that is, demand.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Stroke volume | Volume ejected per beat, ~70 mL; end-diastolic minus end-systolic volume |
| Ejection fraction | Stroke volume / end-diastolic volume, ~55–70% |
| Preload | End-diastolic stretch of the ventricle; approximated by filling pressure or volume |
| Afterload | The load the ventricle ejects against; wall stress, dominated by arterial pressure |
| Contractility (inotropy) | Force developed at a given preload and afterload; set by calcium handling |
| Frank–Starling mechanism | Stroke volume rises with end-diastolic volume |
| Laplace relation | Wall stress ∝ pressure × radius / wall thickness |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Valves open and close actively."** They are flaps moved only by pressure differences; the papillary muscles prevent the mitral and tricuspid leaflets from everting, nothing more.
- **"A normal ejection fraction means a normal heart."** Half of heart failure has a preserved ejection fraction: the ventricle is stiff and fills poorly, so output is low despite ejecting a normal fraction of a small volume.
