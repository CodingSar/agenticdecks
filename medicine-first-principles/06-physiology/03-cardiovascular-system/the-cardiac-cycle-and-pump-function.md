# The cardiac cycle and pump function

## Essence

The heart is two pumps in series, each filling at low pressure and ejecting at high pressure through valves that open and close purely because of pressure differences. Each beat the left ventricle takes in ~120 mL, ejects ~70 mL (an ejection fraction of ~60%) against an aortic pressure of ~120/80 mmHg, and at ~70 beats per minute delivers ~5 L/min. Stroke volume is set by three things: how much the ventricle is stretched before it contracts (preload), what pressure it must overcome (afterload) and how forcefully its muscle contracts at a given stretch (contractility). The Frank–Starling mechanism, by which a more stretched ventricle ejects more, is what lets the two pumps match their outputs beat by beat with no controller at all. The heart's own oxygen supply is the system's weak point: it extracts most of the oxygen in its coronary blood even at rest, so any rise in demand must be met by more flow, during the diastole it shortens when it works harder.

## Core tenets

1. **The cycle is a sequence of pressure crossings, and the valves are passive.** Diastole: the ventricle fills from the atrium while the mitral valve is open, rapidly at first, then slowly, then with the atrial contraction that adds ~20% at rest and more at fast rates. Systole: the ventricle contracts, pressure exceeds atrial pressure and the mitral valve shuts (first heart sound); pressure rises with no volume change (isovolumetric contraction) until it exceeds aortic pressure, the aortic valve opens and blood is ejected; as the ventricle relaxes, aortic pressure exceeds ventricular, the aortic valve shuts (second heart sound) and pressure falls isovolumetrically until the mitral valve opens again. Left ventricular pressure swings from ~5–10 to ~120 mmHg; the right ventricle, pumping into a low-resistance lung, reaches only ~25 mmHg. A murmur is turbulent flow through a valve that fails to open fully or to close.

2. **Diastole is two-thirds of the cycle at rest and shrinks first when heart rate rises, which costs filling and coronary flow.** At 70/min the cycle lasts ~0.85 s, systole ~0.3 s; at 180/min the cycle is 0.33 s and diastole is squeezed to ~0.1 s. The left ventricle is perfused almost entirely in diastole, because systolic wall tension compresses its own intramural vessels. Tachycardia therefore raises oxygen demand while cutting both the filling time and the time in which supply can arrive, and an atrium that no longer contracts (atrial fibrillation) loses its contribution just when it is needed.

3. **Frank–Starling: the more the ventricle is filled, the more it ejects, so venous return and cardiac output are equal without any controller.** Stretching cardiac muscle increases its force, through better myofilament overlap and increased calcium sensitivity, up to a plateau. If venous return rises, the ventricle fills more and ejects more on the next beat; if the right heart delivers more to the lungs, the left heart receives more and matches it. The curve relating stroke volume to end-diastolic volume (or pressure) is the Starling curve; a weakened ventricle has a flatter curve, so it needs a higher filling pressure for the same output, and that pressure is transmitted back into the lungs as congestion.

4. **Afterload is wall stress, and Laplace's law says dilation raises it and hypertrophy lowers it.** Wall stress σ = P·r / 2h for a thick-walled sphere: it rises with the pressure the ventricle must generate and with its radius, and falls with wall thickness. A dilated failing ventricle is at a mechanical disadvantage, needing more tension per unit pressure; a pressure-loaded ventricle (hypertension, aortic stenosis) thickens to normalise stress, at the cost of stiffness and oxygen demand. Lowering arterial pressure or venous return reduces afterload and lets a weak ventricle eject more.

5. **Contractility is the force at a given length, and it is set by calcium.** Each action potential admits calcium through L-type channels, which triggers much larger release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (calcium-induced calcium release); force depends on how much calcium reaches the filaments. Sympathetic β1 stimulation raises cyclic AMP, which increases calcium entry and release (more force), speeds its reuptake (faster relaxation, so filling survives tachycardia) and speeds the pacemaker. Digoxin raises intracellular calcium indirectly; calcium-channel blockers, β-blockers, acidosis and ischaemia lower contractility. On a pressure–volume loop, contractility is the slope of the end-systolic line and stroke work is the area inside the loop.

6. **Cardiac muscle cannot be tetanised and must contract as one.** Its action potential lasts ~250 ms, almost the whole contraction, so a second stimulus cannot summate twitches; the heart always relaxes fully and fills. Gap junctions make the myocardium a functional syncytium, so the whole chamber contracts together; the price is that one abnormal focus can capture the whole chamber ([cardiac electrophysiology](cardiac-electrophysiology.md)).

7. **Myocardial oxygen demand rises with heart rate, wall tension and contractility, and coronary supply can only rise by increasing flow.** The heart at rest extracts ~70–80% of the oxygen from its ~250 mL/min of coronary blood, against ~25% for the body as a whole. Demand can rise four- to fivefold in exercise, met by coronary vasodilation (adenosine, nitric oxide, hypoxia) that raises flow in proportion. A narrowed coronary artery is therefore silent at rest and fails exactly when demand rises, and anything that raises rate or wall tension (anaemia, tachycardia, hypertension) can unmask it. Nitrates relieve angina mainly by lowering venous return and wall tension, that is, demand.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Stroke volume | Volume ejected per beat, ~70 mL; end-diastolic minus end-systolic volume |
| Ejection fraction | Stroke volume / end-diastolic volume, ~55–70% |
| Cardiac output | Heart rate × stroke volume, ~5 L/min at rest (~3 L/min/m² of body surface) |
| Preload | End-diastolic stretch of the ventricle; approximated by filling pressure or volume |
| Afterload | The load the ventricle ejects against; wall stress, dominated by arterial pressure |
| Contractility (inotropy) | Force developed at a given preload and afterload; set by calcium handling |
| Frank–Starling mechanism | Stroke volume rises with end-diastolic volume |
| Laplace relation | Wall stress ∝ pressure × radius / wall thickness |
| Lusitropy | Rate of relaxation; enhanced by sympathetic stimulation |
| Pressure–volume loop | Plot of ventricular pressure against volume over one cycle; area = stroke work |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Valves open and close actively."** They are flaps moved only by pressure differences; the papillary muscles prevent the mitral and tricuspid leaflets from everting, nothing more.
- **"A normal ejection fraction means a normal heart."** Half of heart failure has a preserved ejection fraction: the ventricle is stiff and fills poorly, so output is low despite ejecting a normal fraction of a small volume.
- **"Faster is always better for output."** Beyond ~180/min filling time falls faster than rate rises, and output falls.
