## Essence

The whole circulation exists to deliver blood to some ten billion capillaries, each a tube of single endothelial cells 5–10 µm wide and ~1 mm long, together presenting a few hundred square metres of wall to the tissues. Exchange across that wall is almost entirely by diffusion down concentration gradients, which is fast because the distance is small; a separate, much smaller bulk flow of water is governed by the balance of hydrostatic and oncotic pressures that Starling described, and whatever filters out returns through the lymphatics. Oedema is this balance tipped by any one of its terms. The endothelium is not a passive filter but a tissue that controls local flow, permeability, clotting and leukocyte traffic, and its failure in sepsis is why the circulation can be full yet the tissues starve.

## Core tenets

1. **Diffusion does the exchange, and it works because the geometry is extreme.** Fick's law: flux = D × A × ΔC / Δx. Capillary density scales with metabolic rate (heart and brain ~3,000 per mm², resting muscle fewer, with only a quarter to a third perfused at rest), so no cell is more than ~20–50 µm from a capillary; recruitment of closed capillaries in exercise halves the distance and multiplies the area. Lipid-soluble molecules (oxygen, carbon dioxide, anaesthetics) cross the whole endothelial surface; water-soluble ones (glucose, ions, urea) cross through intercellular clefts and fenestrations ~6–7 nm wide; proteins barely cross at all. Blood spends ~1–2 s in a capillary, enough for small solutes to equilibrate completely, so delivery of oxygen and glucose is limited by flow, not by the wall (flow-limited exchange).

2. **Capillary walls differ by organ, and the differences are the organ's physiology.** Continuous capillaries with tight junctions (muscle, skin, lung) and the still tighter blood–brain barrier (astrocyte-induced junctions, few vesicles, active efflux pumps) keep proteins and most drugs out. Fenestrated capillaries (kidney glomerulus, gut mucosa, endocrine glands) have pores for high water and small-solute flux. Discontinuous sinusoids (liver, spleen, marrow) have gaps that let proteins and whole cells pass, which is how albumin and clotting factors enter blood and red cells leave the marrow.

3. **Water moves by the Starling balance, and the four terms are the four causes of oedema.** Net filtration = K_f [(P_c − P_i) − σ(π_c − π_i)]: hydrostatic pressure in the capillary (~30 mmHg at the arterial end, ~15 at the venous) pushes fluid out, interstitial pressure (near zero or slightly negative) barely resists, plasma oncotic pressure (~25 mmHg, mostly albumin) pulls it back, interstitial oncotic pressure (~5–8) opposes. Net outward force is a few mmHg, filtering ~2–4 L/day over the whole body (more by some estimates) that the lymphatics return. Raise P_c (venous obstruction, heart failure, excess fluid), lower π_c (albumin <~20 g/L in liver failure, nephrotic syndrome, starvation), raise K_f or lower σ (inflammation, burns, sepsis making the wall leaky) or block the lymphatics (surgery, filariasis, tumour), and fluid accumulates.

4. **Oedema has safety factors, which is why it appears late and then suddenly.** As fluid enters the interstitium, its pressure rises (low compliance at first), lymph flow rises up to tenfold or more and washes protein out of the interstitium, lowering π_i. Together these oppose ~15–17 mmHg of disturbance before visible oedema forms, which is why venous pressure or hypoalbuminaemia must be substantial before swelling appears, and why, once the interstitial compliance curve flattens, litres accumulate with little further rise in pressure.

5. **The glycocalyx is the real filter, and the venous end does not reabsorb much.** The endothelial glycocalyx, a protein–glycosaminoglycan layer ~0.5 µm thick, excludes plasma proteins, so the oncotic gradient that matters is across it, not across the whole wall; the protein-poor fluid immediately beneath it keeps filtration small and nearly uniform along the capillary. In steady state most capillaries filter slightly along their whole length and lymph, not venous reabsorption, returns the fluid (Levick and Michel's revision of Starling). The glycocalyx is shed in sepsis, ischaemia–reperfusion and rapid fluid loading, raising K_f, which is one reason large infusions in sepsis leak into tissue within an hour.

6. **The lymphatics return fluid, protein and cells, and a lymphatic cannot be bypassed.** Initial lymphatics are blind-ended, valved tubes that fill when interstitial pressure rises and empty when compressed by muscle or arterial pulsation; collecting vessels have smooth muscle that pumps. They return the ~2–4 L/day of filtrate with its ~100 g of protein to the venous system at the thoracic duct, carry absorbed fat from the gut, and deliver antigen and antigen-presenting cells to lymph nodes, which is why infection and cancer spread along them and why their obstruction produces a protein-rich, non-pitting oedema that does not resolve with diuretics.

7. **The endothelium controls its own flow and the behaviour of blood, and inflammation reprogrammes it.** Shear stress releases nitric oxide and prostacyclin, dilating arterioles and preventing platelet adhesion; endothelin constricts. The resting surface is anticoagulant (heparan sulphate, thrombomodulin, tissue plasminogen activator). Inflammatory signals (histamine, bradykinin, tumour necrosis factor, interleukin-1) contract endothelial cells to open gaps, express adhesion molecules that catch leukocytes, and switch the surface to procoagulant: the local programme of [acute inflammation](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/acute-inflammation.md). Applied to the whole body in sepsis, the same programme causes capillary leak, microthrombosis and maldistributed flow, so cells can be hypoxic while the cardiac output is high.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fick's law of diffusion | Flux ∝ diffusivity × area × concentration gradient / distance |
| Flow-limited vs diffusion-limited exchange | Equilibration complete within transit time (oxygen, glucose) vs not (large or slow solutes) |
| Capillary recruitment | Opening of closed capillaries, raising area and shortening diffusion distance |
| Starling forces | Capillary and interstitial hydrostatic and oncotic pressures that set net filtration |
| Filtration coefficient (K_f) | Hydraulic conductance of the wall × its area |
| Reflection coefficient (σ) | Fraction of protein held back (~0.9–1 in continuous capillaries) |
| Oncotic pressure | Osmotic pressure of plasma proteins, ~25 mmHg, mostly albumin |
| Glycocalyx | Endothelial surface layer that excludes protein and is the true semipermeable barrier |
| Safety factors against oedema | Rising interstitial pressure, rising lymph flow, falling interstitial protein |
| Lymphatic return | ~2–4 L/day of filtrate and protein back to the veins via the thoracic duct |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Oxygen is delivered by filtration."** It diffuses; the bulk flow of water is a small side-stream that carries almost nothing the cell needs.
- **"Arterial ends filter and venous ends reabsorb, so lymph is a minor route."** In most tissues filtration is slight along the whole capillary and lymph returns it all; sustained reabsorption occurs only in special sites (intestinal mucosa, renal peritubular capillaries).
- **"More intravenous fluid means more tissue perfusion."** Beyond restoring filling, fluid raises capillary pressure and dilutes albumin, so it leaks; in a leaky circulation it worsens the oedema that impairs diffusion.
