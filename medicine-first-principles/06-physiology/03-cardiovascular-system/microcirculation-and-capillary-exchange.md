## Essence

The circulation exists to deliver blood to some ten billion capillaries, each a tube of single endothelial cells 5–10 µm wide, together presenting a few hundred square metres of wall to the tissues. Exchange across that wall is almost entirely by diffusion down concentration gradients, fast because the distance is small; a much smaller bulk flow of water is governed by the balance of hydrostatic and oncotic pressures Starling described, and what filters out returns through the lymphatics. Oedema is this balance tipped by any one of its terms. The endothelium is not a passive filter but a tissue that controls flow, permeability, clotting and leukocyte traffic, and its failure in sepsis is why the circulation can be full yet the tissues starve.

## Core tenets

1. **Diffusion does the exchange, and it works because the geometry is extreme.** Fick's law: flux = D × A × ΔC / Δx. Capillary density scales with metabolic rate (heart and brain ~3,000 per mm²; only a quarter to a third of muscle capillaries are perfused at rest), so no cell is more than ~20–50 µm from a capillary; recruitment in exercise halves the distance and multiplies the area. Lipid-soluble molecules (oxygen, carbon dioxide) cross the whole endothelial surface; water-soluble ones (glucose, ions) cross through clefts ~6–7 nm wide; proteins barely cross. Blood spends ~1–2 s in a capillary, enough for small solutes to equilibrate, so delivery of oxygen and glucose is limited by flow, not by the wall.

2. **Capillary walls differ by organ, and the differences are the organ's physiology.** Continuous capillaries (muscle, skin, lung) and the tighter blood–brain barrier keep proteins and most drugs out; fenestrated capillaries (glomerulus, gut, endocrine glands) have pores for high water flux; discontinuous sinusoids (liver, spleen, marrow) let proteins and whole cells pass, which is how albumin enters blood and red cells leave the marrow.

3. **Water moves by the Starling balance, and the four terms are the four causes of oedema.** Net filtration = K_f [(P_c − P_i) − σ(π_c − π_i)]: capillary hydrostatic pressure (~30 mmHg at the arterial end, ~15 at the venous) pushes fluid out, interstitial pressure (near zero) barely resists, plasma oncotic pressure (~25 mmHg, mostly albumin) pulls it back, interstitial oncotic pressure (~5–8) opposes. Net outward force is a few mmHg, filtering ~2–4 L/day that the lymphatics return. Raise P_c (venous obstruction, heart failure), lower π_c (albumin <~20 g/L in liver failure or nephrotic syndrome), raise K_f or lower σ (inflammation, burns, sepsis) or block the lymphatics, and fluid accumulates.

4. **Oedema has safety factors, which is why it appears late and then suddenly.** As fluid enters the interstitium its pressure rises, lymph flow rises tenfold or more and washes protein out, lowering π_i. Together these oppose ~15–17 mmHg of disturbance before visible oedema forms, which is why venous pressure or hypoalbuminaemia must be substantial before swelling appears, and why, once the interstitial compliance curve flattens, litres accumulate with little further rise in pressure.

5. **The glycocalyx is the real filter, and the venous end does not reabsorb much.** The endothelial glycocalyx, a protein–glycosaminoglycan layer ~0.5 µm thick, excludes plasma proteins, so the oncotic gradient that matters is across it, and the protein-poor fluid beneath it keeps filtration small and nearly uniform along the capillary. Most capillaries filter slightly along their whole length and lymph, not venous reabsorption, returns the fluid (Levick and Michel's revision of Starling). The glycocalyx is shed in sepsis, ischaemia–reperfusion and rapid fluid loading, raising K_f, which is one reason large infusions in sepsis leak into tissue within an hour.

6. **The lymphatics return fluid, protein and cells, and a lymphatic cannot be bypassed.** Initial lymphatics are blind-ended, valved tubes that fill when interstitial pressure rises and are emptied by muscle compression and their own smooth muscle. They return the ~2–4 L/day of filtrate with its ~100 g of protein to the veins at the thoracic duct, carry absorbed fat, and deliver antigen to lymph nodes, which is why infection and cancer spread along them and why their obstruction produces a protein-rich oedema that diuretics do not resolve.

7. **The endothelium controls its own flow and the behaviour of blood, and inflammation reprogrammes it.** Shear stress releases nitric oxide and prostacyclin, dilating arterioles and preventing platelet adhesion; the resting surface is anticoagulant (heparan sulphate, thrombomodulin). Inflammatory signals (histamine, bradykinin, tumour necrosis factor) open gaps between endothelial cells, express adhesion molecules that catch leukocytes, and switch the surface to procoagulant: the local programme of [acute inflammation](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/acute-inflammation.md). Applied to the whole body in sepsis, the same programme causes capillary leak, microthrombosis and maldistributed flow, so cells can be hypoxic while cardiac output is high.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fick's law of diffusion | Flux ∝ diffusivity × area × concentration gradient / distance |
| Flow-limited vs diffusion-limited exchange | Equilibration complete within transit time (oxygen, glucose) vs not (large or slow solutes) |
| Starling forces | Capillary and interstitial hydrostatic and oncotic pressures that set net filtration |
| Oncotic pressure | Osmotic pressure of plasma proteins, ~25 mmHg, mostly albumin |
| Glycocalyx | Endothelial surface layer that excludes protein and is the true semipermeable barrier |
| Safety factors against oedema | Rising interstitial pressure, rising lymph flow, falling interstitial protein |

## Common misconceptions

- **"More intravenous fluid means more tissue perfusion."** Beyond restoring filling, fluid raises capillary pressure and dilutes albumin, so it leaks; in a leaky circulation it worsens the oedema that impairs diffusion.
