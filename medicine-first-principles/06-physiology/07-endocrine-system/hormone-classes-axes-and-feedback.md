# Hormone classes, axes and feedback

## Essence

A hormone is a chemical released into the blood by one tissue to change the behaviour of distant tissues that carry its receptor. Endocrine signalling is slow (seconds to days), broadcast (the whole body is exposed) and selective only through receptor expression, which suits it to regulating quantities that must be the same everywhere: glucose, calcium, osmolality, metabolic rate, growth, reproduction. Most hormones sit in negative feedback loops, often three tiers deep (hypothalamus, pituitary, target gland), in which the final hormone suppresses the signals that released it. Reading an endocrine problem is therefore reading a control loop: the pair of values (hormone and the thing it regulates) tells you which element of the loop has failed, because a gland failing on its own looks different from a gland that has lost its drive.

## Core tenets

1. **Chemistry decides where a hormone acts and how fast.** Peptides and catecholamines are water-soluble, stored in granules, released by exocytosis, travel free in plasma, cannot cross membranes and act on surface receptors through second messengers in seconds to minutes. Steroids and thyroid hormones are lipid-soluble, made on demand, travel bound to carrier proteins, enter cells and act on nuclear receptors that change transcription over hours to days. The chemistry also fixes the route of administration and half-life of hormone drugs: insulin must be injected; thyroxine and steroids are taken by mouth ([endocrine drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/endocrine-and-metabolic-drugs.md)).

2. **Only the free fraction is active, and binding proteins buffer it.** Over 99% of thyroxine and most cortisol and sex steroids circulate bound to globulins and albumin. The bound pool is a reservoir that smooths delivery and extends half-life (thyroxine ~7 days); the free fraction equilibrates with tissues and is what feedback senses. Changes in binding protein (pregnancy, oestrogen, liver disease) change the total measured hormone without changing the free level, a frequent source of misread laboratory values ([clinical chemistry](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/02-laboratory-medicine/clinical-chemistry-and-reference-ranges.md)).

3. **Receptor number and affinity set the response, not hormone concentration alone.** Target cells adjust receptor density: sustained high hormone down-regulates receptors (tolerance to catecholamines; insulin resistance in part), low hormone up-regulates them. Some hormones induce receptors for others (oestrogen for progesterone and oxytocin receptors in the uterus). The same hormone thus means different things to different tissues at different times, and resistance at the receptor is a disease mechanism as important as deficiency of the hormone.

4. **The hypothalamic–pituitary axes are three-tier amplifiers with feedback at every tier.** The hypothalamus secretes nanogram quantities of releasing hormones into the portal vessels of the pituitary stalk; the anterior pituitary responds with microgram quantities of trophic hormones; the target gland (thyroid, adrenal cortex, gonad) secretes milligram quantities of the final hormone. The final hormone inhibits both pituitary and hypothalamus (long-loop feedback), and pituitary hormones inhibit the hypothalamus (short loop). Gain is high and the set point is adjustable: cortisol's set point rises in stress, thyroid's falls in starvation.

   ```
   hypothalamus --CRH/TRH/GnRH/GHRH--> anterior pituitary --ACTH/TSH/LH,FSH/GH--> target gland
        ^                                   ^                                       |
        |                                   |                                       v
        +---------- long-loop negative feedback by cortisol / T4,T3 / sex steroids --+
   ```

5. **Pulsatility and rhythm are part of the message.** Gonadotropin-releasing hormone must arrive in pulses roughly hourly; continuous exposure desensitises the pituitary and shuts the reproductive axis down, which is how GnRH agonists are used to suppress it. Cortisol and growth hormone follow a circadian pattern, highest in the early morning and during early sleep respectively, so a single random level can be meaningless and tests are timed or dynamic ([sleep and circadian rhythms](../01-integrative-principles/sleep-and-circadian-rhythms.md)).

6. **Interpreting a loop means measuring two values at once.** A low final hormone with a high trophic hormone (low thyroxine, high thyroid-stimulating hormone) means the gland has failed and the pituitary is responding correctly: primary failure. A low final hormone with a low or inappropriately normal trophic hormone means the drive has failed: secondary (pituitary) or tertiary (hypothalamic). A high final hormone with a suppressed trophic hormone means autonomous secretion by the gland or an external source. The feedback loop makes the pair diagnostic where either value alone is ambiguous; this logic runs through all of [adrenal, pituitary and gonadal disorders](../../12-organ-system-specialties/05-endocrinology/adrenal-pituitary-and-gonadal-disorders.md).

7. **Dynamic tests probe the loop by pushing on it.** When a gland may be autonomous, give the suppressor and see whether it switches off (dexamethasone for cortisol, glucose for growth hormone). When a gland may be failing, give the stimulus and see whether it responds (synthetic corticotropin for the adrenal). A loop that cannot be suppressed is autonomous; one that cannot be stimulated has no reserve. Basal measurements show state; dynamic tests show gain.

8. **Endocrine disease is excess, deficiency or resistance, and each has the same four causes.** Autoimmunity (destruction, or stimulating antibodies as in Graves disease), tumours (autonomous secretion, or mass effect on neighbours in the pituitary), enzymatic or receptor defects, and iatrogenic causes (exogenous steroids suppressing the axis for months) account for most endocrine disorders. Because a trophic hormone is also a growth factor, prolonged lack of feedback enlarges the gland: the goitre of iodine deficiency is the thyroid growing under unsuppressed TSH.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Endocrine, paracrine, autocrine | Signal via blood to distant cells; to neighbours; to the secreting cell itself |
| Free fraction | The unbound, biologically active portion of a protein-bound hormone |
| Binding globulin | Plasma carrier (thyroxine-binding globulin, cortisol-binding globulin, sex hormone-binding globulin) that buffers free hormone |
| Trophic hormone | Pituitary hormone that stimulates both secretion and growth of a target gland |
| Long-loop and short-loop feedback | Final hormone inhibiting pituitary and hypothalamus; pituitary hormone inhibiting hypothalamus |
| Primary, secondary, tertiary | Failure at the target gland, pituitary, or hypothalamus |
| Down-regulation | Reduced receptor number after sustained exposure; the basis of tolerance and some resistance |
| Pulsatile secretion | Release in bursts whose frequency carries information; continuous exposure can switch a response off |
| Dynamic test | Suppression or stimulation test that reveals autonomy or reserve |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A hormone level in the reference range is normal."** Normal is defined relative to the regulated variable: a 'normal' TSH alongside a low thyroxine is inappropriate and points to the pituitary.
- **"More hormone always means more effect."** Receptor down-regulation, binding proteins and continuous versus pulsatile delivery can make a higher level produce less response, or none.
- **"Hormones act only on one target organ."** Receptors are expressed widely; cortisol alters nearly every tissue, and the 'target gland' of an axis is only where feedback is generated.
