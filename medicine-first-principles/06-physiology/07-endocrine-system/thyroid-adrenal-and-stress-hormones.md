## Essence

Two glands set the body's tempo. The thyroid sets the baseline metabolic rate of nearly every cell over weeks, through a hormone whose supply is buffered so heavily that output barely changes from day to day. The adrenal gland handles departures from baseline on two timescales: the medulla releases adrenaline within seconds for fight or flight, and the cortex releases cortisol within minutes to hours to mobilise fuel, sustain blood pressure and restrain inflammation for the duration of a threat, plus aldosterone for salt. Both glands sit at the bottom of a hypothalamic–pituitary axis, so their disorders are read as feedback loops. The stress response is these systems together with the sympathetic nervous system: an integrated programme that is life-saving for hours and harmful for years.

## Core tenets

1. **Thyroid hormone is made slowly, stored in bulk and released as a prohormone, which makes the system stable and slow.** The gland concentrates iodide 20–40-fold, oxidises it onto tyrosines in thyroglobulin, couples them to form thyroxine (T4, four iodines) and triiodothyronine (T3, three), and stores enough in colloid for ~2–3 months. It secretes mainly T4, which is largely inert; deiodinases in liver, kidney, muscle and brain convert it to T3, the active hormone, locally and on demand. With a T4 half-life of ~7 days and bulk storage, plasma levels change over weeks, so the thyroid cannot respond to acute stress and does not need to.

2. **T3 sets metabolic rate by changing gene expression everywhere.** Acting on nuclear receptors, T3 increases the transcription of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, mitochondrial enzymes, beta-adrenergic receptors and uncoupling proteins; basal metabolic rate, heat production, heart rate and contractility, gut motility and the speed of reflexes rise with it. Hypothyroidism is the whole organism running slow and cold; hyperthyroidism is the whole organism running fast and hot, with much of the cardiovascular picture due to amplified sympathetic signalling, which is why beta-blockers relieve it ([thyroid disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/05-endocrinology/thyroid-disease.md), [thermoregulation](../01-integrative-principles/thermoregulation-and-energy-balance.md)). In the fetus and infant T3 is required for brain development; untreated congenital deficiency causes irreversible intellectual disability, which is why newborns are screened.

3. **The thyroid axis has very high gain, so thyroid-stimulating hormone is the sensitive readout.** Thyrotropin-releasing hormone drives pituitary Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone (TSH); TSH drives every step of synthesis and gland growth; T4 and T3 inhibit both. The relationship is log-linear: a halving of free T4 raises TSH roughly tenfold. A mildly low T4 with a greatly raised TSH is therefore early primary hypothyroidism; the gland growing under unrestrained TSH when iodine is scarce is goitre. Autoimmunity is the commonest cause of both failure (destructive) and excess (TSH-receptor-stimulating antibodies in Graves disease, which bypass feedback entirely).

4. **Cortisol is the slow stress hormone: it mobilises fuel, maintains vascular tone and restrains inflammation.** Corticotropin-Releasing Hormone (CRH) and Adrenocorticotropic Hormone (ACTH) drive the adrenal zona fasciculata to make cortisol (~10–20 mg/day, peaking before waking, rising several-fold in illness or surgery). Cortisol raises blood glucose (gluconeogenesis, insulin antagonism, protein and fat breakdown), permits catecholamines to constrict vessels (without it, blood pressure collapses in shock), and suppresses cytokine transcription, prostaglandin synthesis and lymphocyte function. Its steady-state excess produces the catalogue of Cushing syndrome (muscle wasting, central fat, thin skin, osteoporosis, diabetes, hypertension, infection), and the same catalogue is the adverse-effect profile of glucocorticoid drugs ([anti-inflammatory drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/anti-inflammatory-and-immunomodulatory-drugs.md)).

5. **Exogenous glucocorticoid suppresses the axis, and an unprepared axis cannot meet stress.** Weeks of glucocorticoid treatment switch off CRH and ACTH, and the zona fasciculata atrophies; recovery takes months. Sudden withdrawal, or an illness or operation in such a patient, leaves no cortisol for a response that needs it: hypotension, hypoglycaemia and death from adrenal crisis. Primary adrenal failure (Addison disease) loses aldosterone as well, so adds salt wasting, hyperkalaemia and the skin pigmentation of unrestrained ACTH, which shares a precursor with melanocyte-stimulating hormone ([adrenal disorders](../../12-organ-system-specialties/05-endocrinology/adrenal-pituitary-and-gonadal-disorders.md)).

6. **The cortex is zoned by enzyme, so one precursor yields three hormone classes and one enzyme block has predictable results.** All three products come from cholesterol: the outer zona glomerulosa lacks the 17-hydroxylase needed for cortisol and makes aldosterone under angiotensin II and potassium (not ACTH); the fasciculata makes cortisol; the inner reticularis makes adrenal androgens. A block in 21-hydroxylase (the commonest congenital adrenal hyperplasia) cuts cortisol and aldosterone, ACTH rises, and precursors are diverted to androgens: virilisation plus salt wasting, deduced from the pathway alone.

7. **The medulla is a sympathetic ganglion that secretes into blood.** Preganglionic sympathetic fibres release acetylcholine onto chromaffin cells, which release adrenaline (~80%) and noradrenaline within seconds into the systemic circulation, where they act on alpha and beta receptors: heart rate and contractility up, blood diverted from gut and skin to muscle, bronchodilation, glycogenolysis and lipolysis, pupils dilated. Cortisol induces the enzyme that methylates noradrenaline to adrenaline, so the cortex sustains the medulla's capacity. Catecholamine half-life in plasma is ~2 minutes, so the response ends as fast as it began ([autonomic nervous system](../02-nervous-system/the-autonomic-nervous-system.md)).

8. **The stress response is adaptive on the timescale it evolved for and harmful when chronic.** Acute threat produces, in order, sympathetic activation and adrenaline (seconds), cortisol and growth hormone (minutes to hours), and suppression of the thyroid and reproductive axes (days), together shifting fuel to brain and muscle and deferring growth, digestion and reproduction. Sustained activation, by chronic illness, pain, sleep loss or social stress, means sustained hyperglycaemia, hypertension, immunosuppression, bone and muscle loss and depressed mood: the Cushingoid phenotype in a milder form, and part of why chronic stress tracks cardiovascular and metabolic disease ([exercise and stress physiology](../01-integrative-principles/exercise-and-stress-physiology.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3) | Prohormone secreted by the thyroid, and the active hormone made from it by tissue deiodinases |
| Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone (TSH) | Pituitary trophic hormone; log-linearly inverse to free T4, hence the most sensitive index of thyroid status |
| Goitre | Thyroid enlargement, typically from unrestrained TSH drive or stimulating antibodies |
| Cortisol | Zona fasciculata glucocorticoid; fuel mobilisation, vascular permissiveness, anti-inflammation; circadian and stress-responsive |
| ACTH | Pituitary hormone driving cortisol and adrenal androgens; shares a precursor with melanocyte-stimulating hormone |
| Aldosterone | Zona glomerulosa mineralocorticoid regulated by angiotensin II and potassium, largely independent of ACTH |
| Adrenal crisis | Circulatory collapse from absent cortisol under stress; the hazard of axis suppression and Addison disease |
| Chromaffin cell | Adrenal medullary cell; a postganglionic sympathetic neurone without an axon, secreting adrenaline into blood |
| Permissive action | A hormone (cortisol) required for another (catecholamines) to act fully |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Adrenaline is the stress hormone."** Adrenaline is the fast one; cortisol sustains the response and is the one whose absence is lethal. The two are complementary in timescale, not synonyms.
- **"Thyroxine is the active thyroid hormone."** T4 is mostly a prohormone; tissues make their own T3 with deiodinases, which lets each organ set its own exposure and lets illness lower T3 without touching T4.
- **"Stopping a steroid is safe because the body makes its own."** After weeks of treatment it does not; the axis is suppressed and may need months to recover, during which stress can precipitate crisis.
