## Essence

The brain burns ~120 g of glucose a day, cannot store it and cannot use fatty acids, so plasma glucose must stay between roughly 4 and 8 mmol/L whether the body has just eaten 100 g of carbohydrate or has fasted for two days. Two pancreatic hormones form a push–pull controller: insulin, released when glucose is high, moves glucose into muscle and fat and tells the liver to store; glucagon, released when glucose is low, tells the liver to release and to make glucose. Insulin is the only hormone that lowers glucose, whereas several raise it, an asymmetry that reflects the evolutionary cost of hypoglycaemia (immediate brain failure) against hyperglycaemia (slow damage). Diabetes is this loop failing, by loss of insulin secretion, by resistance to its action, or both.

## Core tenets

1. **The beta cell is a glucose sensor coupled to a secretory machine.** Glucose enters the beta cell through a low-affinity transporter and is phosphorylated by glucokinase, whose Michaelis constant (~8 mmol/L) places the steepest part of its response in the physiological range. Metabolism raises the ATP/ADP ratio, closing ATP-sensitive potassium channels; the cell depolarises, voltage-gated calcium channels open and insulin granules are released. Secretion is biphasic: a rapid burst from docked granules and a slower sustained phase. Sulfonylureas close the same potassium channel pharmacologically; the loss of the first phase is the earliest beta-cell defect in type 2 [diabetes](../../12-organ-system-specialties/05-endocrinology/diabetes-mellitus.md).

2. **Insulin acts through a receptor tyrosine kinase to move glucose in and switch metabolism to storage.** Receptor autophosphorylation recruits insulin receptor substrate proteins and the phosphoinositide 3-kinase pathway, which translocates GLUT4 transporters to the surface of muscle and fat cells within minutes; this is the step that lowers plasma glucose. In parallel, insulin activates glycogen synthase, lipogenesis and protein synthesis and inhibits glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, lipolysis and ketogenesis. Insulin is thus the fed-state signal for all three fuels, not only for glucose ([fed–fasted cycle](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/02-metabolism/metabolic-integration-and-the-fed-fasted-cycle.md)).

3. **The liver is the controlled plant; muscle and fat are the sinks.** Insulin reaches the liver first through the portal vein at concentrations two to three times systemic, and the liver clears about half of it. In the fed state insulin suppresses hepatic glucose output and promotes glycogen storage; in fasting, glucagon raises cyclic adenosine monophosphate in hepatocytes, activating glycogen phosphorylase and the gluconeogenic enzymes, and the liver releases ~2 mg/kg/min of glucose, most of it for the brain. Hepatic insulin resistance means the liver keeps making glucose through a meal; this unsuppressed output is the main cause of fasting hyperglycaemia in type 2 diabetes ([the liver](../06-gastrointestinal-system/the-liver-as-metabolic-hub.md)).

4. **Glucagon and insulin are mutually antagonistic, and the ratio is the signal.** Alpha cells release glucagon when glucose and insulin fall and when amino acids rise; insulin from neighbouring beta cells inhibits them directly within the islet. The insulin/glucagon ratio, rather than either level, sets hepatic direction. A protein meal raises both (so amino acids are stored without hypoglycaemia); fasting lowers insulin and raises glucagon; in type 1 diabetes the lack of intra-islet insulin leaves glucagon unrestrained, which contributes to hyperglycaemia and makes ketoacidosis possible.

5. **Counter-regulation is layered, because hypoglycaemia is the greater danger.** As glucose falls below ~4.5 mmol/L, insulin secretion stops; below ~3.8, glucagon and adrenaline are released (adrenaline also causes the sweating, tremor and palpitations that warn the patient); over hours, cortisol and growth hormone reduce peripheral glucose uptake and sustain gluconeogenesis. Below ~3 mmol/L cognition fails. Repeated hypoglycaemia blunts these responses and their symptoms (hypoglycaemia unawareness), a hazard of insulin treatment, and beta-blockers mask the adrenergic warning.

6. **The gut anticipates: incretins amplify insulin for oral glucose.** Glucose by mouth provokes two to three times the insulin response of the same glucose given intravenously, because intestinal L and K cells release glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP), which potentiate glucose-stimulated secretion, suppress glucagon, slow gastric emptying and reduce appetite. Their action is glucose-dependent, so they cannot cause hypoglycaemia on their own, which is the basis of the GLP-1 receptor agonist drug class ([endocrine and metabolic drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/endocrine-and-metabolic-drugs.md)).

7. **Insulin resistance is compensated until the beta cell cannot keep up.** Excess intracellular lipid in muscle and liver, inflammatory signalling from expanded adipose tissue and other mechanisms impair insulin signalling downstream of the receptor. The beta cell compensates by secreting more, and glucose stays normal with hyperinsulinaemia, often for years, while high insulin drives the dyslipidaemia, hypertension and fatty liver of the [metabolic syndrome](../../12-organ-system-specialties/05-endocrinology/obesity-lipids-and-metabolic-syndrome.md). Type 2 diabetes begins when secretion falls short of the demand: it is a disease of two defects, and the second is progressive.

8. **Chronic hyperglycaemia injures through the chemistry of glucose itself.** Glucose reacts non-enzymatically with proteins, and the stable products (glycated haemoglobin HbA1c, advanced glycation end-products) record the average concentration over weeks and stiffen and inflame vessels; glucose flux through the polyol and hexosamine pathways and excess mitochondrial superoxide in cells that cannot restrict glucose uptake (endothelium, nerve, retina, glomerulus) explain why complications cluster in those tissues. The regulated variable, when it escapes regulation, becomes the toxin.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Glucokinase | Beta-cell and hepatic glucose sensor; its low affinity tunes the response to the physiological range |
| ATP-sensitive potassium channel | Links beta-cell metabolism to depolarisation and insulin release; sulfonylurea target |
| GLUT4 | Insulin-regulated glucose transporter in muscle and fat; moved to the surface within minutes |
| Hepatic glucose output | Glycogenolysis plus gluconeogenesis; the controlled variable in fasting, suppressed by insulin |
| Insulin/glucagon ratio | The signal that sets the liver to storage or release |
| Counter-regulation | Glucagon, adrenaline, cortisol and growth hormone raising glucose during hypoglycaemia |
| Incretin effect | Greater insulin response to oral than intravenous glucose, via GLP-1 and GIP |
| Insulin resistance | Reduced response to a given insulin concentration, compensated by hypersecretion until it fails |
| HbA1c | Glycated haemoglobin; an integral of plasma glucose over ~8–12 weeks |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Insulin's job is to lower blood glucose."** Its job is to signal the fed state to every fuel pathway; lowering glucose is one consequence. Insulin deficiency therefore causes lipolysis and ketoacidosis as well as hyperglycaemia.
- **"Type 2 diabetes is caused by eating sugar."** It is insulin resistance (largely from excess adiposity and inactivity, on a genetic background) plus a beta cell that fails to compensate; the glucose rise is the result, not the cause.
- **"The body regulates glucose symmetrically."** One hormone lowers it, four raise it; the system is built to fear hypoglycaemia, which is why insulin treatment carries that specific hazard.
