# Tubular transport and the countercurrent system

## Essence

The tubule turns 180 L of protein-free plasma a day into 1–2 L of urine whose composition is exactly what the body needs to lose. It does this with one energy source, the basolateral sodium–potassium pump (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase), which keeps intracellular sodium low so that sodium pours in across the apical membrane through a different transporter in each segment, dragging other solutes with it. Water follows osmotically where the epithelium is permeable and is withheld where it is not. The loop of Henle exploits this separation of salt and water movement to build a countercurrent multiplier that makes the medullary interstitium hyperosmotic, so that the final urine can be concentrated to four times plasma or diluted to a fifth of it, depending only on whether the collecting duct is told to be water-permeable.

## Core tenets

1. **One pump powers everything; the apical transporter decides what is reabsorbed.** Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase on the blood-facing membrane exports sodium and keeps the cell at ~15 mmol/L sodium against ~140 mmol/L in the lumen. That gradient is spent on the apical side by segment-specific proteins: sodium–glucose and sodium–amino-acid cotransporters and the sodium–hydrogen exchanger in the proximal tubule, the Na⁺-K⁺-2Cl⁻ cotransporter in the thick ascending limb, the Na⁺-Cl⁻ cotransporter in the distal tubule and the epithelial sodium channel in the collecting duct. Each is a drug target: the loop, thiazide and potassium-sparing diuretics are named for exactly these segments ([cardiovascular drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/autonomic-and-cardiovascular-drugs.md)).

2. **The proximal tubule does bulk, iso-osmotic reclamation.** It reabsorbs ~65% of filtered sodium and water, nearly all glucose, amino acids and bicarbonate (via carbonic anhydrase, hydrogen secretion and carbon dioxide diffusion), and most phosphate. Its leaky tight junctions and water channels mean water follows solute instantly, so tubular fluid stays iso-osmotic with plasma. It also *secretes* organic anions and cations (urate, many drugs, creatinine in small amounts) from blood into the lumen, which is why clearance of some drugs exceeds GFR.

3. **Reabsorption saturates: there is a transport maximum.** Carrier-mediated transport obeys saturation kinetics. For glucose the transporters are saturated at a filtered load reached when plasma glucose is ~10–11 mmol/L; above that, glucose spills into urine and drags water with it osmotically. This explains the polyuria and thirst of untreated [diabetes](../../12-organ-system-specialties/05-endocrinology/diabetes-mellitus.md) and the mechanism of drugs that block the proximal sodium–glucose cotransporter to lower the threshold deliberately.

4. **The loop of Henle separates salt from water, and that separation is the engine of concentration.** The descending limb is permeable to water but not salt; the ascending limb is impermeable to water and actively pumps sodium chloride out. Fluid entering the loop is iso-osmotic (~300 mOsm/kg); as salt is pumped from the ascending limb into the interstitium, water leaves the descending limb to join it, so fluid turning the hairpin is concentrated and fluid leaving the ascending limb is dilute (~100 mOsm/kg). The single effect (a ~200 mOsm/kg step between limbs at any level) is multiplied along the length of the loop because flow is countercurrent: each level starts from the output of the level above.

   ```
   cortex  300 | 300 |          interstitium
          desc. asc.             300
   ------>  400 | 200 ------>    400
            600 | 400            600
            900 | 700            900
   medulla 1200 | 1000 (turn)   1200   <- deepest papilla, ~4 x plasma
   water leaves descending limb; NaCl pumped out of ascending limb
   ```

5. **Urea adds to the medullary gradient, and the vasa recta preserve it.** The inner medullary collecting duct is urea-permeable under Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH), so urea recycles into the deep interstitium and contributes up to half of its osmolality in antidiuresis. The medullary capillaries (vasa recta) run as hairpins too, exchanging solute and water between descending and ascending vessels so that blood leaves only slightly hyperosmotic and does not wash the gradient away. The price is that medullary blood flow must be low, leaving the thick ascending limb, the most oxygen-hungry segment, poised at the edge of hypoxia and first to die in [acute kidney injury](../../12-organ-system-specialties/03-nephrology/acute-kidney-injury.md).

6. **The distal nephron fine-tunes under hormonal command.** The distal convoluted tubule and collecting duct handle the last ~10% of sodium but with the highest regulatory gain. Aldosterone increases the number of epithelial sodium channels and pumps in principal cells, trading sodium reabsorption for potassium secretion. ADH inserts aquaporin-2 water channels into the collecting duct's apical membrane. Intercalated cells secrete acid or bicarbonate. Because these segments decide the final urine, a small change in their transport is a large change in excretion.

7. **Concentration and dilution are the same machine with one switch.** Fluid entering the collecting duct is already dilute. With no ADH, the duct is water-tight: dilute fluid passes through the hyperosmotic medulla unchanged and urine can be as dilute as ~50 mOsm/kg at up to ~20 L/day. With maximal ADH, aquaporins let water equilibrate with the interstitium and urine reaches ~1,200 mOsm/kg at ~0.5 L/day. Free-water clearance, positive or negative, is therefore set by the gradient built by the loop and gated by ADH.

8. **Transport costs oxygen in proportion to sodium reabsorbed.** Roughly one ATP moves three sodium ions, and ~25,000 mmol of sodium are reclaimed daily, so renal oxygen consumption tracks GFR times fractional reabsorption. A kidney that filters less also works less, which is one reason a hypoperfused kidney shutting down filtration is protective in the short term and why tubular cells, not glomeruli, bear the brunt of ischaemia.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase | Basolateral pump that creates the low intracellular sodium powering all tubular reabsorption |
| Transport maximum (Tm) | Saturation of carrier-mediated reabsorption, above which the solute appears in urine |
| Single effect | The ~200 mOsm/kg gradient the ascending limb can generate between tubular fluid and interstitium |
| Countercurrent multiplier | Opposed flow in the loop of Henle that stacks single effects into a 300 to 1,200 mOsm/kg axial gradient |
| Countercurrent exchanger | The vasa recta's hairpin arrangement that preserves the gradient while supplying the medulla |
| Aquaporin-2 | ADH-regulated water channel of the collecting duct |
| Principal cell | Collecting duct cell that reabsorbs sodium and secretes potassium under aldosterone |
| Intercalated cell | Collecting duct cell that secretes acid (type A) or bicarbonate (type B) |
| Free-water clearance | Urine volume minus the volume that would be iso-osmotic with plasma; positive in dilution, negative in concentration |

## Common misconceptions

- **"The kidney concentrates urine by pumping water."** No water pump exists; water moves only by osmosis. The loop pumps salt to build a gradient, and ADH opens channels so water can follow it.
- **"ADH makes the kidney reabsorb more fluid in general."** ADH changes only water permeability of the collecting duct; it does not touch bulk sodium handling. Volume is regulated through sodium (aldosterone and haemodynamics); osmolality is regulated through water (ADH).
- **"Diuretics work by increasing filtration."** They block tubular reabsorption; filtered load barely changes. Their power depends on which segment they block and whether downstream segments can compensate.
