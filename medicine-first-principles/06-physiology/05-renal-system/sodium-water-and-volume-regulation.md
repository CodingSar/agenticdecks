## Essence

The body regulates two different quantities with two different loops that are easy to confuse. **Osmolality** (how concentrated the body fluids are) is sensed by hypothalamic osmoreceptors and corrected by water: thirst and Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH). **Effective circulating volume** (how full the arterial tree is) is sensed by baroreceptors and the kidney's own afferent arteriole and corrected by sodium: the Renin–Angiotensin–Aldosterone System (RAAS), sympathetic nerves and natriuretic peptides. Sodium is the body's main extracellular osmole, so total body sodium sets extracellular volume, and water follows to hold osmolality constant. Almost every disorder of oedema, blood pressure or plasma sodium is one of these two loops doing exactly what it was designed to do in circumstances where that is the wrong answer.

## Core tenets

1. **Osmolality is regulated by water, volume by sodium.** Add salt and the body retains water until osmolality is back to ~285–295 mOsm/kg, so extracellular volume expands. Add pure water and ADH falls, so the kidney excretes it within hours and volume barely changes. The plasma sodium concentration is therefore a statement about water balance, not about sodium balance: hyponatraemia means excess water relative to sodium, whoever has what total.

2. **The osmoregulatory loop is tight, fast and has high gain.** Hypothalamic osmoreceptors detect a ~1% rise in osmolality and release ADH from the posterior pituitary within minutes; ADH inserts aquaporin-2 into the collecting duct and urine osmolality rises. A 1–2% rise also triggers thirst. Between the kidney (which can vary free-water excretion from ~0.5 to ~20 L/day) and drinking, plasma osmolality is held within ~1–2% in health. The loop fails when ADH is secreted for non-osmotic reasons (pain, nausea, hypovolaemia, some tumours), producing dilutional hyponatraemia, or when the kidney cannot respond to it.

3. **Effective circulating volume is sensed as pressure and stretch, not measured as litres.** High-pressure baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch, low-pressure receptors in the atria and the afferent arteriole's own stretch all report arterial filling. The body has no sodium sensor and no volume sensor; it infers volume from the adequacy of arterial perfusion. This is why heart failure and cirrhosis, in which total body sodium and water are grossly expanded but arterial filling is poor, are read by the kidney as hypovolaemia and met with avid sodium retention ([oedema](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/edema-and-fluid-imbalance.md), [heart failure](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/heart-failure.md)).

4. **RAAS is an amplifying cascade that converts a perfusion signal into a sodium-retaining, vasoconstricting response.** Juxtaglomerular cells release renin when afferent arteriolar pressure falls, when sympathetic beta-1 input rises, or when the macula densa senses low distal sodium chloride delivery. Renin cleaves angiotensinogen to angiotensin I; Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme (ACE), mostly in lung endothelium, makes angiotensin II. Angiotensin II constricts arterioles (efferent most), stimulates proximal sodium reabsorption, triggers thirst and ADH, and releases aldosterone from the adrenal zona glomerulosa. Aldosterone, acting over hours through gene transcription, increases sodium channels and pumps in collecting duct principal cells.

   ```
   low renal perfusion / low distal NaCl / sympathetic tone
            |
          renin -> angiotensinogen -> angiotensin I --ACE--> angiotensin II
                                                                |
            +-------------+--------------+---------------------+
            |             |              |                     |
     vasoconstriction  proximal Na+   thirst + ADH        aldosterone
     (efferent > afferent) reabsorption                  -> distal Na+ in, K+ out
            |
     restored arterial pressure -> renin falls (negative feedback)
   ```

5. **Pressure natriuresis is the long-term controller of blood pressure.** Raise arterial pressure and the kidney excretes more sodium and water until volume, and so pressure, fall back; the curve is steep, so in a normal kidney sustained hypertension is impossible unless the curve itself shifts. Guyton's argument follows: any sustained hypertension implies a kidney that needs a higher pressure to excrete the daily sodium intake, whether because of its own disease, excess RAAS or sympathetic drive, or reduced nephron number. This is why the kidney is central to [hypertension](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/hypertension-and-vascular-disease.md) and why blocking RAAS lowers pressure.

6. **Natriuretic peptides are the brake.** Atrial and B-type natriuretic peptides are released by stretched atrial and ventricular myocytes. They dilate the afferent arteriole (raising GFR), inhibit sodium reabsorption in the collecting duct, suppress renin and aldosterone, and oppose ADH. In health they trim volume after a salt load; in heart failure they are high but overwhelmed, and their plasma level becomes a marker of ventricular stretch.

7. **The loops conflict, and when they do, volume wins.** Severe hypovolaemia releases ADH regardless of osmolality, because defending perfusion matters more than defending concentration. The body accepts hyponatraemia to keep volume. Conversely a patient given large volumes of hypotonic fluid while ADH is high (post-operative, in pain) retains the water and becomes dangerously hyponatraemic. Interpreting plasma sodium always requires asking what the volume loop is doing.

8. **Sodium balance is a mass balance with a lag.** Intake (~100–200 mmol/day on a typical diet) must equal output over time. A step increase in intake produces positive balance for 3–5 days while aldosterone falls and pressure natriuresis rises, with weight gain of a kilogram or two of extracellular fluid; then a new steady state is reached at a slightly higher volume. The time constant of this adjustment is why diuretics initially cause loss, then a "braking" plateau as RAAS re-engages.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Osmolality | Solute concentration per kilogram of water, ~285–295 mOsm/kg in plasma; set by water balance |
| Effective circulating volume | The functional filling of the arterial tree as sensed by baroreceptors; may be low when total volume is high |
| Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH, vasopressin) | Posterior pituitary peptide that makes the collecting duct water-permeable; also a vasoconstrictor at high levels |
| Renin | Enzyme from juxtaglomerular cells, rate-limiting step of RAAS; released by low perfusion, low distal sodium chloride, sympathetic drive |
| Angiotensin II | Octapeptide that constricts arterioles, promotes sodium reabsorption and releases aldosterone |
| Aldosterone | Adrenal mineralocorticoid; increases distal sodium reabsorption and potassium secretion over hours |
| Natriuretic peptides | Cardiac peptides that promote sodium loss and oppose RAAS and ADH |
| Pressure natriuresis | Rising arterial pressure increases sodium excretion; the long-term set point of blood pressure |
| Hyponatraemia | Low plasma sodium concentration; a disorder of water excess relative to sodium, usually ADH-mediated |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Low plasma sodium means the patient is short of salt."** It means water excess relative to sodium. Most hyponatraemic patients have normal or increased total body sodium with inappropriate water retention.
- **"Oedema means the body has too much fluid and will excrete it."** The kidney in heart failure or cirrhosis senses under-filling and retains more; the excess sits in the wrong compartment, outside the arterial tree it is trying to fill.
- **"Thirst and ADH respond to volume."** Primarily to osmolality; volume drives them only when the deficit is large (~10% or more), at which point the osmotic set point is overridden.
