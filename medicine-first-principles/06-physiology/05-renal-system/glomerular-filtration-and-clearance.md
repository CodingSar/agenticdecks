## Essence

The kidney cleans the blood by a wasteful-looking strategy: it filters a fifth of the plasma that passes through it, about 180 L a day, into a tubule, then reabsorbs 99% of the water and almost all the solute it wants to keep. Filtration is pressure-driven ultrafiltration across the glomerular capillary wall, governed by the same Starling balance as any capillary but with a far higher hydraulic conductance. The Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) is the single number that summarises kidney function, and because it cannot be measured directly it is inferred from **clearance**: the virtual volume of plasma a substance is completely removed from per unit time. A substance that is freely filtered and neither reabsorbed nor secreted has a clearance equal to GFR, and creatinine is the everyday approximation.

## Core tenets

1. **Filtration is Starling's balance applied to a very leaky capillary.** Net filtration pressure is glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure (~60 mmHg) minus Bowman's space pressure (~15 mmHg) minus plasma oncotic pressure (~25 mmHg rising to ~35 mmHg along the capillary as water leaves). GFR = Kf × net pressure, where the filtration coefficient Kf combines membrane permeability and area. The glomerular Kf is roughly a hundred times that of a muscle capillary, so a modest net pressure of ~10 mmHg yields ~125 mL/min (~180 L/day) in an adult.

2. **The filter is a size-and-charge sieve.** Fenestrated endothelium, a negatively charged basement membrane and the slit diaphragms between podocyte foot processes pass water and small solutes freely, retard molecules above ~5 kDa and exclude albumin (~66 kDa, anionic) almost completely. The filtrate is therefore plasma without protein, which is why protein in the urine always signals a damaged filter ([glomerular and tubular diseases](../../12-organ-system-specialties/03-nephrology/glomerular-and-tubular-diseases.md)).

3. **Two resistors in series make GFR controllable independently of renal blood flow.** The afferent arteriole sits upstream of the glomerulus and the efferent arteriole downstream. Constricting the afferent lowers both flow and glomerular pressure, so GFR falls. Constricting the efferent lowers flow but raises glomerular pressure, so GFR is held or rises. Angiotensin II preferentially constricts the efferent arteriole, which is how the kidney defends GFR when perfusion pressure falls and why blocking angiotensin can drop GFR in a kidney that depends on it.

4. **Autoregulation holds GFR nearly constant across arterial pressures of ~80–180 mmHg.** Two mechanisms act on the afferent arteriole: a myogenic response (stretch opens channels, the smooth muscle contracts) and tubuloglomerular feedback, in which the macula densa senses sodium chloride delivery to the distal tubule and releases adenosine to constrict the afferent arteriole when delivery is high. The loop closes on the output (filtered sodium load), not on pressure, which is why it protects the distal tubule from being overwhelmed.

5. **Clearance turns an unmeasurable rate into a measurable one.** Clearance C = (U × V) / P: urinary concentration times urine flow divided by plasma concentration. For a marker that is filtered freely and not handled by the tubule (inulin experimentally), filtered load = excreted amount, so C = GFR. Creatinine is produced at a near-constant rate by muscle, filtered freely and only slightly secreted, so its clearance approximates GFR and, at steady state, plasma creatinine ∝ 1/GFR. Clearance is a first-order process: the kidney removes a constant *fraction* of plasma content per unit time, which gives drugs eliminated renally an exponential decay ([elimination and half-life](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/01-pharmacokinetics/elimination-half-life-and-dosing.md)).

6. **The reciprocal relationship hides early loss.** Because plasma creatinine ∝ 1/GFR, halving GFR from 120 to 60 mL/min roughly doubles creatinine from ~70 to ~140 µmol/L, but a loss from 120 to 90 mL/min moves it by only ~25 µmol/L, within the reference range. A "normal" creatinine can coexist with a quarter of nephron function gone, and a given creatinine means a different GFR in a frail elderly woman than in a muscular young man. Estimated GFR equations correct for age and sex but not for muscle mass.

7. **Clearance compared with GFR reveals tubular handling.** If a substance's clearance is below GFR it was reabsorbed (glucose, clearance ~0 until the transporters saturate); if above, it was secreted (para-aminohippurate, clearance ≈ renal plasma flow because nearly all of it is removed in one pass). Fractional excretion = clearance of X / clearance of creatinine says what fraction of the filtered load escaped reabsorption, the logic behind using fractional sodium excretion to tell a hypoperfused kidney (avid, <1%) from a damaged tubule.

8. **Filtered load, not GFR alone, is what the tubule must handle.** Filtered load = GFR × plasma concentration. At GFR 125 mL/min and plasma sodium 140 mmol/L the kidney filters ~25,000 mmol of sodium a day and must reabsorb ~99.5% of it, which costs most of the kidney's oxygen consumption. The kidney receives ~20–25% of cardiac output (~1.2 L/min) despite being ~0.5% of body mass, not because it needs the oxygen but because flow is what produces filtrate; its renal vein is relatively oxygen-rich while the medulla, supplied through a countercurrent arrangement, sits close to hypoxia.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) | Volume of plasma filtered per unit time by all glomeruli; ~125 mL/min (~180 L/day) in a young adult |
| Filtration coefficient (Kf) | Hydraulic permeability × filtering area; very high in the glomerulus |
| Net filtration pressure | Glomerular hydrostatic minus Bowman's space hydrostatic minus plasma oncotic pressure |
| Filtration fraction | GFR / renal plasma flow, ~0.2; raised efferent tone raises it |
| Clearance | (U × V) / P: the virtual plasma volume cleared of a substance per unit time |
| Tubuloglomerular feedback | Macula densa senses distal sodium chloride delivery and adjusts afferent tone |
| Filtered load | GFR × plasma concentration: the quantity presented to the tubule |
| Fractional excretion | Fraction of filtered load appearing in urine; clearance of X / clearance of creatinine |
| Estimated GFR (eGFR) | GFR inferred from plasma creatinine (or cystatin C), age and sex |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A normal creatinine means normal kidneys."** The reciprocal curve means the first 25–50% of GFR can be lost with creatinine still inside the reference range.
- **"Kidney blood flow is high because the kidney works hard."** Flow is high because filtrate volume is proportional to it; the kidney extracts only ~10% of the oxygen delivered, yet the medulla is nonetheless hypoxic because of countercurrent oxygen shunting.
- **"GFR falls whenever renal blood flow falls."** Efferent constriction by angiotensin II can hold GFR while flow drops; the filtration fraction rises. GFR and flow are coupled but separately controlled.
