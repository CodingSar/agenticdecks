## Essence

Beyond sodium and water, the kidney is the final arbiter of four quantities whose plasma concentrations must stay within narrow bands because excitable cells and the skeleton depend on them: potassium, calcium, phosphate and hydrogen ion. Each is handled by the same logic as sodium (filter, reabsorb most of it proximally, adjust the last few percent distally under hormonal control) but with its own sensor and hormone: aldosterone for potassium, Parathyroid Hormone (PTH) and vitamin D for calcium and phosphate, and the tubule's own acid-secreting cells for hydrogen ion. For acid–base the kidney does what the lung cannot: it excretes the ~70 mmol of fixed acid produced daily and regenerates the bicarbonate that buffered it, which makes it the slow, high-capacity half of acid–base control.

## Core tenets

1. **Potassium is 98% intracellular, so plasma potassium is a poor guide to total stores but a precise determinant of membrane excitability.** Total body potassium is ~3,500 mmol; only ~60 mmol is extracellular, held at 3.5–5.0 mmol/L. The ratio of intracellular to extracellular potassium sets the resting membrane potential through the Nernst relation ([membrane potential](../02-nervous-system/membrane-potential-and-the-action-potential.md)). Doubling plasma potassium depolarises cells by ~18 mV, enough to inactivate sodium channels and silence the heart; halving it hyperpolarises and promotes ectopic rhythms ([arrhythmias](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/arrhythmias.md)). A meal can deliver 40 mmol, two-thirds of the entire extracellular pool, so a fast internal buffer is essential.

2. **Internal shifts buffer potassium within minutes; the kidney balances it within hours to days.** Insulin and beta-2 adrenergic stimulation drive potassium into cells via Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase; acidosis pushes it out as hydrogen ions enter in exchange. These shifts explain why intravenous insulin lowers plasma potassium and why acidaemia raises it. External balance is renal: ~90% of excreted potassium comes from secretion by collecting duct principal cells, driven by aldosterone, by the lumen-negative potential created by sodium entry, and by tubular flow rate. High distal sodium delivery plus high aldosterone (as with loop diuretics) wastes potassium; low delivery or aldosterone blockade retains it.

3. **Aldosterone serves two masters, and potassium regulates it directly.** Hyperkalaemia stimulates the adrenal zona glomerulosa to release aldosterone without any involvement of renin, closing a loop that increases potassium secretion. Angiotensin II does the same for volume. The kidney reconciles the two by varying sodium delivery: in hypovolaemia, proximal avidity leaves little sodium for the distal exchange, so aldosterone is high but potassium is spared. Drugs that block the system (ACE inhibitors, mineralocorticoid antagonists) and kidney failure both cause hyperkalaemia for the same reason.

4. **Calcium is regulated through its ionised fraction by PTH, and the kidney is one of three effector organs.** Of plasma calcium (~2.2–2.6 mmol/L total), ~50% is free ionised, ~40% bound to albumin and ~10% complexed; only the ionised part is sensed and biologically active, and acidosis raises it by displacing calcium from albumin. The parathyroid calcium-sensing receptor releases PTH within minutes of a fall in ionised calcium. PTH raises calcium by three routes: bone resorption, increased distal tubular calcium reabsorption, and stimulation of renal 1α-hydroxylase to make active vitamin D (calcitriol), which increases gut absorption. The hormone logic is covered in [calcium and bone mineral metabolism](../07-endocrine-system/calcium-and-bone-mineral-metabolism.md); here the point is that distal calcium reabsorption is PTH-sensitive and separable from sodium, whereas proximal and loop calcium reabsorption are passive and follow sodium, which is why loop diuretics waste calcium and thiazides retain it.

5. **Phosphate is controlled by what the proximal tubule declines to reabsorb.** ~80–90% of filtered phosphate is reclaimed proximally by sodium–phosphate cotransporters. PTH and Fibroblast Growth Factor 23 (FGF23, from bone) remove those transporters from the membrane, so both are phosphaturic; calcitriol raises phosphate absorption from the gut. As GFR falls in [chronic kidney disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/03-nephrology/chronic-kidney-disease-and-dialysis.md), phosphate retention drives FGF23 and PTH up and calcitriol down, producing the secondary hyperparathyroidism and bone disease that are a direct consequence of lost nephrons.

6. **The kidney's acid–base job is to regenerate bicarbonate, not merely to excrete acid.** Metabolism produces ~15,000 mmol of carbon dioxide daily, which the lungs remove, and ~50–100 mmol of non-volatile acid (sulphuric from sulphur amino acids, phosphoric, organic acids) that consumes an equal amount of bicarbonate. The kidney must first reclaim the ~4,500 mmol of filtered bicarbonate each day (proximal tubule, via carbonic anhydrase and the sodium–hydrogen exchanger; otherwise it would be lost in minutes) and then make ~70 mmol of new bicarbonate by secreting hydrogen ions into the distal lumen, where they must be trapped on a buffer.

7. **Urinary buffers set the capacity; ammonium is the adjustable one.** Free hydrogen ion in urine is trivial: even at the minimum urine pH of ~4.5, it is ~0.03 mmol/L. Secreted acid is carried as titratable acid (mostly phosphate, ~30 mmol/day, limited by filtered phosphate) and as ammonium (~40 mmol/day, can rise fivefold). Proximal cells make ammonium from glutamine, generating bicarbonate in the process; the ammonium is trapped in the acidic lumen of the collecting duct. Chronic acidosis up-regulates ammoniagenesis over several days, which is why the renal response to an acid load is slow compared with the respiratory response but much larger in capacity ([acid–base physiology](../01-integrative-principles/acid-base-physiology.md)).

8. **Potassium and acid–base are coupled in the distal nephron.** Hypokalaemia stimulates hydrogen secretion and ammoniagenesis and so maintains alkalosis; hyperkalaemia suppresses ammoniagenesis and causes acidosis. Aldosterone drives both potassium and hydrogen secretion, so aldosterone excess gives hypokalaemic alkalosis and deficiency gives hyperkalaemic acidosis. Any potassium disorder should prompt a look at the bicarbonate and vice versa.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Transcellular potassium shift | Movement of potassium between cells and extracellular fluid driven by insulin, catecholamines and pH |
| Principal cell potassium secretion | Collecting duct potassium loss, driven by aldosterone, lumen negativity from sodium entry, and flow |
| Ionised calcium | The free, biologically active ~50% of plasma calcium; the quantity the parathyroid senses |
| Parathyroid Hormone (PTH) | Raises ionised calcium via bone, distal tubule and vitamin D activation; lowers phosphate |
| Calcitriol | 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, made by renal 1α-hydroxylase; increases gut calcium and phosphate absorption |
| Fibroblast Growth Factor 23 (FGF23) | Bone-derived phosphaturic hormone that also suppresses calcitriol synthesis |
| Titratable acid | Secreted hydrogen ion carried on phosphate and other filtered buffers |
| Ammoniagenesis | Proximal tubular production of ammonium from glutamine, yielding new bicarbonate; the adjustable arm of acid excretion |
| Net acid excretion | Titratable acid + ammonium − urinary bicarbonate; ~70 mmol/day on a Western diet |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Plasma potassium reflects body potassium."** It reflects the small extracellular pool and the state of transcellular shifts; a patient in diabetic ketoacidosis can have high plasma potassium and a large total deficit at the same time.
- **"The kidney corrects acidosis by excreting hydrogen ions into the urine."** The free hydrogen ion excreted is negligible; what matters is hydrogen ion trapped on ammonium and phosphate, because each trapped ion represents one bicarbonate returned to blood.
- **"Total calcium is what matters."** The parathyroid and the cell respond to ionised calcium; low albumin lowers total calcium without changing the ionised fraction, and alkalosis lowers ionised calcium (tetany) without changing the total.
