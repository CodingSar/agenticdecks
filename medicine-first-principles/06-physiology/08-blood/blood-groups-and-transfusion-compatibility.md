## Essence

Red cells carry hundreds of inherited surface molecules, and a transfusion is a transplant of tissue the recipient's immune system may recognise as foreign. Two systems matter above all the others. The ABO antigens are sugars, and every person carries pre-formed antibodies against whichever ABO sugar they lack, so an ABO-mismatched transfusion destroys the donor cells within minutes and can kill. The Rhesus D antigen is a protein against which antibodies form only after exposure, so the first mismatch is silent and the second, or a pregnancy, is the dangerous one. Compatibility is therefore a matter of antigen and antibody logic, and of the probabilities that govern which donor can safely give to which recipient. The same logic, extended to white cells and platelets, is the basis of transplantation.

## Core tenets

1. **ABO antigens are terminal sugars added by inherited enzymes.** A common precursor (the H antigen) carries an extra N-acetylgalactosamine in group A, galactose in group B, both in AB, and nothing in O, according to which glycosyltransferase alleles the person inherited. A and B are codominant and O is recessive, so two group A parents can have an O child. The antigens are also on endothelium and epithelium and in secretions, which is why ABO matters in organ transplantation too ([glycobiology](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/01-macromolecules/carbohydrates-and-glycobiology.md)).

2. **Anti-A and anti-B are "naturally occurring", present without prior transfusion, and they fix complement.** Within the first months of life, exposure to gut bacteria bearing A-like and B-like sugars provokes Immunoglobulin M (IgM) against whichever sugar the body does not itself make; the immune system tolerates its own. Group O plasma contains anti-A and anti-B, group AB plasma contains neither. IgM antibodies agglutinate cells and activate complement to lysis in the vessel, so an ABO-incompatible transfusion causes intravascular haemolysis, with free haemoglobin, shock, disseminated coagulation and renal failure within minutes to hours ([hypersensitivity](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/04-immunity-in-disease/hypersensitivity-and-allergy.md), type II). It remains the commonest cause of fatal transfusion reaction, and almost always results from giving the right unit to the wrong patient.

3. **Compatibility follows from the antibody, not the antigen: O cells go to anyone, AB plasma goes to anyone.** The recipient's plasma must not contain antibody against the donor's cells. Group O red cells have no A or B antigen and can be given to any ABO group (the "universal donor" for red cells); AB recipients have no anti-A or anti-B and can receive any group. For plasma the direction reverses: AB plasma contains no antibody and is universal; O plasma contains both and can go only to O recipients. In practice red cell units contain little plasma, so the cell rule dominates for red cells and the plasma rule for plasma and platelets.

   | Recipient | Can receive red cells from | Can receive plasma from |
   |---|---|---|
   | O | O | O, A, B, AB |
   | A | A, O | A, AB |
   | B | B, O | B, AB |
   | AB | AB, A, B, O | AB |

4. **Rhesus D is a protein antigen with no natural antibody; sensitisation requires exposure.** About 85% of Europeans and over 95% of East Asian and African populations are D-positive. A D-negative person makes anti-D (Immunoglobulin G, IgG) only after D-positive cells enter their blood, by transfusion or by fetal cells crossing the placenta. IgG does not fix complement as efficiently, so a later incompatible transfusion causes slower, extravascular haemolysis in the spleen over days, with jaundice and a falling haemoglobin rather than immediate collapse. D-negative women of childbearing age are therefore given D-negative blood, and D-negative blood is the universal choice when the group is unknown ([transfusion medicine](../../12-organ-system-specialties/06-haematology/transfusion-medicine.md)).

5. **IgG crosses the placenta, so Rhesus incompatibility is a disease of the second pregnancy.** A D-negative mother carrying a D-positive fetus is usually sensitised at delivery, when fetal cells enter her circulation. In a later D-positive pregnancy, maternal anti-D crosses the placenta, coats fetal red cells and destroys them: haemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn, with anaemia, heart failure and hydrops in utero and severe jaundice after birth. Giving the mother passive anti-D immunoglobulin around delivery and sensitising events clears fetal cells before her own immune system responds, and has reduced the disease from a major cause of perinatal death to a rarity ([pregnancy and its complications](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/pregnancy-and-its-complications.md)). ABO incompatibility between mother and fetus rarely does this, because the maternal antibody is mostly IgM and fetal ABO antigens are weakly expressed.

6. **Dozens of other antigens matter after repeated exposure.** Kell, Duffy, Kidd, MNS and others provoke IgG alloantibodies in a minority of recipients after transfusion or pregnancy; the more transfusions, the more likely. Compatibility testing therefore has three steps: ABO and D typing, an antibody screen of the recipient's plasma against panel cells, and a crossmatch of the recipient's plasma against the actual unit. Patients with sickle cell disease or thalassaemia who need lifelong transfusion accumulate antibodies and become progressively harder to match, which is why they receive extended-matched units from the start.

7. **Compatibility is also a problem in population probability.** The chance that a random donor is compatible depends on the recipient's antibody set and the antigen frequencies in the donor population: for a recipient with anti-D and anti-c, only a few percent of donors qualify; with several antibodies, the search can require national registries. Blood group frequencies differ by ancestry (group B is ~10% in Western Europe and ~25–30% in parts of South Asia; the Duffy-null phenotype is common in West African ancestry and rare elsewhere), so supply depends on the donor pool resembling the recipient population ([probability](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/01-probability-and-measurement/probability.md)).

8. **Transfusion carries risks beyond incompatibility, so the decision is a benefit–harm judgement.** Febrile reactions to donor white cells, allergic reactions to plasma proteins, transfusion-related acute lung injury from donor antibodies against recipient neutrophils, circulatory overload, iron overload after many units, and transmitted infection (now very rare with screening in high-income countries) all add to the immunological hazards. Modern practice therefore transfuses to a physiological need (oxygen delivery, haemostasis) rather than to a number, and each component (red cells, platelets, plasma, cryoprecipitate) is given for the deficiency it corrects ([blood products](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/04-non-drug-therapeutics/fluids-nutrition-and-blood-products.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ABO system | Terminal sugar antigens A and B on the H precursor; codominant alleles; naturally occurring IgM antibodies |
| Rhesus D | Protein antigen; antibodies form only after exposure and are IgG |
| Alloantibody | Antibody against an antigen the person lacks, made after transfusion or pregnancy |
| Intravascular vs extravascular haemolysis | Complement-mediated lysis in the circulation (ABO) versus splenic removal of antibody-coated cells (most IgG antibodies) |
| Universal donor / recipient | Group O D-negative red cells; group AB plasma; AB D-positive recipient of red cells |
| Antibody screen and crossmatch | Testing recipient plasma against panel cells, then against the chosen unit |
| Haemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn | Maternal IgG crossing the placenta and destroying fetal red cells |
| Anti-D prophylaxis | Passive anti-D immunoglobulin given to D-negative mothers to prevent sensitisation |
| Direct antiglobulin test | Detects antibody bound to the patient's own red cells, as in haemolytic reactions and autoimmune haemolysis |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Group O is universal for everything."** O red cells are universal; O plasma is the least universal because it contains both anti-A and anti-B.
- **"A first Rhesus-incompatible transfusion or pregnancy is dangerous."** It is usually silent; it sensitises. The danger is the next exposure, which is why prevention targets the first.
- **"Blood group antibodies are all inherited."** The antigens are inherited; anti-A and anti-B are acquired in infancy from environmental exposure, and all other alloantibodies from transfusion or pregnancy.
