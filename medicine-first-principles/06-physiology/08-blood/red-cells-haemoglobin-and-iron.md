## Essence

Oxygen is poorly soluble in water (~3 mL per litre of plasma at arterial tension), so the body packs ~150 g/L of haemoglobin into cells that carry ~200 mL of oxygen per litre of blood, seventy times more. The red cell is a bag of haemoglobin stripped of nucleus and mitochondria, built for deformability and for nothing else, and replaced at ~2 million cells per second from the marrow under the control of a single hormone, erythropoietin, which the kidney secretes in proportion to how short of oxygen it is. Iron is the atom that binds the oxygen, and because the body has no way to excrete it, iron balance is run entirely at the point of absorption and by a hormone, hepcidin, that locks iron away during inflammation. Anaemia is any failure of this loop: too few cells made, cells lost or destroyed too fast, or iron withheld.

## Core tenets

1. **Oxygen delivery is the product of flow and content, and haemoglobin is nearly all of content.** Arterial oxygen content ≈ 1.34 mL/g × haemoglobin × saturation + 0.03 mL/L/mmHg × partial pressure of oxygen. At 150 g/L and 98% saturation that is ~200 mL/L; with cardiac output ~5 L/min, delivery is ~1,000 mL/min against consumption of ~250 mL/min. Halving haemoglobin halves delivery for the same cardiac output, which the heart compensates by raising output; the symptoms of anaemia (fatigue, breathlessness, tachycardia, angina in a narrowed coronary) are this compensation reaching its limit ([supply and demand](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#supply-and-demand)).

2. **Haemoglobin's cooperativity makes it a loader at the lung and an unloader in tissue.** The four subunits switch between a low-affinity tense and a high-affinity relaxed state; binding one oxygen makes the next easier, giving the sigmoid dissociation curve. The steep part sits at tissue tensions (~20–40 mmHg) and the flat part at alveolar tension (~100 mmHg), so modest falls in alveolar oxygen cost little saturation while modest falls in tissue oxygen release much. Carbon dioxide, hydrogen ion, temperature and 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate all shift the curve right, lowering affinity exactly where tissue is working hardest (the Bohr effect); fetal haemoglobin sits to the left so it can take oxygen from maternal blood ([gas exchange and transport](../04-respiratory-system/gas-exchange-and-transport.md)). Carbon monoxide binds ~250 times more tightly than oxygen and also shifts the curve left, which is why it is doubly lethal.

3. **The red cell's shape and metabolism are a solution to a plumbing problem.** A biconcave disc ~8 µm across must pass capillaries ~3 µm wide and the splenic cords, so its membrane skeleton (spectrin, ankyrin, band 3) is built to deform and recover a million times over a 120-day life. Lacking mitochondria it runs on glycolysis, using the pentose phosphate pathway to make the reduced glutathione that keeps haemoglobin iron in the ferrous state and the membrane unoxidised. Inherited faults in the skeleton (spherocytosis), the enzymes (glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency) or the globin (sickle cell, thalassaemia) all shorten survival by making cells rigid or oxidised, and the spleen removes them ([anaemias](../../12-organ-system-specialties/06-haematology/anaemias.md)).

4. **Erythropoietin closes a loop on tissue oxygen, sensed by the kidney.** Peritubular interstitial cells in the renal cortex measure local oxygen tension through the hypoxia-inducible factor pathway: when oxygen is low, the factor escapes degradation and switches on erythropoietin transcription. The hormone acts on marrow progenitors to prevent their apoptosis, and red cell output can rise five to tenfold within a week or two. Any fall in delivery (anaemia, hypoxaemia, altitude) raises output; polycythaemia at altitude and the anaemia of [chronic kidney disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/03-nephrology/chronic-kidney-disease-and-dialysis.md), where the sensor cells are lost, are the loop working and the loop broken.

5. **Making red cells needs iron, vitamin B12 and folate, and each deficiency has a signature.** The marrow synthesises ~6 g of haemoglobin a day, needing ~20–25 mg of iron, almost all recycled from old cells by macrophages. Iron shortage limits haemoglobin per cell: small, pale cells (microcytic). Vitamin B12 or folate shortage limits DNA synthesis, so nuclei lag behind cytoplasm and large, fragile cells are released (macrocytic, megaloblastic), with the same defect in every dividing tissue. Red cell size and the reticulocyte count therefore sort anaemia into production failure, nutrient deficiency and increased loss before any specialised test ([haematology testing](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/02-laboratory-medicine/haematology-and-coagulation-testing.md)).

6. **Iron is regulated by absorption and sequestration because it cannot be excreted.** Total body iron is ~3–4 g: ~2.5 g in haemoglobin, ~1 g stored as ferritin and haemosiderin, a few milligrams in transit on transferrin. Losses (~1–2 mg/day from shed cells, more with menstruation and pregnancy) are matched by duodenal absorption of ~10% of a 10–20 mg daily intake. Free iron is toxic (it catalyses hydroxyl radical formation), so it is always bound: ferritin for storage, transferrin for transport, and the marrow's uptake via the transferrin receptor. Men have no physiological way to lose iron, so iron deficiency in a man or a post-menopausal woman is bleeding until proved otherwise, usually from the gut.

7. **Hepcidin is the iron hormone, and inflammation uses it to starve pathogens.** Hepatic hepcidin binds ferroportin, the only known cellular iron exporter, on enterocytes and macrophages, causing its degradation; iron absorption stops and recycled iron is trapped in macrophages. Hepcidin rises with iron stores (closing a loop on iron) and with interleukin-6 during inflammation; it falls with erythropoietic demand and hypoxia. The anaemia of chronic inflammation is thus functional iron deficiency with full stores, the opposite laboratory picture from true deficiency, and hereditary haemochromatosis is a hepcidin deficiency in which the gut absorbs unchecked and iron overload damages liver, pancreas, heart and joints.

8. **Red cell destruction recycles the metal and discards the ring.** Splenic and hepatic macrophages ingest senescent or damaged cells, return iron to transferrin, and open the haem ring to biliverdin, then bilirubin, which the liver conjugates and excretes in bile. Haemolysis at any rate above normal raises unconjugated bilirubin and the reticulocyte count and lowers haptoglobin, the plasma protein that binds free haemoglobin. The same pathway explains neonatal jaundice (high turnover of fetal cells, immature conjugation) and the pigment gallstones of chronic haemolysis.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Oxygen content | Haemoglobin-bound plus dissolved oxygen per litre of blood, ~200 mL/L arterial |
| Oxygen dissociation curve | Sigmoid relation of saturation to oxygen tension; shifted right by acid, carbon dioxide, heat and 2,3-BPG |
| Erythropoietin | Renal hormone, induced by hypoxia-inducible factor, that increases red cell production |
| Reticulocyte | Newly released red cell; its count measures marrow output |
| Mean cell volume | Average red cell size; sorts anaemia into microcytic, normocytic and macrocytic |
| Transferrin, ferritin | Plasma iron carrier; intracellular iron store whose plasma level reflects stores (and inflammation) |
| Ferroportin | The cellular iron exporter; the target of hepcidin |
| Hepcidin | Hepatic hormone that blocks iron absorption and release; raised by iron sufficiency and inflammation |
| Haemolysis | Premature red cell destruction; raised bilirubin, reticulocytes and lactate dehydrogenase, low haptoglobin |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Anaemia means iron deficiency."** Iron deficiency is one cause among production failure, haemolysis, nutrient deficiency and inflammation; giving iron to the anaemia of inflammation does nothing and to haemochromatosis does harm.
- **"Plasma carries meaningful oxygen."** Dissolved oxygen is ~1.5% of content at normal pressure; it becomes significant only in hyperbaric conditions or when haemoglobin is absent or blocked.
- **"Ferritin measures iron stores."** It does when inflammation is absent; as an acute-phase protein it rises with inflammation regardless of stores, so a normal ferritin can hide iron deficiency in an inflamed patient.
