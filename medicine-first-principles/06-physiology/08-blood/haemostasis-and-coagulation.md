## Essence

Blood must stay liquid inside vessels and solidify within minutes at a hole, and nowhere else. Haemostasis achieves this with a system that is poised to explode: a cascade of proteases, each activating many copies of the next, that can turn a trace of tissue factor into a gram of fibrin, held in check by inhibitors, by surfaces that are only present at injury, and by an opposing enzyme system that dissolves what has formed. Platelets provide the first plug and the surface on which the cascade assembles; fibrin provides the mesh that makes the plug permanent. Everything about bleeding, thrombosis and anticoagulant drugs follows from one question: where is the amplifier, and where are the brakes?

## Core tenets

1. **Intact endothelium is anticoagulant; injury exposes a procoagulant surface.** Healthy endothelial cells present heparan sulphate (which activates antithrombin), thrombomodulin (which turns thrombin into an anticoagulant), nitric oxide and prostacyclin (which inhibit platelets), and keep tissue factor and collagen hidden behind them. A cut, or an inflamed or atherosclerotic wall, exposes subendothelial collagen and tissue factor, and the balance flips. Virchow's triad (wall injury, stasis, hypercoagulable blood) lists the three ways the balance can flip without a cut ([thrombosis](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/thrombosis-embolism-and-infarction.md)).

2. **Primary haemostasis is platelets adhering, activating and aggregating.** Von Willebrand factor bridges exposed collagen to platelet glycoprotein Ib, catching platelets from flowing blood (essential at the high shear of arterioles). Adhered platelets activate: they change shape, release adenosine diphosphate and thromboxane A2 from granules, which recruit and activate neighbours, and they switch glycoprotein IIb/IIIa to a conformation that binds fibrinogen, cross-linking platelets into a plug within 1–3 minutes. Aspirin blocks thromboxane synthesis and the P2Y12 antagonists block the adenosine diphosphate receptor; both weaken this step and are the antiplatelet drugs of arterial disease ([cardiovascular drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/autonomic-and-cardiovascular-drugs.md)).

3. **Coagulation is an amplifying protease cascade whose output is fibrin.** Each factor is a serine protease zymogen that, once cleaved, activates many molecules of the next; the gain across the cascade is enormous. In vivo it starts with tissue factor binding factor VIIa (initiation), which makes a little factor Xa and thrombin. That first thrombin then activates factors V, VIII and XI and platelets (amplification), so that the tenase (IXa–VIIIa) and prothrombinase (Xa–Va) complexes assemble on the activated platelet surface and produce thrombin in bulk (propagation). Thrombin cleaves fibrinogen to fibrin, and factor XIIIa cross-links the strands. Calcium and phospholipid are needed at each complex, which is why citrate stops clotting in a tube.

   ```
   INITIATION    tissue factor + VIIa --> small Xa --> trace thrombin
                                              |
   AMPLIFICATION thrombin activates V, VIII, XI and platelets  (positive feedback)
                                              |
   PROPAGATION   IXa-VIIIa (tenase) --> Xa-Va (prothrombinase) --> thrombin in bulk
                                              |
                 fibrinogen --> fibrin --XIIIa--> cross-linked clot
   BRAKES        tissue factor pathway inhibitor | antithrombin | protein C/S (inactivate Va, VIIIa)
   REMOVAL       plasminogen --tPA--> plasmin --> fibrin degraded (D-dimer)
   ```

4. **The classical intrinsic and extrinsic pathways describe the test tube; the cell-based model describes the body.** The prothrombin time (tissue factor added) and activated partial thromboplastin time (contact activation) remain the standard laboratory tests and each is sensitive to a different set of factors ([coagulation testing](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/02-laboratory-medicine/haematology-and-coagulation-testing.md)), but contact factor XII deficiency prolongs the partial thromboplastin time without causing bleeding, while factor VIII or IX deficiency (haemophilia A and B) prolongs the same test and causes severe bleeding. The resolution is that in vivo everything starts with tissue factor, and factors VIII and IX matter because they form the propagation-phase tenase on platelets; XII is dispensable.

5. **Brakes are as important as the amplifier, and deficiency of a brake is a thrombotic disease.** Tissue factor pathway inhibitor shuts off initiation once a little Xa exists. Antithrombin inactivates thrombin and Xa, slowly on its own and a thousandfold faster when heparin binds it; this is how heparin works. Thrombin bound to endothelial thrombomodulin activates protein C, which with protein S destroys factors Va and VIIIa, switching off propagation. Inherited deficiency of antithrombin, protein C or protein S, or a factor V resistant to protein C (factor V Leiden), each raise venous thrombosis risk several-fold ([bleeding and clotting disorders](../../12-organ-system-specialties/06-haematology/bleeding-and-clotting-disorders.md)).

6. **Fibrinolysis removes the clot once it has served, and runs in parallel from the start.** Endothelium releases tissue plasminogen activator, which converts plasminogen bound within the clot to plasmin; plasmin digests fibrin into fragments, including D-dimer, which signals that cross-linked fibrin has formed and been dissolved somewhere. Plasminogen activator inhibitor and alpha-2-antiplasmin limit the process to the clot. Pharmacological plasminogen activators dissolve the thrombus of a stroke or myocardial infarction; antifibrinolytic drugs such as tranexamic acid hold clots together in haemorrhage.

7. **Vitamin K is the cofactor that makes four factors work, and the liver makes nearly all of them.** Factors II, VII, IX and X and proteins C and S need gamma-carboxylation of glutamate residues, a vitamin-K-dependent step, to bind calcium and phospholipid. Warfarin blocks vitamin K recycling, so functional factors fall over days as the existing ones decay (factor VII first, half-life ~6 hours; prothrombin last, ~60 hours). Liver failure, vitamin K deficiency (newborns, malabsorption, antibiotics) and warfarin all lengthen the prothrombin time by the same mechanism; the direct oral anticoagulants instead inhibit thrombin or factor Xa immediately and reversibly.

8. **The system fails in two opposite directions, and can do both at once.** Too little clotting (platelet deficiency or dysfunction, factor deficiency, anticoagulants) causes bleeding, with a pattern that identifies the step: mucosal and skin bleeding for platelet problems, joint and deep muscle bleeding for factor deficiencies. Too much (loss of a brake, stasis, inflamed endothelium, cancer, pregnancy, oestrogens) causes thrombosis. Disseminated intravascular coagulation, in sepsis or obstetric catastrophe, is both: tissue factor exposure everywhere consumes platelets and factors in microvascular thrombi while the patient bleeds from every puncture ([sepsis](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/hemorrhage-shock-and-sepsis.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Virchow's triad | Endothelial injury, stasis and hypercoagulability: the three routes to thrombosis |
| Von Willebrand factor | Multimeric protein tethering platelets to collagen under shear and carrying factor VIII |
| Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa | Platelet fibrinogen receptor whose activation allows aggregation |
| Tissue factor | Membrane protein outside the vessel lumen that starts coagulation on contact with blood |
| Tenase and prothrombinase | Platelet-surface enzyme complexes (IXa–VIIIa, Xa–Va) that produce bulk thrombin |
| Antithrombin | Plasma inhibitor of thrombin and Xa; the effector of heparin |
| Protein C and S | Thrombin-activated brake that destroys Va and VIIIa |
| Vitamin K gamma-carboxylation | Modification that lets factors II, VII, IX, X and proteins C, S bind calcium; blocked by warfarin |
| D-dimer | Fragment of cross-linked fibrin released by plasmin; evidence that clot has formed and lysed |

## Common misconceptions

- **"The intrinsic pathway starts clotting in the body."** Clotting in vivo starts with tissue factor; the contact pathway matters in the test tube and on artificial surfaces, and its first factor can be absent without bleeding.
- **"Anticoagulants dissolve clots."** They stop clots growing and new ones forming; only fibrinolysis, natural or pharmacological, removes fibrin.
- **"A long clotting time means the patient will bleed."** Factor XII deficiency and lupus anticoagulant both prolong tests without bleeding (the latter promotes thrombosis); the test measures the tube, the body measures the platelet surface.
