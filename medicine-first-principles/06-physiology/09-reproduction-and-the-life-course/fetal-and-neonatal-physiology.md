## Essence

The fetus is not a small adult. It gets oxygen from a placenta, not lungs, so its circulation routes blood around the lungs and through the liver; its lungs are full of fluid; its temperature is the mother's; its glucose comes continuously by diffusion; its immunity is borrowed. At birth, in the space of a few breaths, all of this must change: the lungs must inflate and take over gas exchange, the circulatory shunts must close so that blood goes through the lungs, the newborn must make its own heat and its own glucose, and its kidneys, liver and gut must process what the placenta used to. The transition at birth is the most dangerous physiological event of a normal life, and most neonatal illness is one part of it failing or being incomplete, with prematurity the commonest reason.

## Core tenets

1. **The fetal circulation is built in parallel, not in series, with three shunts that bypass the organs not yet in use.** Oxygenated blood from the placenta travels in the umbilical vein; the ductus venosus lets about half bypass the liver into the inferior vena cava. In the right atrium, this stream is directed by the crista dividens across the foramen ovale into the left atrium, so the best-oxygenated blood goes to the coronary arteries and brain. Less oxygenated blood from the superior vena cava passes into the right ventricle and pulmonary artery, but the fluid-filled, hypoxic lungs have very high vascular resistance, so ~90% of right ventricular output crosses the ductus arteriosus into the descending aorta, and from there to the body and back to the placenta through the umbilical arteries. The two ventricles pump in parallel into one circulation, the right doing most of the work.

   ```
   placenta --umbilical vein--> ductus venosus --> IVC --> right atrium
                                        foramen ovale --> left atrium --> left ventricle --> brain, coronaries
   SVC --> right atrium --> right ventricle --> pulmonary artery --ductus arteriosus--> descending aorta --> body, placenta
   lungs: high resistance, ~10% of output
   ```

2. **The first breath and the clamped cord flip the circulation within minutes.** Lung inflation and rising oxygen tension dilate the pulmonary arterioles (nitric oxide and prostacyclin release; oxygen itself relaxes them), and pulmonary vascular resistance falls several-fold, so blood flows through the lungs and returns to the left atrium. Clamping the cord removes the low-resistance placental bed, so systemic resistance rises. Left atrial pressure now exceeds right, pressing the flap of the foramen ovale shut; the ductus arteriosus, no longer supplied by placental prostaglandin and exposed to high oxygen, constricts over hours and closes anatomically within weeks. The circulation is now in series. If pulmonary resistance stays high (hypoxia, acidosis, lung disease, prematurity), blood keeps shunting right-to-left through the open ducts and the infant stays blue: persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn ([the newborn and neonatology](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/the-newborn-and-neonatology.md)).

3. **Breathing air requires surfactant, and the fetus makes enough only late.** The lungs develop as a gland secreting fluid, which keeps them distended and shapes their growth; the fluid is cleared at birth by catecholamine-driven sodium absorption and by compression in the birth canal. Alveoli can then stay open only if surface tension is reduced, and type II pneumocytes begin secreting surfactant at ~24–28 weeks, reaching sufficiency at ~34–36 weeks under cortisol's stimulus. A baby born before that needs enormous pressure to open alveoli that collapse again on every expiration: respiratory distress syndrome, now treated with antenatal maternal steroids to accelerate maturation and with instilled surfactant after birth ([ventilation and lung mechanics](../04-respiratory-system/ventilation-and-lung-mechanics.md)). The first breath itself needs pressures of ~40–60 cmH₂O to overcome surface tension and fluid viscosity.

4. **The fetus runs its oxygen economy on fetal haemoglobin and switches after birth.** Gamma-chain synthesis gives way to beta-chain in the months around birth, and the high fetal red cell mass (haematocrit ~55%) is broken down faster than an immature liver can conjugate the bilirubin: physiological jaundice peaks at day 3–5. Bilirubin that exceeds albumin's binding capacity enters the brain and damages the basal ganglia (kernicterus), so jaundice in the first day, or above a threshold, is treated with light that isomerises bilirubin to a water-soluble form. The newborn is also born with vitamin K stores too low to support clotting, hence the routine injection.

5. **The placenta's continuous supply ends, and the newborn must defend glucose and temperature itself.** Fetal glucose arrives by facilitated diffusion and the fetus does not need gluconeogenesis; at birth, with insulin still high and glycogen stores limited, glucose falls over the first hours until glucagon and catecholamines mobilise liver glycogen and fat. Infants of diabetic mothers (hyperinsulinaemic), premature and growth-restricted infants (no stores) are the ones who become hypoglycaemic. Heat is lost rapidly because the surface-to-volume ratio is ~3 times an adult's, the skin is wet and the infant cannot shiver; it relies on brown adipose tissue, in which uncoupling protein 1 turns fat oxidation directly into heat, driven by noradrenaline. Cold stress raises oxygen consumption, worsens acidosis and keeps pulmonary resistance high, so drying and warming is the first act of resuscitation ([thermoregulation](../01-integrative-principles/thermoregulation-and-energy-balance.md), [scale and surface-to-volume](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#scale-and-surface-to-volume)).

6. **The newborn kidney, liver and gut are immature in predictable ways.** Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) at birth is ~25% of the adult value corrected for surface area and reaches adult levels by ~2 years; concentrating ability is limited (maximum ~600–800 mOsm/kg), so the infant dehydrates and overloads easily and renally cleared drugs need adjusted dosing. Hepatic conjugation and cytochrome P450 enzymes are immature for weeks, prolonging the half-life of many drugs. The gut is sterile at birth and colonised within days from the birth canal, skin and milk; the ileum absorbs intact Immunoglobulin A (IgA) and other milk proteins; the premature gut is vulnerable to necrotising enterocolitis, an ischaemic and inflammatory injury of a poorly perfused, newly colonised bowel.

7. **Immunity is borrowed first, then built.** Maternal Immunoglobulin G (IgG) transferred across the placenta mainly in the third trimester gives the term infant antibody levels near the mother's, which decay with a half-life of ~3–4 weeks and reach their nadir at ~3–6 months, the age at which infections rise and the vaccination schedule is timed to replace them. Premature infants miss much of this transfer. Breast milk adds secretory IgA, lactoferrin and oligosaccharides at the mucosal surface. The newborn's own adaptive responses are present but naive and biased away from inflammation, which makes sepsis in the first weeks both common and subtle ([immunological memory and vaccination](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/03-adaptive-immunity/immunological-memory-and-vaccination.md)).

8. **Fetal physiology is designed for in utero conditions, so persistence of fetal features after birth is a disease, and so is their absence before it.** A patent ductus arteriosus in a premature infant steals blood from the systemic circulation into the lungs; a foramen ovale that stays open allows paradoxical embolism in adult life; premature closure of the ductus in utero (as with maternal non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, which block the prostaglandins that keep it open) loads the fetal right ventricle. Fetal structures are not defects; they are the correct design for a body with a placenta. Much of [congenital heart disease](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/congenital-and-genetic-disease-in-childhood.md) is understood by asking which fetal shunt the malformation depends on and what happens when it closes.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ductus venosus, foramen ovale, ductus arteriosus | The three fetal shunts bypassing liver, lungs and lungs respectively |
| Pulmonary vascular resistance | High in the fluid-filled fetal lung; falls with inflation and oxygen at birth, the hinge of the transition |
| Persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn | Failure of that fall, with continued right-to-left shunting and cyanosis |
| Surfactant | Type II pneumocyte phospholipid that lowers alveolar surface tension; sufficient from ~34–36 weeks |
| Respiratory distress syndrome | Surfactant deficiency of prematurity: stiff, collapsing lungs |
| Physiological jaundice | Unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia from high fetal red cell turnover and immature conjugation, peaking at day 3–5 |
| Brown adipose tissue | Heat-producing fat using uncoupling protein 1; the newborn's substitute for shivering |
| Passive immunity | Maternal IgG transferred across the placenta, waning over the first months |
| Prematurity | Birth before 37 weeks; the commonest reason the transition fails |

## Common misconceptions

- **"The fetus is hypoxic and struggling."** It is adapted to low oxygen tension with a left-shifted haemoglobin, high haematocrit and a circulation that sends the best blood to the brain; it is the transition, not fetal life, that is precarious.
- **"The foramen ovale closes at birth."** It is pressed shut by the pressure reversal within minutes but fuses over months, and remains probe-patent in ~25% of adults.
- **"Newborns are too small to be given adult drugs in proportion."** They are not scaled-down adults: immature kidneys, liver, protein binding and body water fraction change clearance and distribution in ways that weight alone does not predict.
