## Essence

The gut is a muscular tube about nine metres long that must move food forward at the pace digestion and absorption can handle, mix it with secretions, hold it when storage is needed and expel what is left. It does this with its own nervous system. The enteric nervous system contains some 500 million neurones, as many as the spinal cord, arranged in two plexuses within the gut wall, and it can run peristalsis, mixing and secretion with no input from the brain. The central nervous system modulates rather than commands: the vagus and sympathetic nerves set the gain, and hormones from the gut's own endocrine cells coordinate distant segments. Smooth muscle provides the oscillator (a rhythmic slow wave of membrane potential), the enteric circuits provide the programmes, and the autonomic and endocrine systems decide which programme runs.

## Core tenets

1. **Gut smooth muscle is an electrical oscillator whose frequency is set by pacemaker cells, not by nerves.** Interstitial cells of Cajal, coupled to smooth muscle by gap junctions, generate a slow wave of depolarisation at a fixed regional frequency: ~3 per minute in the stomach, ~12 per minute in the duodenum falling to ~8 in the ileum. A slow wave alone produces no contraction. Only when excitatory input raises the plateau above threshold do calcium action potentials fire on its crest, and the muscle contracts. Nerves and hormones therefore set the *force* and *whether* of contraction; the slow wave sets the *maximum rate* and the direction of propagation ([oscillations](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/03-systems-and-models/nonlinear-dynamics-oscillations-and-networks.md)).

2. **Peristalsis is a local reflex wired into the wall.** Stretch or mucosal stroking excites intrinsic sensory neurones; through interneurones they excite ascending motor neurones that release acetylcholine and tachykinins to contract muscle behind the bolus, and descending motor neurones that release nitric oxide and vasoactive intestinal peptide to relax muscle ahead of it. Bayliss and Starling described this "law of the intestine" in 1899 in a gut with all extrinsic nerves cut. The polarity is built into the circuit, so transit is directional without any central map.

   ```
            bolus
   <--------[####]-------->  direction of flow
   contract behind     relax ahead
   (ACh, tachykinins)  (NO, VIP)
   ascending excitatory    descending inhibitory
            \   enteric sensory neurone   /
             --- stretch / mucosal signal ---
   ```

3. **The two plexuses divide the work.** The myenteric plexus between the muscle layers controls motility; the submucosal plexus controls secretion, blood flow and the mucosal epithelium. Both contain sensory neurones, interneurones and motor neurones and both receive autonomic input. Loss of enteric neurones in a segment (congenital absence in Hirschsprung disease, or destruction in Chagas disease) leaves muscle that cannot relax ahead of a bolus, producing functional obstruction and dilation above the lesion.

4. **The autonomic system modulates and coordinates over distance.** Parasympathetic (vagal, and sacral for the distal colon) input is mostly excitatory to enteric circuits and increases motility and secretion; sympathetic input inhibits enteric motor neurones, contracts sphincters and diverts blood flow, as in the ileus after surgery or during pain. Long reflexes run through the brainstem and prevertebral ganglia: gastric distension stimulates colonic motility (gastrocolic reflex), and distension of one bowel segment inhibits others (intestino-intestinal reflex). Emotion reaches the gut through these pathways, which is one reason stress alters bowel habit.

5. **The stomach is a reservoir and a grinder with a regulated outlet.** Receptive relaxation (vagally mediated, via nitric oxide) lets the proximal stomach accept a litre with little pressure rise. The distal stomach grinds food against a nearly closed pylorus, retropelling particles until they are under ~2 mm. Emptying is then governed by feedback from duodenal sensors: acid, fat, hypertonicity and distension release cholecystokinin and secretin and activate vagal afferents that slow gastric emptying. Liquids leave with a half-time of ~20 minutes, solids over 1–3 hours, fat slowest of all. The duodenum thus never receives more than it can neutralise and digest.

6. **The small intestine alternates mixing with transport, and sweeps itself clean between meals.** During a meal, segmentation (stationary rings) mixes chyme with enzymes and exposes it to the mucosa; short peristaltic waves move it slowly, taking 2–4 hours for a meal to traverse ~6 m. During fasting, the migrating motor complex, a band of intense contraction, passes from stomach to ileum every ~90 minutes, triggered by motilin, clearing residue and bacteria distally. Its absence allows bacterial overgrowth.

7. **Sphincters are tonically contracted gates opened by inhibitory enteric input.** The lower oesophageal, pyloric, ileocaecal and internal anal sphincters hold pressure by myogenic tone and relax when a peristaltic wave arrives, through the same descending nitric oxide neurones. The lower oesophageal sphincter relaxes transiently for swallowing and belching; relaxations that are too frequent or too long let acid reflux into an epithelium not built for it ([acid-peptic disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/04-gastroenterology-and-hepatology/acid-peptic-disease-and-gi-bleeding.md)). The external anal sphincter and the swallowing reflex are the only parts of gut motility under direct somatic control.

8. **The colon stores, salvages and defecates on command.** Colonic transit takes 1–3 days. Haustral mixing exposes contents to an epithelium that absorbs sodium and water (~1.5 L/day) and the short-chain fatty acids made by bacterial fermentation. Mass movements a few times a day propel stool into the rectum; rectal distension triggers reflex relaxation of the internal sphincter and the sensation of urge, which the external sphincter and cortex may veto. Defecation is therefore an enteric reflex gated by a somatic one, and either half can fail independently.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Enteric nervous system | The gut's intrinsic nervous system (~500 million neurones in myenteric and submucosal plexuses) able to run reflexes without the brain |
| Interstitial cells of Cajal | Pacemaker cells generating the slow wave that sets maximal contraction frequency in each region |
| Slow wave | Rhythmic smooth-muscle depolarisation; contraction occurs only when it crosses threshold and fires action potentials |
| Peristaltic reflex | Contraction behind and relaxation ahead of a bolus, wired into the enteric plexus |
| Receptive relaxation | Vagally mediated, nitric-oxide-dependent relaxation of the proximal stomach as food arrives |
| Duodenal feedback | Acid, fat, osmolality and distension in the duodenum slow gastric emptying via hormones and vagal afferents |
| Segmentation | Stationary rings of contraction that mix chyme without net propulsion |
| Migrating motor complex | Interdigestive sweep of contraction from stomach to ileum every ~90 minutes, triggered by motilin |
| Ileus | Loss of coordinated motility, typically from sympathetic inhibition after surgery, peritonitis or opioids |

## Common misconceptions

- **"The brain controls digestion."** The enteric nervous system runs it; a transplanted or denervated gut still propels food. The brain adjusts gain and coordinates distant segments.
- **"Gut contraction frequency increases when the gut is stimulated."** The slow wave fixes the maximum frequency; stimulation raises the fraction of slow waves that produce contraction and their force.
- **"Gastric emptying is a passive overflow."** It is actively braked by duodenal feedback; a fatty meal empties slowly because fat in the duodenum tells the stomach to wait.
