# The liver as metabolic hub

## Essence

Everything absorbed from the gut (except fat in chylomicrons) passes through the liver before it reaches the rest of the body, and the liver decides what the body sees. It stores and releases glucose to hold plasma concentration steady between meals, builds and exports the plasma proteins and lipoproteins, detoxifies the nitrogen from protein as urea, makes bile, and chemically transforms foreign molecules so they can be excreted. The organ's position, 1.5 kg sitting across a 1.5 L/min portal-plus-arterial blood flow, is the explanation for all of this: it is the gatekeeper between the variable, hazardous input of the gut and the constant internal environment. Its functional reserve is so large that symptoms appear only when most of it is gone, which is why liver disease is silent until it is severe.

## Core tenets

1. **The portal circulation puts the liver in series between gut and body.** The portal vein carries ~75% of hepatic blood flow, nutrient-rich, bacteria-exposed and low in oxygen; the hepatic artery supplies the remaining 25% and most of the oxygen. Blood percolates through sinusoids lined by fenestrated endothelium with no basement membrane, so plasma contacts hepatocytes directly through the space of Disse. Every absorbed drug, toxin and gut-derived bacterial product is sampled first, which gives the liver first-pass metabolism of oral drugs and makes it the body's largest filter for portal endotoxin (via Kupffer cells, the resident macrophages). Portal pressure is normally only ~5–10 mmHg above caval pressure; when fibrosis raises resistance, the back-pressure produces varices and ascites ([liver disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/04-gastroenterology-and-hepatology/liver-disease.md)).

2. **The liver buffers blood glucose by storing it as glycogen and making it from scratch.** After a meal, insulin drives glucose uptake and glycogen synthesis (hepatic glycogen ~100 g, enough for ~12–18 hours). As glucose falls, glucagon and adrenaline activate glycogenolysis and then gluconeogenesis from lactate, alanine and glycerol, so plasma glucose stays near 4–6 mmol/L through an overnight fast. The hepatocyte's glucokinase and GLUT2 are low-affinity, so flux in and out tracks plasma concentration rather than saturating, and glucose-6-phosphatase, present in liver and kidney but not muscle, is what lets the liver export free glucose. Prolonged fasting adds ketogenesis from fatty acids to supply the brain. The hormonal side is in [glucose homeostasis](../07-endocrine-system/glucose-homeostasis.md); in liver failure the buffering fails and hypoglycaemia is a late, grave sign.

3. **The liver makes most plasma proteins, so its failure shows as oncotic and clotting failure.** Albumin (~10–15 g/day, half-life ~20 days) provides ~80% of plasma oncotic pressure; low albumin lets fluid leave capillaries ([oedema](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/edema-and-fluid-imbalance.md)). The liver also makes nearly all clotting factors, the anticoagulants (protein C, S, antithrombin) and fibrinolytic proteins; factors II, VII, IX and X need hepatic vitamin K-dependent carboxylation. Factor VII's half-life is ~6 hours, so the prothrombin time lengthens within a day of acute hepatocyte failure and is the most sensitive routine marker of synthetic function ([haemostasis](../08-blood/haemostasis-and-coagulation.md)). Transport proteins (transferrin, caeruloplasmin, hormone-binding globulins), complement and acute-phase proteins come from the same source.

4. **Nitrogen is disposed of as urea, and the brain pays when it is not.** Amino acids surplus to protein synthesis are deaminated; the ammonia (toxic above ~50 µmol/L) is converted in periportal hepatocytes to urea (~30 g/day) through the urea cycle, at a cost of four ATP per molecule, and the kidney excretes it. Gut bacteria also generate ammonia from urea and protein, which the healthy liver clears on first pass. When hepatocytes are few or portal blood bypasses them through shunts, ammonia and other gut-derived substances reach the brain: hepatic encephalopathy is a failure of first-pass clearance.

5. **Bile is both an excretory route and a digestive secretion.** Hepatocytes secrete ~500 mL/day of bile into canaliculi against a concentration gradient using ATP-driven pumps: bile salts (synthesised from cholesterol, ~0.5 g/day, the body's only quantitatively important route for cholesterol disposal), phospholipid, cholesterol, conjugated bilirubin, and the metabolites of drugs and hormones. Bilirubin, from haem breakdown (~250–300 mg/day), is insoluble until the hepatocyte conjugates it with glucuronic acid; jaundice therefore sorts into pre-hepatic (too much unconjugated bilirubin made), hepatic (conjugation or uptake failing) and post-hepatic (conjugated bilirubin that cannot drain), and the pattern of bilirubin, urine colour and stool colour tells which.

6. **Drug metabolism is a two-phase strategy for making lipid-soluble molecules water-soluble.** Phase I (mostly cytochrome P450 enzymes) adds or exposes a functional group by oxidation; phase II conjugates it with glucuronide, sulphate or glutathione so the kidney or bile can excrete it. The system is broad, inducible and inhibitable: one drug can raise or lower the clearance of another by changing P450 activity, and genetic variants in P450s explain much individual variation ([drug metabolism and interactions](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/01-pharmacokinetics/drug-metabolism-and-interactions.md)). Some metabolites are more toxic than the parent (paracetamol's reactive intermediate, detoxified by glutathione until glutathione runs out), so metabolism can cause injury as well as prevent it.

7. **Lipoprotein traffic is routed through the liver.** The liver takes up chylomicron remnants, assembles very-low-density lipoprotein to export triglyceride and cholesterol to the periphery, clears low-density lipoprotein through its receptors (the step up-regulated when hepatic cholesterol synthesis is inhibited by statins), and receives cholesterol back on high-density lipoprotein. It is the only organ that can excrete cholesterol, as bile salts or free cholesterol in bile. Fat accumulates in hepatocytes whenever delivery and synthesis exceed export and oxidation, which links obesity and insulin resistance to fatty liver disease ([lipid metabolism](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/02-metabolism/lipid-metabolism.md)).

8. **The liver regenerates and has large reserve, so damage is hidden until late.** Hepatocytes are quiescent but re-enter the cell cycle after loss; two-thirds of a rat liver regrows within ~10 days, and a human donor's remnant regains near-normal mass in weeks. The liver's functions have reserve too: about 70–80% must be lost before bilirubin, albumin and clotting are clearly abnormal. The consequence is that chronic injury (alcohol, viral hepatitis, fat) progresses silently to fibrosis, and the first presentation may be a complication of portal hypertension rather than of lost function.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Portal circulation | Venous blood from gut, spleen and pancreas delivered to the liver before the systemic circulation |
| First-pass metabolism | Fraction of an oral drug removed by gut wall and liver before reaching systemic blood |
| Hepatic acinus / zonation | Periportal hepatocytes (oxygen-rich; gluconeogenesis, urea) versus perivenous (glycolysis, P450 detoxification) |
| Glucose-6-phosphatase | Enzyme letting liver and kidney export free glucose; absent in muscle |
| Urea cycle | Conversion of toxic ammonia to urea for renal excretion |
| Conjugation | Phase II addition of glucuronide, sulphate or glutathione, making bilirubin and drugs water-soluble |
| Cytochrome P450 | Family of phase I oxidising enzymes; inducible, inhibitable and genetically variable |
| Bile salts | Amphipathic cholesterol derivatives for fat emulsification and cholesterol excretion |
| Kupffer cell | Resident hepatic macrophage clearing portal endotoxin and particulates |

## Common misconceptions

- **"The liver detoxifies, so metabolism always makes drugs safer."** Phase I can create reactive intermediates; paracetamol and several other drugs injure the liver through their metabolites, not the parent compound.
- **"Liver tests measure liver function."** Transaminases measure hepatocyte injury (leak), not function; albumin, bilirubin and prothrombin time measure function, and they stay normal until most reserve is gone.
- **"Jaundice means the liver is failing."** Pre-hepatic haemolysis and post-hepatic duct obstruction both cause jaundice with a working liver; the conjugated fraction and the urine tell them apart.
