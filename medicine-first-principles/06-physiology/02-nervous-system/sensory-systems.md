## Essence

A sensory system converts one form of physical energy (pressure, stretch, temperature, photons, sound, chemicals) into a receptor potential, encodes its intensity as spike frequency and its quality as *which* fibre is firing, relays it through a small number of synapses to the thalamus and cortex, and sharpens it at every stage by inhibition and adaptation. Modality is not in the signal but in the wiring: a labelled line from a specific receptor to a specific cortical area, so that pressing on the eyeball produces light. Because the pathways cross at known levels and map the body in known order, a sensory deficit localises a lesion, which is why the neurological examination works.

## Core tenets

1. **Transduction is a channel opened by the stimulus, and the receptor's protein decides the modality.** Mechanical force opens Piezo channels in touch receptors and stretches tip links on hair cells to open their channels; warmth and cold open members of the transient receptor potential family (TRPV1 for heat and capsaicin, TRPM8 for cold and menthol); odorants and tastants bind G-protein-coupled receptors; light isomerises retinal in opsin, which through a G-protein cascade *closes* cyclic-GMP-gated channels, so photoreceptors hyperpolarise to light and release less transmitter. The receptor potential is graded, and spikes are generated only when it reaches the first spiking node.

2. **Intensity is coded by firing rate and recruitment, and the code is compressive.** Firing rate rises roughly with the logarithm or a power of stimulus intensity (Weber–Fechner, Stevens), so the body distinguishes ratios, not differences: a just-noticeable change is a constant fraction of the starting value. Stronger stimuli also recruit higher-threshold receptors. Hearing spans twelve orders of magnitude of intensity (hence the decibel scale); vision spans ten, with rods covering the dim end and cones the bright.

3. **Adaptation makes receptors report change more than state.** Rapidly adapting receptors (Pacinian corpuscles for vibration, hair-follicle receptors) fire at onset and offset only; slowly adapting ones (Merkel discs, Ruffini endings, muscle spindles, nociceptors) keep firing while the stimulus lasts. Sensation of a steady stimulus fades, which is why clothing is not felt and why a bad smell is noticed only on entering a room. Pain is the exception: nociceptors adapt little, because a persisting threat should not be ignored.

4. **Receptive fields and lateral inhibition set spatial resolution, and the cortex magnifies what has many receptors.** Each afferent reports from a patch of skin or retina; resolution is set by patch size and density (two-point discrimination ~2 mm on a fingertip, ~40 mm on the back; the fovea packs ~150,000 cones per mm²). At each relay, an active neuron inhibits its neighbours through interneurons, sharpening edges and contrast (centre–surround fields in the retina). Cortical area is proportional to receptor density, not body size, so the fingers, lips and tongue dominate the somatosensory map (the homunculus), and the map reorganises after amputation or training.

5. **The somatosensory pathways split by modality and cross at different levels, so a lesion is localised by what is lost and on which side.** Fine touch, vibration and proprioception ascend ipsilaterally in the dorsal columns and cross in the medulla; pain and temperature synapse in the dorsal horn, cross within one or two segments, and ascend in the contralateral spinothalamic tract. A hemisection of the cord therefore loses touch and position sense below the lesion on the same side and pain and temperature on the opposite side (Brown-Séquard), a dissociation no peripheral lesion can produce. All ascending pathways except smell relay in the thalamus before the cortex.

6. **Vision is optics, then a two-stage retina, then a crossing that maps the visual field onto the brain.** Cornea (~40 dioptres) and lens (~20, adjustable for accommodation) focus an inverted image on the retina; the retina is itself inverted, with photoreceptors behind the processing layers. Rods are sensitive but monochromatic and absent from the fovea; three cone types give colour from the ratio of their responses. Ganglion cells with centre–surround fields send axons through the optic nerve; fibres from the nasal retina (temporal field) cross at the chiasm, so a chiasmal lesion loses both temporal fields, while a lesion behind the chiasm loses the opposite half-field in both eyes.

7. **Hearing and balance are mechanical frequency analysis by the inner ear.** The middle ear bones match the impedance of air to fluid (area ratio and lever, ~20-fold pressure gain), without which most sound would reflect off the cochlea. The basilar membrane is stiff and narrow at the base and wide and floppy at the apex, so each frequency vibrates one place (tonotopy); outer hair cells actively amplify the vibration, and their loss (noise, ageing, aminoglycosides) is the common form of deafness. The semicircular canals sense angular acceleration and the otolith organs linear acceleration and gravity; mismatch between vestibular, visual and proprioceptive reports is experienced as vertigo and nausea.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Receptor potential | Graded voltage change in a receptor produced by the stimulus; converted to spikes downstream |
| Labelled line | The principle that modality is determined by which pathway is active, not by the signal's form |
| Adequate stimulus | The energy form a receptor is tuned to; other forms can still excite it at high intensity |
| Receptive field | The region of skin, retina or frequency space that drives one neuron |
| Lateral inhibition | Active neurons suppressing neighbours, sharpening contrast at each relay |
| Adaptation | Decline in firing during a constant stimulus; rapid in vibration receptors, slow in nociceptors |
| Weber–Fechner law | Just-noticeable difference is a constant fraction of the stimulus |
| Dorsal column / spinothalamic | Touch–vibration–proprioception (crosses in medulla) / pain–temperature (crosses in cord) |
| Tonotopy | Place coding of sound frequency along the basilar membrane and up to cortex |
| Somatotopy | Orderly mapping of the body surface onto cord, thalamus and cortex |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Sensation is a faithful copy of the stimulus."** It is a compressed, adapted, edge-enhanced code of change; constant stimuli vanish and contrasts are exaggerated.
- **"Photoreceptors fire when light arrives."** They hyperpolarise and release less transmitter; the retina signals light by a decrease.
- **"Loss of sensation on one side means a lesion on that side."** It depends on the modality and the level: pain and temperature cross in the cord, touch in the medulla.
