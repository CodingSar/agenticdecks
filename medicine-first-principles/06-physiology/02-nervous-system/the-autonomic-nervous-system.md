## Essence

The autonomic nervous system is the motor system of the viscera: it runs the heart, vessels, airways, gut, bladder, pupils, glands and metabolism without conscious command, and it is the fast effector of nearly every homeostatic loop. It has two divisions with different anatomy, transmitters and receptors. The sympathetic division mobilises the body and is organised for mass action; the parasympathetic conserves and is organised for targeted, organ-by-organ action. Most organs receive both, tonically active, so the output is a balance that can be shifted either way. Because the postganglionic transmitters act on a small set of receptor subtypes with known second messengers, the autonomic nervous system is the most predictable pharmacological target in medicine.

## Core tenets

1. **Both divisions are two-neuron chains with acetylcholine at the ganglion, and they differ in where the ganglion sits.** A preganglionic neuron in the brainstem or cord synapses on nicotinic receptors of a postganglionic neuron, which innervates the target. Sympathetic preganglionic cells lie in thoracolumbar segments T1–L2 and synapse close to the cord in the paravertebral chain or prevertebral ganglia, so a short preganglionic fibre fans out to a long postganglionic one that reaches many targets (divergence, mass action). Parasympathetic preganglionic cells lie in cranial nerves III, VII, IX and X and sacral segments S2–S4, and synapse in ganglia within or beside the target organ, so each output is local. The vagus carries about three-quarters of parasympathetic outflow, to heart, lungs and gut as far as the transverse colon.

2. **The postganglionic transmitter is noradrenaline for the sympathetic and acetylcholine for the parasympathetic, with instructive exceptions.** Sympathetic fibres to sweat glands release acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors (so anticholinergic drugs cause dry skin and heat intolerance); the adrenal medulla is a sympathetic ganglion whose cells secrete adrenaline (~80%) and noradrenaline into the blood, turning a neural discharge into a hormone that reaches every tissue including those without sympathetic nerves. Co-transmitters (adenosine triphosphate, neuropeptide Y, vasoactive intestinal peptide, nitric oxide) add slower components; erection, for example, is parasympathetic nitric oxide, not acetylcholine.

3. **The receptor subtype, with its G-protein, predicts the response and the drug effect.** α1 (Gq → inositol trisphosphate, calcium): vascular and sphincter smooth muscle contraction, pupil dilation. α2 (Gi): presynaptic inhibition of noradrenaline release; central sympathetic inhibition. β1 (Gs → cyclic AMP): faster, stronger heart; renin release. β2 (Gs): relaxation of bronchial, uterine and skeletal-muscle-vessel smooth muscle; glycogenolysis; potassium uptake into cells. β3: lipolysis, bladder relaxation. Muscarinic M2 (Gi): slows the sinus node and atrioventricular conduction. M3 (Gq): contracts bronchial, gut and bladder smooth muscle, drives glandular secretion, and releases nitric oxide from endothelium. A β-blocker, an α1-blocker and a muscarinic antagonist each have effects you can derive from this table ([autonomic drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/autonomic-and-cardiovascular-drugs.md)).

4. **Most organs are dually innervated with tonic activity in both, so control is a balance and removing one arm reveals the other.** The sinus node fires at ~100/min when denervated; resting heart rate is ~70 because vagal tone outweighs sympathetic. Atropine (muscarinic block) raises the rate; a β-blocker lowers it; in a transplanted, denervated heart it is ~100 and rises slowly in exercise only as adrenaline arrives. The two arms are not always antagonists: salivation is driven by both, and in sexual function erection is parasympathetic and emission sympathetic.

5. **Blood vessels are the exception: mostly sympathetic only, so vascular tone is set by the level of one signal.** Arterioles and veins receive tonic sympathetic vasoconstrictor fibres (α1) and almost no parasympathetic supply. Vasodilation is produced by *withdrawing* sympathetic tone, by local metabolites, or by circulating adrenaline on β2 receptors in muscle vessels. This is why the baroreflex can raise or lower resistance with a single variable, and why spinal cord injury above the sympathetic outflow or spinal anaesthesia drops blood pressure: the vessels dilate because nothing is telling them to constrict.

6. **The system is run from the hypothalamus and brainstem, and the baroreflex is its model loop.** Baroreceptor afferents (glossopharyngeal and vagus) reach the nucleus of the solitary tract; from there, inhibition of the rostral ventrolateral medulla (sympathetic drive) and excitation of the nucleus ambiguus (vagal drive) lower pressure within one or two heartbeats ([hemodynamics](../03-cardiovascular-system/hemodynamics-and-blood-pressure-regulation.md)). The hypothalamus layers temperature, volume, hunger, circadian and emotional inputs on top, and can command the whole sympathetic outflow at once (the fight-or-flight discharge) or a single channel (sympathetic activation of the gut after a meal).

7. **Autonomic failure and autonomic excess are clinical syndromes, and denervated targets become supersensitive.** Loss of sympathetic vasoconstriction (diabetic neuropathy, multiple system atrophy, drugs) gives orthostatic hypotension without the compensatory tachycardia; loss of parasympathetic tone gives a fixed heart rate, constipation, urinary retention and a dry mouth. Excess sympathetic discharge (phaeochromocytoma, autonomic dysreflexia after high cord injury, severe pain) produces hypertensive crisis. A target deprived of its transmitter increases its receptors, so a denervated organ over-responds to circulating agonists, which is why a Horner's pupil dilates excessively to dilute adrenaline.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Preganglionic / postganglionic | First (cholinergic, nicotinic synapse) and second neuron of the autonomic chain |
| Thoracolumbar / craniosacral outflow | Origin of the sympathetic (T1–L2) and parasympathetic (III, VII, IX, X; S2–S4) divisions |
| Adrenal medulla | Modified sympathetic ganglion releasing adrenaline and noradrenaline into blood |
| Adrenoceptors (α1, α2, β1, β2, β3) | Noradrenaline and adrenaline receptors; each with a characteristic G-protein and tissue |
| Muscarinic receptors (M1–M3) | Acetylcholine receptors on targets; M2 heart, M3 smooth muscle and glands |
| Tonic activity | Resting discharge in both divisions, so output can be raised or lowered |
| Vasomotor tone | Resting sympathetic constriction of vessels; its withdrawal is the main route to vasodilation |
| Baroreflex | Pressure-sensing loop through the nucleus of the solitary tract that adjusts both divisions |
| Denervation supersensitivity | Upregulation of receptors on a target that has lost its innervation |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Sympathetic means excitation, parasympathetic means inhibition."** The sympathetic system inhibits gut motility and the parasympathetic excites it; the receptor and tissue decide.
- **"The two divisions always oppose each other."** Many targets have one supply (vessels, sweat glands, adrenal medulla), and some functions use both in sequence.
- **"Autonomic means beyond control."** Breathing, bladder and, with training, heart rate are all subject to higher input; the hypothalamus and cortex sit on top of the reflexes.
