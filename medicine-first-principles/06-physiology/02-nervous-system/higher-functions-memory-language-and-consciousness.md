## Essence

The cerebral cortex, some 16 billion neurons in a sheet a few millimetres thick, adds to the reflexes and maps below it the capacities that define a person: storing experience, manipulating symbols, directing attention, and being awake and aware. Each depends on identifiable structures whose loss produces a specific deficit, which is how the functions were discovered: memory needs the medial temporal lobe to be formed but not kept; language lives mostly in two left-hemisphere regions; consciousness has two separable components, arousal from the brainstem and content from the thalamocortical system. All of it runs on a tissue that is 2% of body mass, uses 20% of resting oxygen and glucose, stores neither, and fails within ten seconds of losing its blood supply.

## Core tenets

1. **The brain is the organ with the least tolerance of interrupted supply.** Cerebral blood flow is ~50 mL per 100 g per minute (~750 mL/min, ~15% of cardiac output), autoregulated between mean pressures of ~60 and 150 mmHg and driven by arterial carbon dioxide (hyperventilation constricts, hypercapnia dilates). Neurons burn glucose almost exclusively and keep no reserve; consciousness is lost ~10 s after circulatory arrest and irreversible injury begins within minutes. Hypoglycaemia, hypoxia and hypotension therefore present first as confusion.

2. **Memory is several systems, and the medial temporal lobe is needed to form declarative memories but not to keep them.** Patient H.M., whose hippocampi were removed in 1953, could learn no new facts or events yet retained his childhood, his vocabulary and his ability to learn motor skills. Declarative memory (events, facts) is encoded through the hippocampus and consolidated into cortex, a process sleep accelerates ([sleep](../01-integrative-principles/sleep-and-circadian-rhythms.md)); non-declarative memory (skills, habits, conditioning) uses striatum, cerebellum and amygdala and survives hippocampal loss; working memory holds a few items for seconds in prefrontal circuits. Alzheimer's disease begins in the medial temporal lobe, so recent memory fails first.

3. **At the cellular level memory is changed synaptic strength.** Long-term potentiation at glutamate synapses ([synaptic transmission](synaptic-transmission-and-neurotransmitters.md)), consolidated by protein synthesis and growth of spines, is the mechanism; its blockade prevents learning in animals. Retrieval re-stores the trace, so memories are rewritten each time they are recalled, which is why they are durable and unreliable.

4. **Language is lateralised and modular: a front region for producing it, a back region for understanding it, joined by a tract.** In ~95% of right-handers and ~70% of left-handers language is in the left hemisphere. Damage to the inferior frontal region (Broca's area) gives effortful, non-fluent speech with comprehension largely intact; damage to the posterior superior temporal region (Wernicke's area) gives fluent speech empty of meaning with poor comprehension; damage to the arcuate fasciculus joining them ruins repetition. The middle cerebral artery supplies all three, so aphasia is a cardinal sign of left-hemisphere stroke and its type localises the lesion.

5. **Attention, planning and the regulation of behaviour depend on the prefrontal and parietal association cortices.** The right parietal lobe directs attention to space; its damage produces neglect of the left half of the world. The prefrontal cortex holds goals, inhibits impulses and sequences actions; its damage, as in Phineas Gage, leaves intelligence and memory intact and personality and judgement changed. Reward is signalled by dopamine neurons of the ventral tegmental area projecting to the nucleus accumbens, which fire to a reward better than predicted: the learning signal that drugs of abuse hijack.

6. **Consciousness has two components, and they are lost separately.** *Arousal* is produced by the ascending reticular activating system of the upper brainstem and thalamus; a lesion there, or diffuse metabolic depression, produces coma with an intact cortex. *Awareness* requires widespread thalamocortical integration and can be lost with arousal preserved, as in the vegetative state, where sleep–wake cycles continue without evidence of experience. The locked-in patient has both but cannot move; the Glasgow Coma Scale grades eye, verbal and motor responses. General anaesthetics abolish awareness largely by disrupting long-range cortical integration.

7. **The electroencephalogram reads the cortex's collective rhythm, and the rhythm reports the state.** Synchronised thalamocortical oscillations appear as alpha (8–13 Hz) in relaxed waking, delta in deep sleep and coma, and generalised spike-and-wave in seizures. Rhythm is a property of circuits, not cells, which is why it indexes integration.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cerebral autoregulation | Constant cerebral blood flow across mean arterial pressures of ~60–150 mmHg |
| Declarative vs non-declarative memory | Facts and events (hippocampus-dependent) vs skills, habits and conditioning |
| Consolidation | Transfer of a memory from hippocampal dependence to distributed cortical storage |
| Broca's / Wernicke's aphasia | Non-fluent, comprehension spared / fluent, comprehension lost |
| Neglect | Failure to attend to one side of space, typically after right parietal damage |
| Arousal vs awareness | Wakefulness (brainstem) vs content of experience (thalamocortical) |
| Reticular activating system | Brainstem–thalamic network whose activity is required for wakefulness |
| Glasgow Coma Scale | Eye, verbal and motor scoring of responsiveness, 3–15 |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Memories are stored like files and replayed."** They are reconstructed from distributed synaptic changes each time, and altered in the process.
- **"A confused elderly patient has a psychiatric problem."** Acute confusion is most often a failing supply or a toxin acting on a brain with little reserve; look for infection, hypoxia, drugs and metabolic derangement first.
