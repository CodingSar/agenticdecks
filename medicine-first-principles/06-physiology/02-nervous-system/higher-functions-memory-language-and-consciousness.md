## Essence

The cerebral cortex, some 16 billion neurons in a sheet a few millimetres thick, adds to the reflexes and maps below it the capacities that define a person: storing experience, manipulating symbols, directing attention, and being awake and aware. These are not vague properties of the whole brain; each depends on identifiable structures whose loss produces a specific deficit, which is how the functions were discovered. Memory needs the medial temporal lobe to be formed but not to be kept; language lives mostly in the left hemisphere in two cooperating regions; consciousness has two separable components, arousal from the brainstem and content from the thalamocortical system. All of it runs on a tissue that is 2% of body mass, uses 20% of resting oxygen and glucose, stores neither, and loses function within ten seconds of losing its blood supply.

## Core tenets

1. **The brain is the organ with the least tolerance of interrupted supply.** Cerebral blood flow is ~50 mL per 100 g per minute (~750 mL/min, ~15% of cardiac output), autoregulated between mean pressures of roughly 60 and 150 mmHg and driven strongly by arterial carbon dioxide (hyperventilation constricts, hypercapnia dilates). Neurons burn glucose almost exclusively and keep no reserve; consciousness is lost ~10 s after circulatory arrest and irreversible injury begins within minutes. Hypoglycaemia, hypoxia and hypotension therefore present first as confusion, which is a sign of a failing supply, not a psychiatric symptom.

2. **Memory is several systems, and the medial temporal lobe is needed to form declarative memories but not to keep them.** Patient H.M., whose hippocampi were removed in 1953, could learn no new facts or events yet retained his childhood, his vocabulary and his ability to learn motor skills. Hence: declarative memory (episodic events, semantic facts) is encoded through the hippocampus and gradually consolidated into cortex, a process sleep accelerates ([sleep](../01-integrative-principles/sleep-and-circadian-rhythms.md)); non-declarative memory (skills, habits, conditioning, priming) uses striatum, cerebellum and amygdala and survives hippocampal loss; working memory holds a few items for seconds in prefrontal circuits. Alzheimer's disease begins in the medial temporal lobe, so recent memory fails first while distant memory and skills persist.

3. **At the cellular level memory is changed synaptic strength.** Long-term potentiation at glutamate synapses ([synaptic transmission](synaptic-transmission-and-neurotransmitters.md)), consolidated by new protein synthesis and structural growth of spines, is the mechanism in the hippocampus; its blockade prevents learning in animals. Retrieval reactivates and re-stores the trace, so memories are rewritten each time they are recalled, which is why they are both durable and unreliable.

4. **Language is lateralised and modular: a front region for producing it, a back region for understanding it, joined by a tract.** In ~95% of right-handers and ~70% of left-handers language is in the left hemisphere. Damage to the inferior frontal region (Broca's area) gives effortful, non-fluent, grammatically stripped speech with comprehension largely intact; damage to the posterior superior temporal region (Wernicke's area) gives fluent, well-articulated speech empty of meaning, with poor comprehension; damage to the arcuate fasciculus joining them spares both but ruins repetition. Because the middle cerebral artery supplies all three, aphasia is a cardinal sign of left-hemisphere stroke, and its type localises the lesion within it.

5. **Attention, planning and the regulation of behaviour depend on the prefrontal and parietal association cortices.** The right parietal lobe directs attention to space; its damage produces neglect of the left half of the world (the patient shaves one side of the face and denies the paralysed arm is theirs). The prefrontal cortex holds goals, inhibits impulses and sequences actions; its damage, as in Phineas Gage, leaves intelligence and memory intact and personality, judgement and social behaviour changed. Reward is signalled by dopamine neurons of the ventral tegmental area projecting to the nucleus accumbens; they fire to a reward better than predicted, which is the learning signal that drugs of abuse hijack.

6. **Consciousness has two components, and they are lost separately.** *Arousal* (wakefulness) is produced by the ascending reticular activating system of the upper brainstem and thalamus; a lesion there, or diffuse metabolic depression, produces coma even with an intact cortex. *Awareness* (content) requires widespread thalamocortical integration; it can be lost with arousal preserved, as in the vegetative state after cortical injury, where sleep–wake cycles continue without evidence of experience. The locked-in patient has both but cannot move; the Glasgow Coma Scale grades eye, verbal and motor responses to estimate both. General anaesthetics abolish awareness largely by disrupting the long-range cortical integration, not by silencing the cortex.

7. **The electroencephalogram reads the cortex's collective rhythm, and the rhythm reports the state.** Synchronised thalamocortical oscillations appear as alpha (8–13 Hz) in relaxed waking, slow delta in deep sleep and coma, and generalised spike-and-wave in seizures; a flat trace with absent brainstem reflexes defines death by neurological criteria in many jurisdictions. Rhythm is a property of circuits, not cells, which is why it indexes integration.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cerebral autoregulation | Constant cerebral blood flow across mean arterial pressures of ~60–150 mmHg |
| Declarative vs non-declarative memory | Facts and events (hippocampus-dependent) vs skills, habits and conditioning |
| Consolidation | Transfer of a memory from hippocampal dependence to distributed cortical storage |
| Working memory | Seconds-long active maintenance of a few items in prefrontal circuits |
| Lateralisation | Specialisation of one hemisphere, as for language on the left |
| Broca's / Wernicke's aphasia | Non-fluent, comprehension spared / fluent, comprehension lost |
| Neglect | Failure to attend to one side of space, typically after right parietal damage |
| Arousal vs awareness | Wakefulness (brainstem) vs content of experience (thalamocortical) |
| Reticular activating system | Brainstem–thalamic network whose activity is required for wakefulness |
| Glasgow Coma Scale | Eye, verbal and motor scoring of responsiveness, 3–15 |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Memories are stored like files and replayed."** They are reconstructed from distributed synaptic changes each time, and altered in the process.
- **"A confused elderly patient has a psychiatric problem."** Acute confusion is most often a failing supply or a toxin acting on a brain with little reserve; look for infection, hypoxia, drugs and metabolic derangement first.
- **"Coma means the whole brain has stopped."** A small brainstem lesion abolishes arousal with the cortex intact; a large cortical injury can leave arousal with no awareness.
