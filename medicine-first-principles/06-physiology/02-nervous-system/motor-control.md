## Essence

Movement is produced by one final common path, the alpha motor neuron and the muscle fibres it owns, and everything else in the motor system decides when, how strongly and in what pattern those neurons fire. Three levels do this: the spinal cord holds the reflexes and rhythm generators that handle load and timing locally; the brainstem holds posture and balance; the cortex plans and launches voluntary movement. Two side loops correct the plan: the basal ganglia select and scale actions; the cerebellum compares intended with actual movement and tunes the next. Each level fails in its own way, so weakness, stiffness, slowness, tremor and incoordination each point to a place.

## Core tenets

1. **The motor unit is the quantum of force, and force is graded by recruiting units in a fixed order and firing them faster.** One alpha motor neuron and its fibres (a few in eye muscles, ~2,000 in calf) contract together. Henneman's size principle: small, slow, fatigue-resistant units are recruited first, large fast ones last, so fine control is automatic at low force. Rate coding adds force by summing twitches toward tetanus. Loss of motor neurons (lower motor neuron lesion) gives flaccid weakness, wasting, fasciculation and lost reflexes, because the final path is gone.

2. **The stretch reflex is a servo that holds muscle length against load, and its gain is set from above.** Muscle spindles signal length and its rate of change through Ia afferents, which excite the same muscle's alpha motor neurons monosynaptically and inhibit the antagonist (reciprocal inhibition). Gamma motor neurons keep the spindle taut as the muscle shortens (alpha–gamma coactivation); Golgi tendon organs signal force and inhibit their own muscle. Descending pathways set the loop's gain: remove them and the reflex is exaggerated (spasticity, clonus, the upgoing plantar response of an upper motor neuron lesion), and the weakness is of skilled, fractionated movement.

3. **The cord contains pattern generators, so the brain commands locomotion rather than each step.** Networks of interneurons produce alternating flexor–extensor rhythm without descending input, as a spinal cat on a treadmill shows; load and hip-position feedback adjust timing. Withdrawal from pain with extension of the opposite limb (crossed extensor reflex) is a multi-segment response the cortex uses as a building block.

4. **Descending systems divide by what they control: medial pathways for posture and axial muscles, the lateral corticospinal tract for the hands.** Vestibulospinal and reticulospinal tracts keep the body upright and set background tone, acting bilaterally on trunk and proximal muscles. The corticospinal tract, ~1 million fibres crossing at the pyramidal decussation, is the only pathway with direct connections to finger motor neurons; its loss leaves a stroke patient able to stand but unable to button a shirt. Corticobulbar fibres serve the face, bilaterally for the forehead, which is why a cortical stroke spares forehead wrinkling and a facial nerve palsy does not.

5. **The basal ganglia select actions by disinhibition, and dopamine sets the balance between go and stop.** The striatum's direct pathway inhibits the output nuclei (internal globus pallidus, substantia nigra reticulata), which tonically inhibit the thalamus, so direct-pathway activity *releases* a movement; the indirect pathway, through the external pallidum and subthalamic nucleus, increases output firing and suppresses competing movements. Dopamine from the substantia nigra compacta excites the direct pathway (D1) and inhibits the indirect (D2). Lose dopamine and the brake dominates: slowness, rigidity and rest tremor (parkinsonism). Lose indirect-pathway neurons and movements escape: chorea. Levodopa and dopamine antagonists push the balance each way.

6. **The cerebellum is a feedforward error corrector and learns from mismatch.** It receives a copy of the motor command and the sensory result, compares them, and adjusts the next command; climbing fibres from the inferior olive carry the error signal that drives long-term depression at Purkinje cell synapses. It neither initiates movement nor produces weakness; lesions give ipsilateral ataxia, intention tremor, dysmetria and nystagmus, all failures of timing and scaling. Alcohol intoxication is reversible cerebellar failure.

7. **Voluntary movement is planned before it is executed, and the plan is sensorimotor.** Posterior parietal cortex places body and targets in space, premotor and supplementary motor areas assemble sequences, and the readiness potential precedes movement by several hundred milliseconds. Internal models predict the sensory consequences of a command, which is why you cannot tickle yourself. Rehabilitation after stroke exploits the plasticity of these maps.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Motor unit | One alpha motor neuron and all the fibres it innervates; the final common path |
| Size principle | Recruitment from small, slow units to large, fast ones as force rises |
| Muscle spindle / Golgi tendon organ | Length (and velocity) sensor in parallel with fibres / force sensor in series with tendon |
| Upper vs lower motor neuron signs | Spasticity, hyperreflexia, extensor plantar / flaccidity, wasting, fasciculation, areflexia |
| Central pattern generator | Spinal network producing rhythmic alternating output without descending drive |
| Direct / indirect pathway | Striatal routes that release / suppress movement through the basal ganglia output nuclei |
| Disinhibition | Releasing a tonically inhibited target by inhibiting its inhibitor |
| Ataxia | Incoordination of timing and scaling without weakness; the cerebellar sign |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Spasticity means the muscle is too strong."** It is loss of descending inhibition on a spinal reflex; the muscle is weak and the reflex is loud.
- **"The cerebellum produces movement."** It corrects it. Cerebellar patients are not weak; they are inaccurate.
