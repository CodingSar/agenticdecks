## Essence

An action potential cannot jump between cells, so at the synapse it is converted into a chemical signal: calcium enters the terminal, vesicles release transmitter into a 20 nm cleft, and receptors on the next cell turn the transmitter back into current or into a slower biochemical change. The conversion costs half a millisecond but buys what a wire cannot do: the sign of the signal can be flipped, its gain adjusted, thousands of inputs summed, and the connection strengthened by use, which is the physical basis of learning. The meaning of a transmitter is set by its receptor, not the molecule: acetylcholine contracts skeletal muscle through one receptor and slows the heart through another. Nearly every psychoactive and anaesthetic drug acts at one of the steps described here.

## Core tenets

1. **Release is triggered by calcium and quantised in vesicles.** The arriving spike opens voltage-gated calcium channels; calcium binds synaptotagmin, which drives fusion mediated by soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor (SNARE) proteins of docked vesicles ([membrane trafficking](../../03-cell-biology/01-cell-structure/membrane-trafficking-and-secretion.md)) within ~0.2 ms. Each vesicle releases a fixed packet (a quantum), and the response is an integer number of quanta, as Katz showed at the neuromuscular junction. Release probability rises roughly with the fourth power of calcium entry, so anything that changes presynaptic calcium changes output strongly; botulinum and tetanus toxins cleave SNARE proteins and abolish release.

2. **Receptors come in two kinds, fast and slow, and the slow kind is how the brain sets its own state.** Ionotropic receptors are ligand-gated channels: current flows within a millisecond and stops when the transmitter leaves. Metabotropic receptors are G-protein-coupled ([receptors and ligands](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/04-cell-signalling/receptors-and-ligands.md)); they act through [second messengers](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/04-cell-signalling/second-messengers-and-kinase-cascades.md) over hundreds of milliseconds to minutes, open or close channels indirectly, and change the excitability of the cell rather than firing it. Glutamate and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) do most of the fast work; dopamine, noradrenaline, serotonin, histamine and acetylcholine, released diffusely from small brainstem and basal forebrain nuclei, set arousal, mood, attention and reward.

3. **Excitation and inhibition are decided by which ion the receptor passes, and the cell sums both.** Glutamate receptors pass sodium (and, for the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) type, calcium), depolarising the cell toward threshold (an excitatory postsynaptic potential). GABA-A and glycine receptors pass chloride, holding the membrane near E_Cl (~−70 mV) and shunting excitatory current (an inhibitory postsynaptic potential). Potentials from thousands of synapses sum in space and time at the axon initial segment. Inhibition is an active current, which is why removing it (strychnine at glycine receptors, tetanus toxin on GABA release) causes convulsions and enhancing it (benzodiazepines, barbiturates, propofol, ethanol at GABA-A) causes sedation.

4. **Transmission is ended by removal of the transmitter, and the removal step is the main drug target.** Acetylcholine is hydrolysed in the cleft by acetylcholinesterase (inhibited by neostigmine in myasthenia and by organophosphates in poisoning). Monoamines and GABA are taken back by transporters: the serotonin and noradrenaline transporters are blocked by most antidepressants, the dopamine transporter by cocaine. Glutamate is cleared by astrocytes; when clearance fails in ischaemia it accumulates and kills neurons through calcium overload (excitotoxicity).

5. **The NMDA receptor is a coincidence detector, and the synapse it controls grows stronger with use: that is memory at the cellular level.** The NMDA receptor needs glutamate bound *and* the postsynaptic membrane depolarised to expel the magnesium ion that blocks its pore. Only then does calcium enter, activating kinases that insert more α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptors and enlarge the synapse: long-term potentiation (LTP). Uncorrelated activity does the reverse (long-term depression). Synapses that fire together wire together (Hebb), which is how experience is stored. Ketamine blocks NMDA receptors; anti-NMDA-receptor antibodies cause an encephalitis of psychosis, seizures and amnesia.

6. **The neuromuscular junction is the synapse with a safety margin, and its failure is weakness that worsens with use.** Each motor nerve spike releases far more acetylcholine than needed to fire the fibre through nicotinic receptors on the folded end-plate. Antibodies against the receptor (myasthenia gravis) erode the margin so that repeated firing, which depletes vesicles, fails first. Competitive blockers (rocuronium) and depolarising blockers (suxamethonium) paralyse for surgery by occupying or desensitising the receptor.

7. **Most transmitters are modulated at the terminal, so output depends on history and neighbours.** Presynaptic autoreceptors (α2-adrenergic, dopamine D2, GABA-B) sense the cell's own release and damp it; co-released peptides (substance P, opioids) act at higher firing rates and longer timescales. Opioid receptors on terminals reduce calcium entry and release, which is how morphine silences pain afferents and also breathing.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Quantum | The transmitter content of one vesicle; the unit of release |
| Ionotropic receptor | Ligand-gated ion channel; millisecond signalling |
| Metabotropic receptor | G-protein-coupled receptor acting through second messengers; slow, modulatory |
| EPSP / IPSP | Excitatory / inhibitory postsynaptic potential; summed to decide firing |
| Reuptake transporter | Protein returning transmitter to the terminal or glia; target of antidepressants and stimulants |
| Long-term potentiation | Lasting increase in synaptic strength after correlated activity; NMDA-dependent in hippocampus |
| Autoreceptor | Presynaptic receptor for the cell's own transmitter, giving negative feedback on release |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Each transmitter has one effect."** Acetylcholine excites skeletal muscle (nicotinic) and inhibits the heart (muscarinic M2); the receptor decides.
- **"Low serotonin causes depression."** Antidepressants raise synaptic monoamines within hours but act over weeks, through plasticity; the simple deficit model does not fit the evidence.
