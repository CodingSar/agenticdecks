## Essence

Bone looks inert and is the opposite: the adult skeleton is dissolved and rebuilt at ~10% of its mass a year, so that a bone is wholly replaced in about a decade. The rebuilding is done by coupled teams of cells, osteoclasts that excavate and osteoblasts that refill, directed by osteocytes buried in the matrix that sense strain. This lets bone repair fatigue damage, adapt its shape and mass to the loads it carries, release calcium when the blood needs it and heal fractures without a scar. Joints are where bones meet and must move against each other for a lifetime; articular cartilage, which has no blood supply and almost no capacity to repair, is the component that wears out. The physiology of the skeleton is therefore a story of coupling: resorption to formation, load to mass, and mineral reserve to the demands of the rest of the body.

## Core tenets

1. **Bone is a composite material, and its two components give it two properties.** Type I collagen fibres (about a third of dry weight) give tensile strength and toughness; hydroxyapatite crystals (calcium phosphate, two-thirds) deposited on them give compressive stiffness. Either alone fails: demineralised bone bends like rubber (rickets, osteomalacia), decollagenised bone shatters like chalk (osteogenesis imperfecta). Cortical bone forms the dense shell and takes bending and torsion; trabecular bone's lattice in vertebrae and the ends of long bones distributes load and offers the large surface on which most remodelling occurs, which is why it is lost first in osteoporosis ([mechanics and materials](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/mechanics-and-materials.md)).

2. **Remodelling is a sequence in a basic multicellular unit, and formation is coupled to resorption.** A remodelling cycle begins when osteocytes, sensing microdamage or strain or lack of it, signal through RANK ligand to recruit osteoclast precursors; osteoclasts (multinucleated, of macrophage lineage) seal onto the surface, pump acid and secrete cathepsin K, and dig a pit over ~2–3 weeks. Factors released from the matrix and from osteoclasts then recruit osteoblasts, which lay down new collagen over ~3 months and mineralise it; some osteoblasts become buried as new osteocytes, others become lining cells. Osteoprotegerin, a decoy receptor for RANK ligand made by osteoblasts, brakes the cycle. In the young adult the pit is exactly refilled; from mid-life each cycle refills slightly less, and the sum of millions of small deficits is age-related bone loss.

   ```
   osteocyte strain signal -> RANKL (osteoblast lineage) -> osteoclast recruited
   -> resorption pit (2-3 weeks) -> coupling factors -> osteoblasts fill pit (~3 months) -> mineralise
   brake: osteoprotegerin binds RANKL   |   oestrogen, mechanical load: raise OPG, lower RANKL, keep osteocytes alive
   accelerators: PTH (continuous), cortisol, inflammation (IL-1, TNF), immobility, oestrogen loss
   ```

3. **Mass follows load: bone is a structure that tunes itself to its strains.** Osteocytes in their fluid-filled canaliculi sense strain-driven fluid flow. Strains above a set point suppress sclerostin (an osteocyte protein that inhibits osteoblasts) and promote formation; strains below it increase sclerostin and resorption (Frost's mechanostat). A tennis player's dominant arm has ~10–20% more cortical bone; astronauts and bed-bound patients lose ~1–2% of hip bone per month; a paralysed limb's bones thin for want of muscle pull. The same mechanism makes bone strongest along the lines of habitual stress and redistributes it when a fracture heals in a new alignment (Wolff's law). Antibodies against sclerostin are a direct pharmacological use of this loop.

4. **Hormones set the balance of the cycle, and bone pays for mineral homeostasis.** Parathyroid Hormone (PTH), given continuously, favours resorption to raise plasma calcium; given in brief pulses, it favours formation. Oestrogen restrains osteoclast lifespan and keeps osteocytes alive, so its loss at menopause doubles remodelling rate with a net deficit, the main cause of post-menopausal osteoporosis. Glucocorticoids suppress osteoblasts and kill osteocytes; thyroid hormone excess speeds the cycle; growth hormone and insulin-like growth factor 1 drive growth. Vitamin D supplies the calcium and phosphate that mineralisation requires. The endocrine logic is in [calcium and bone mineral metabolism](../07-endocrine-system/calcium-and-bone-mineral-metabolism.md); here the point is that the skeleton is the body's mineral bank and is drawn down when the plasma needs it ([bone and calcium disorders](../../12-organ-system-specialties/05-endocrinology/bone-and-calcium-disorders.md)).

5. **Bone grows in length at a cartilage plate and in width by surface deposition, and the plate closes under sex steroids.** Long bones lengthen by endochondral ossification: chondrocytes in the epiphyseal growth plate proliferate, enlarge in columns, mineralise their matrix and die, and osteoblasts replace the cartilage scaffold with bone. Growth hormone, thyroid hormone and insulin-like growth factor 1 drive proliferation; oestrogen, in both sexes, exhausts the plate at puberty so growth stops. Fractures through the plate in children can arrest or distort growth. Width increases by periosteal apposition throughout life, which is why older bones are wider with thinner cortices: the same strength from a larger diameter until the cortex thins too far.

6. **Fracture healing is remodelling at scale, with a cartilage intermediate when the bone is not held still.** A fracture bleeds; the haematoma becomes granulation tissue and, if the fragments move, a soft callus of cartilage that is then replaced by woven bone (endochondral repair) over weeks; rigid fixation allows direct cortical remodelling without callus. Woven bone is replaced by lamellar bone along the lines of stress over months to years, restoring the original shape in children almost perfectly. Healing fails (non-union) when blood supply, stability or the host's biology (smoking, diabetes, steroids, infection) is inadequate ([orthopaedics, fractures and joints](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/orthopaedics-fractures-and-joints.md)). Bone is the one tissue that regenerates without a scar, because remodelling is what it does anyway.

7. **Synovial joints achieve near-frictionless motion with a tissue that cannot repair itself.** Articular cartilage is a few millimetres of type II collagen holding aggrecan, a proteoglycan whose charged chains attract water to ~70–80% of the tissue's weight. Under load, water is squeezed out slowly, so the tissue behaves like a stiff fluid cushion and the exuded fluid lubricates the surfaces (friction coefficient ~0.001–0.01, lower than ice on ice). Chondrocytes are sparse, avascular and fed by diffusion from synovial fluid pumped by movement; they cannot replace lost matrix in adults. Cartilage loss is therefore cumulative. Osteoarthritis is the whole joint failing (cartilage thinning, subchondral bone sclerosis, osteophytes, synovial inflammation) under the combined effects of load, injury, age and genes, not simple wear ([osteoarthritis](../../12-organ-system-specialties/09-rheumatology-and-clinical-immunology/osteoarthritis-gout-and-regional-pain.md)).

8. **The synovium is a vascular membrane that feeds the joint and is the target of inflammatory arthritis.** It secretes hyaluronan-rich synovial fluid (an ultrafiltrate of plasma) and clears debris. Because it is well supplied with blood and immune cells and encloses a space in which antigen can persist, it is where autoimmune and crystal inflammation take hold: rheumatoid synovitis grows into pannus that erodes cartilage and bone through the same RANK ligand pathway as remodelling, and urate crystals in gout trigger the inflammasome ([inflammatory arthritis](../../12-organ-system-specialties/09-rheumatology-and-clinical-immunology/inflammatory-arthritis.md)). Joint stability comes from the fit of the bony surfaces, ligaments and capsule, and from muscle; proprioceptors in all of these report position, and their loss after injury predisposes to re-injury.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Hydroxyapatite on type I collagen | The composite that gives bone compressive stiffness and tensile toughness |
| Osteoclast, osteoblast, osteocyte | Resorbing cell (macrophage lineage), forming cell, and matrix-embedded strain sensor and controller |
| Basic multicellular unit | The coupled team that excavates and refills one site over ~4–6 months |
| RANK ligand and osteoprotegerin | Osteoclast-activating signal and its decoy receptor; the balance sets resorption rate |
| Sclerostin | Osteocyte inhibitor of bone formation, suppressed by mechanical loading |
| Mechanostat | Frost's model: strain above or below a set point adds or removes bone |
| Endochondral ossification | Lengthening by replacement of a growth-plate cartilage scaffold with bone |
| Callus | Cartilage-then-woven-bone bridge of a mobile fracture, later remodelled to lamellar bone |
| Aggrecan | Cartilage proteoglycan whose charge binds the water that gives cartilage its load-bearing and lubricating behaviour |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Bone is a fixed mineral scaffold."** It is turned over at ~10% a year, adapts to load within weeks, and is drawn on for calcium daily.
- **"Osteoarthritis is wear and tear from use."** Habitual loading maintains cartilage; osteoarthritis is a biological failure of the whole joint with mechanical, inflammatory and genetic components, and injury and obesity are its main modifiable drivers.
- **"Calcium intake builds strong bones."** Load, oestrogen and the remodelling balance decide bone mass; calcium and vitamin D are necessary substrates, not stimuli, beyond adequacy.
