## Essence

Skeletal muscle is ~40% of body mass and the only tissue that converts chemical energy into movement on command. It does so with a molecular motor, myosin, that pulls on actin filaments in a repeating cycle powered by Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) and switched on by calcium. A nerve impulse releases the calcium; the number of fibres recruited and the rate at which they fire set the force; and the fuel for sustained work must be supplied at a rate that matches demand, which is why muscle contains three energy systems with different capacities and speeds. Every disorder of weakness, fatigue or cramp sits somewhere on the chain from nerve to neuromuscular junction to membrane to calcium to cross-bridge to fuel, and every effect of training is an adaptation of one of those links.

## Core tenets

1. **Force is made by cross-bridges cycling, and the cycle is ATP hydrolysis coupled to a conformational change.** Myosin heads bind ATP, hydrolyse it and cock; they bind actin, release phosphate and swing (the power stroke, ~10 nm, ~a few piconewtons each); they release Adenosine Diphosphate (ADP), and a fresh ATP detaches them. Thick and thin filaments slide past each other while neither shortens (the sliding filament theory of Huxley and Huxley, 1954). Rigor mortis is the cycle stopping with heads attached when ATP runs out after death: ATP is needed to let go, not only to pull.

   ```
   myosin-ADP-Pi (cocked) --binds actin--> power stroke (Pi released) --> myosin-ADP bound (rigor)
          ^                                                                        |
          +---- ATP hydrolysis re-cocks head <--- ATP binds, head detaches <-------+
   Ca2+ on troponin moves tropomyosin so actin sites are exposed; no Ca2+, no binding
   ```

2. **Calcium is the switch, and excitation–contraction coupling delivers it in milliseconds.** At rest, tropomyosin covers the myosin-binding sites on actin. A motor neurone action potential releases acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction, nicotinic receptors depolarise the end-plate, a muscle action potential propagates along the sarcolemma and down the transverse tubules, where voltage sensors mechanically open ryanodine receptors in the sarcoplasmic reticulum; calcium floods the cytosol from ~0.1 to ~10 µmol/L, binds troponin C, and tropomyosin moves. Relaxation is calcium being pumped back by the sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase, which costs ATP too. Each link is a disease site: antibodies against the acetylcholine receptor (myasthenia gravis), toxins blocking release (botulinum) or receptors (curare and the neuromuscular blockers of [anaesthesia](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/general-and-regional-anaesthesia.md)), and ryanodine receptor mutations that release calcium uncontrollably under volatile anaesthetics (malignant hyperthermia) ([neuromuscular disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/demyelinating-and-neuromuscular-disease.md)).

3. **The nervous system grades force by recruitment and rate, not by varying the twitch.** A single action potential gives an all-or-none twitch in every fibre of a motor unit (one motor neurone and the 10 to 2,000 fibres it innervates). Force is increased by recruiting more units, from small slow ones to large fast ones in a fixed order (Henneman's size principle), and by raising firing rate so that twitches fuse into a sustained tetanus with three to five times twitch force. Fine control in the hand uses small units; the gastrocnemius uses huge ones. Loss of motor neurones reduces the number of units, and surviving units enlarge by reinnervating orphaned fibres, which is why strength is preserved until late in motor neurone disease ([motor control](../02-nervous-system/motor-control.md)).

4. **Force depends on length and velocity, which is why posture and joint angle matter.** A sarcomere generates most force at ~2.0–2.2 µm, where filament overlap is optimal, and less when overstretched (too few cross-bridges) or shortened (filaments collide). Force falls steeply as shortening velocity rises, because cross-bridges have less time to attach, and it exceeds isometric force during active lengthening (eccentric contraction), where fewer cross-bridges hold more load and the muscle is most easily damaged. Power (force × velocity) peaks at about a third of maximum velocity, which is the cadence a cyclist settles on. Tendons add elastic storage, and levers at joints convert high force over short distances into speed ([biomechanics](../../05-tissues-and-anatomy/02-body-organization/biomechanics-of-the-musculoskeletal-system.md)).

5. **Three energy systems supply ATP on three timescales.** Muscle holds ATP for only ~2 seconds of maximal work. Phosphocreatine rephosphorylates ADP instantly and lasts ~10 seconds (a sprint). Anaerobic glycolysis of glycogen supplies ATP fast for ~1–2 minutes but accumulates lactate and hydrogen ions. Oxidative phosphorylation of glycogen, glucose and fatty acids is slow to ramp up but limited only by oxygen delivery and substrate, and runs for hours. The crossover from carbohydrate to fat as the main fuel occurs as intensity falls; glycogen depletion after ~90 minutes of hard effort is the marathoner's wall ([bioenergetics](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/02-metabolism/bioenergetics-atp-and-redox-carriers.md)).

6. **Fibre types match motor and metabolism to the job, and training shifts the match.** Type I fibres (slow myosin, many mitochondria and capillaries, myoglobin-red) produce force slowly and fatigue slowly; type IIx fibres (fast myosin, glycolytic, white) produce force fast and fatigue in a minute; type IIa are intermediate. The proportion is largely inherited and partly plastic: endurance training increases mitochondria, capillaries and oxidative enzymes in all fibres (the shift toward type IIa), while resistance training increases fibre cross-sectional area through added myofibrils (hypertrophy; fibre number barely changes in humans). Disuse reverses both within weeks, and a bed-bound patient loses ~1–1.5% of muscle mass a day in the first week ([sports and lifestyle medicine](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/sports-occupational-and-lifestyle-medicine.md)).

7. **Fatigue is mostly a failure of excitation–contraction coupling and energy supply inside the fibre, not of the nerve or of lactate alone.** In intense exercise, accumulating inorganic phosphate reduces calcium release and cross-bridge force, potassium leaks out and depolarises the membrane, and acidosis has modest direct effects; in prolonged exercise, glycogen depletion and central (brain) reduction of drive dominate. Lactate is a fuel transported to heart and liver, not a waste product, and the muscle soreness of the following day is micro-damage and inflammation from eccentric work, not retained lactate. Recovery of phosphocreatine takes minutes, of glycogen a day, of damaged fibres days to weeks.

8. **Muscle is also a metabolic and endocrine organ.** It is the main sink for glucose after a meal (through insulin-stimulated GLUT4), the main store of protein that is broken down in starvation and critical illness to supply gluconeogenesis, the main source of heat in shivering and exercise, and a source of signalling proteins (myokines such as interleukin-6) released with contraction. Loss of muscle with age (sarcopenia) therefore impairs glucose tolerance and resilience to illness as well as mobility, which is why strength is a predictor of survival in older people and why [rehabilitation](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/rehabilitation-and-disability.md) is physiology, not merely encouragement.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sarcomere | The repeating unit between Z-discs; thick (myosin) and thin (actin) filaments whose overlap sets force |
| Cross-bridge cycle | ATP-driven attach–stroke–detach cycle of a myosin head on actin |
| Excitation–contraction coupling | Chain from sarcolemmal action potential through transverse tubule voltage sensors to sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium release |
| Troponin–tropomyosin | The calcium switch that exposes myosin-binding sites on actin |
| Motor unit | One motor neurone and all fibres it innervates; the unit of recruitment |
| Tetanus | Fused twitches at high firing rate, giving sustained maximal force |
| Length–tension and force–velocity relations | Force depends on sarcomere length (overlap) and on shortening speed (attachment time) |
| Phosphocreatine | Immediate ATP buffer lasting ~10 seconds of maximal effort |
| Fibre types I, IIa, IIx | Slow oxidative, fast oxidative-glycolytic, fast glycolytic fibres |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Lactic acid causes fatigue and next-day soreness."** Lactate is cleared within an hour and is used as fuel; fatigue is mostly phosphate, potassium and calcium handling plus fuel depletion, and soreness is eccentric micro-injury.
- **"A muscle contracts harder by making each fibre contract harder."** Each fibre's twitch is all-or-none; force is graded by how many units fire and how fast.
- **"Muscle turns to fat with disuse."** Fibres atrophy and fat may be deposited separately; the two tissues do not interconvert.
