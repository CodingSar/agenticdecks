## Essence

Smooth muscle lines every hollow organ and every blood vessel larger than a capillary, and it does a job skeletal muscle cannot: it holds tension for hours at a tiny energy cost, shortens to a fraction of its length, and answers to nerves, hormones, local metabolites and stretch at once. It uses the same actin–myosin motor as striated muscle but switches it on differently: calcium does not uncover actin, it activates a kinase that phosphorylates myosin, so the cell's force is set by the balance between a kinase and a phosphatase that dozens of signals can tilt. That design makes smooth muscle slow, economical and tunable, and it makes it the final effector of blood pressure, airway calibre, gut transit, bladder emptying and labour, and the target of a large part of the formulary.

## Core tenets

1. **The motor is actin and myosin, but the arrangement trades speed for range.** There are no sarcomeres or striations; thin filaments anchor to dense bodies scattered through the cell and to the membrane, with relatively few thick filaments between them, arranged obliquely. The cell can therefore shorten to a third of its resting length and still generate force, and it works over a wide range of lengths (the bladder and stomach fill without a rise in pressure, then empty). Force per cross-sectional area is similar to skeletal muscle, but the cross-bridge cycle is 10–100 times slower, so maximal shortening velocity is low and contraction takes seconds, not milliseconds ([muscle tissue](../../05-tissues-and-anatomy/01-tissues/muscle-tissue.md)).

2. **Contraction is switched on by phosphorylating myosin, so force is a ratio of kinase to phosphatase activity.** A rise in cytosolic calcium binds calmodulin, which activates myosin light-chain kinase (MLCK); phosphorylation of the regulatory light chain lets myosin bind actin and cycle. Myosin light-chain phosphatase (MLCP) removes the phosphate and relaxes the cell. Signals that raise calcium (depolarisation, inositol trisphosphate from Gq-coupled receptors) contract; signals that inhibit MLCP, chiefly the RhoA–Rho kinase pathway downstream of the same Gq and G12/13 receptors, raise force at any given calcium (calcium sensitisation); signals that raise cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) or cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) relax by lowering calcium, inhibiting MLCK and activating MLCP ([second messengers](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/04-cell-signalling/second-messengers-and-kinase-cascades.md)).

   ```
   depolarisation --> L-type Ca2+ channels --+
   Gq receptor (α1, M3, AT1, ET-A, oxytocin, histamine H1) --> IP3 --> SR Ca2+ release --+--> Ca2+-calmodulin --> MLCK --> myosin-P --> contraction
   Rho kinase -----------------------------------------------------------------> inhibits MLCP (sensitisation)
   β2 (Gs) --> cAMP --> PKA ;  NO --> guanylyl cyclase --> cGMP --> PKG   --> lower Ca2+, activate MLCP --> relaxation
   ```

3. **The latch state lets smooth muscle hold tension almost for free.** Once a cross-bridge has formed, dephosphorylation of the attached myosin does not detach it at once; it leaves a slowly cycling "latch" bridge that maintains force while consuming very little adenosine triphosphate (ATP). A vessel wall or a sphincter can therefore hold tone for hours at roughly one three-hundredth of the energy a skeletal muscle would spend on the same force. This is why the body can afford tonically contracted arterioles and sphincters everywhere, and why smooth muscle has few mitochondria and little need for a fast fuel supply.

4. **Calcium can enter by several routes, so contraction does not require an action potential.** Depolarisation opens voltage-gated L-type calcium channels (electromechanical coupling, blocked by dihydropyridines). Agonists at Gq-coupled receptors release calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum through inositol trisphosphate receptors and open receptor-operated channels with no change in membrane potential (pharmacomechanical coupling), which is how angiotensin II or noradrenaline can constrict a vessel that never fires a spike. Stretch opens mechanosensitive channels, the basis of the myogenic response in which arterioles constrict when transmural pressure rises ([hemodynamics](../03-cardiovascular-system/hemodynamics-and-blood-pressure-regulation.md)). Relaxation depends on pumping calcium back into the reticulum and out of the cell, and on potassium channels (including ATP-sensitive channels opened by metabolic stress) that hyperpolarise the membrane and shut the voltage-gated route.

5. **Tonic and phasic smooth muscles use the same machinery to different ends.** Tonic muscle (arteries and arterioles, airways, sphincters, the bladder neck) maintains a sustained, graded tone with no action potentials, set by the level of agonist and stretch and held cheaply by latch bridges. Phasic muscle (stomach, intestine, ureter, uterus in labour, bladder detrusor when voiding) contracts rhythmically, firing calcium action potentials on the crests of slow waves generated by pacemaker cells and relaxing between them ([motility](../06-gastrointestinal-system/motility-and-its-neural-control.md)). A sphincter and the segment behind it are the two types side by side.

6. **Single-unit muscle is a coupled syncytium; multi-unit muscle is controlled fibre by fibre.** In single-unit (visceral) smooth muscle, gap junctions couple the cells electrically so that a pacemaker or a stretch drives the whole sheet as one, and nerves modulate rather than command: gut, ureter, uterus and most small vessels. In multi-unit muscle each cell is innervated separately through varicosities releasing transmitter along the nerve's length, there is no spontaneous activity, and force is graded by how many fibres are driven: the iris, ciliary muscle, piloerector muscles, vas deferens and the large airways and arteries. The distinction decides whether an organ can be denervated and still work (the transplanted gut can) or not (the pupil cannot).

7. **The receptor and its G-protein, not the transmitter, decide whether a smooth muscle contracts or relaxes.** Noradrenaline contracts vascular muscle through α1 (Gq) and relaxes bronchial and uterine muscle through β2 (Gs). Acetylcholine contracts gut, bladder and bronchial muscle through M3 (Gq) and dilates vessels indirectly by releasing endothelial nitric oxide. Nitric oxide from nerves and endothelium relaxes through cGMP: erection, receptive relaxation of the stomach, the descending inhibition of peristalsis. Angiotensin II, endothelin, vasopressin, oxytocin, histamine, serotonin, prostaglandins and leukotrienes each have their receptors on particular muscles, and local metabolites (adenosine, hydrogen ion, carbon dioxide, potassium) relax arterioles directly. The same transmitter list read against the receptor table of [the autonomic nervous system](../02-nervous-system/the-autonomic-nervous-system.md) predicts most drug effects on hollow organs.

8. **Smooth muscle divides and remodels, so chronic stimulation changes the organ.** Unlike striated muscle it can re-enter the cell cycle. The uterus grows from ~50 g to ~1 kg in pregnancy by hyperplasia and hypertrophy, then acquires gap junctions and oxytocin receptors in the final weeks so that a quiescent sheet becomes a synchronised phasic muscle ([labour and delivery](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/labour-delivery-and-the-puerperium.md)). Arterial muscle thickens under sustained hypertension and hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, raising resistance further; airway muscle increases in asthma and amplifies bronchoconstriction ([asthma and COPD](../../12-organ-system-specialties/02-pulmonology/asthma-and-copd.md)); the bladder wall thickens against an obstructed outlet. Medial smooth muscle cells migrating into the intima are part of atherosclerosis and of restenosis after angioplasty.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Myosin light-chain kinase (MLCK) | Calcium–calmodulin-activated enzyme whose phosphorylation of myosin switches contraction on |
| Myosin light-chain phosphatase (MLCP) | Enzyme that removes the phosphate and relaxes the cell; inhibited by Rho kinase |
| Calcium sensitisation | More force at the same calcium through MLCP inhibition; the Rho kinase pathway |
| Latch state | Slowly cycling dephosphorylated cross-bridges that hold tension at very low ATP cost |
| Electromechanical vs pharmacomechanical coupling | Contraction via depolarisation and voltage-gated calcium entry versus via receptor-triggered calcium release without a potential change |
| Tonic vs phasic | Sustained graded tone (vessels, airways, sphincters) versus rhythmic contractions on slow waves (gut, ureter, uterus) |
| Single-unit vs multi-unit | Gap-junction-coupled syncytium with pacemakers versus individually innervated fibres with no spontaneous activity |
| Myogenic response | Stretch-induced contraction of vascular muscle; the basis of autoregulation |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Smooth muscle is weak because it is slow."** Its force per unit area matches skeletal muscle; it is slow and economical, which is what tonic organs need.
- **"Smooth muscle contracts only when its nerves fire."** Much of it is driven by pacemakers, stretch, hormones and local chemistry; nerves set the gain, and denervated gut or vessel still has tone.
- **"Calcium works the same way in all muscle."** In striated muscle calcium unblocks actin through troponin; in smooth muscle it activates a kinase that phosphorylates myosin, which is why force can be changed without changing calcium.
