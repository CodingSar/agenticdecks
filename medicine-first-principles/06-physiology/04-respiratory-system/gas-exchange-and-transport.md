## Essence

Oxygen moves from air to mitochondrion down a staircase of partial pressures, and carbon dioxide climbs the same staircase in reverse; the lung's job is to keep the top of the staircase high and the carriers in the blood do the rest. Alveolar oxygen is set by how much is breathed in against how much is taken up; diffusion across a membrane less than a micrometre thick brings capillary blood to equilibrium in a quarter of a second; haemoglobin then carries seventy times more oxygen than plasma could dissolve, and its sigmoid binding curve loads fully in the lung yet unloads readily in tissue. Carbon dioxide travels mostly as bicarbonate, made and unmade by carbonic anhydrase in the red cell. Oxygen delivery, cardiac output times arterial content, is the quantity the whole cardiorespiratory system exists to defend.

## Core tenets

1. **Alveolar oxygen is inspired oxygen minus what metabolism takes, and the alveolar gas equation says how much.** P_AO2 = F_iO2 × (P_B − P_H2O) − P_aCO2 / R ≈ 0.21 × (760 − 47) − 40/0.8 ≈ 150 − 50 = 100 mmHg (13.3 kPa) at sea level, where R is the respiratory exchange ratio. Alveolar carbon dioxide is set only by production over alveolar ventilation (P_ACO2 ∝ V̇CO2 / V̇A): halve the ventilation and PCO2 doubles, and alveolar oxygen falls by the same 40 mmHg divided by R. Hypoventilation therefore lowers oxygen and raises carbon dioxide together, and the fall in oxygen is fully explained by the rise in carbon dioxide; the alveolar–arterial oxygen difference (normally 5–15 mmHg, rising with age) stays normal. A widened difference means the lung itself is failing to transfer gas.

2. **Diffusion is fast because the barrier is thin and vast, and carbon dioxide diffuses twenty times faster than oxygen.** Fick: transfer ∝ area × solubility × ΔP / (thickness × √molecular weight). The alveolar–capillary membrane is ~0.3 µm thick over ~70 m², so a red cell, spending ~0.75 s in a pulmonary capillary, reaches alveolar PO2 in ~0.25 s; oxygen transfer is normally limited by how much blood flows past (perfusion-limited), not by the membrane. Carbon monoxide, bound avidly by haemoglobin so that its partial pressure in blood stays near zero, is diffusion-limited, which is why it is used to measure diffusing capacity. Thickened membranes (fibrosis, oedema) show their limitation first in exercise, when transit time shortens, and at altitude, when the gradient is small.

3. **Haemoglobin's cooperative binding gives a sigmoid curve with the properties the body needs.** Each gram binds 1.34 mL of oxygen when saturated; with 150 g/L, arterial blood carries ~200 mL/L bound and only ~3 mL/L dissolved (0.03 mL/L per mmHg). Binding of oxygen to one haem shifts the tetramer from a tense to a relaxed state that binds the rest more readily ([protein allostery](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/01-macromolecules/proteins-structure-and-folding.md)), so saturation is ~97% at PO2 100 mmHg, ~90% at 60, ~75% at 40 (mixed venous) and 50% at ~27 (the P50). The flat top means alveolar PO2 can fall to 60 before saturation drops below 90% (a safety margin and the reason 60 mmHg is the usual threshold for respiratory failure); the steep middle means a small fall in tissue PO2 releases much oxygen.

4. **The curve shifts to unload oxygen where it is needed.** Raised carbon dioxide and hydrogen ion (the Bohr effect), raised temperature and raised 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG, made in red cells and increased in chronic hypoxia and anaemia) all lower affinity and shift the curve right, so working, acidotic, warm tissue extracts more at the same PO2. The lung, cool and alkaline, shifts it back. Fetal haemoglobin binds 2,3-BPG poorly, so its curve lies left (P50 ~19 mmHg) and pulls oxygen from maternal blood across the placenta. Stored blood loses 2,3-BPG and unloads poorly for hours after transfusion.

5. **Oxygen content and delivery, not partial pressure, are what tissues receive.** Content = 1.34 × [Hb] × saturation + 0.003 × PO2 (mL/dL); delivery = cardiac output × content ≈ 5 L/min × 200 mL/L ≈ 1,000 mL/min against a resting consumption of 250 mL/min (extraction 25%, mixed venous saturation ~75%). Halve the haemoglobin and delivery halves with a normal PO2 and a normal pulse oximeter reading; halve the cardiac output and the same follows. Hypoxia is therefore of four kinds, hypoxaemic, anaemic, stagnant (circulatory) and histotoxic (cyanide blocking the mitochondrion), and only the first shows in the arterial PO2.

6. **Carbon monoxide poisoning and pulse oximetry together show why saturation can lie.** Carbon monoxide binds haemoglobin ~200–250 times more tightly than oxygen, occupying sites and shifting the remaining curve left; arterial PO2 is normal and a standard pulse oximeter, which cannot distinguish carboxyhaemoglobin from oxyhaemoglobin, reads normal while content is halved. Methaemoglobin behaves similarly. Cyanosis requires ~50 g/L of deoxyhaemoglobin, so it appears readily in polycythaemia and may never appear in severe anaemia.

7. **Carbon dioxide is carried mostly as bicarbonate, made in the red cell and exchanged for chloride, and deoxygenated haemoglobin carries it better.** Of carbon dioxide entering blood, ~10% stays dissolved, ~20–30% binds amino groups on haemoglobin (carbamino), and ~60–70% is hydrated by carbonic anhydrase to carbonic acid, which dissociates; the bicarbonate leaves the cell through the band 3 exchanger in return for chloride (the chloride shift) while deoxyhaemoglobin buffers the proton. Deoxygenation thus increases the blood's capacity for carbon dioxide (the Haldane effect), and oxygenation in the lung releases it: the Bohr and Haldane effects are the same linkage seen from each gas. The carbon dioxide content curve is nearly linear and steep, so venous blood carries only ~40 mL/L more than arterial for a PCO2 rise of ~6 mmHg (40 to 46), and ventilated regions can readily blow off extra carbon dioxide, which they cannot do for oxygen ([acid–base physiology](../01-integrative-principles/acid-base-physiology.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Alveolar gas equation | P_AO2 = F_iO2(P_B − P_H2O) − P_aCO2 / R; ~100 mmHg at sea level |
| Alveolar–arterial oxygen difference | Normal 5–15 mmHg; widened by diffusion defect, mismatch or shunt, not by hypoventilation |
| Perfusion- vs diffusion-limited | Equilibration complete within capillary transit (oxygen, nitrous oxide) vs incomplete (carbon monoxide) |
| Oxygen capacity | 1.34 mL per gram of haemoglobin; ~200 mL/L of arterial blood |
| P50 | PO2 at 50% saturation, ~27 mmHg in adult blood; the index of affinity |
| Bohr effect | Acid and carbon dioxide lower haemoglobin's oxygen affinity (right shift) |
| 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate | Red cell metabolite that stabilises deoxyhaemoglobin and shifts the curve right |
| Oxygen delivery (DO2) | Cardiac output × arterial oxygen content, ~1,000 mL/min at rest |
| Haldane effect | Deoxygenation increases carbon dioxide carriage |
| Chloride shift | Exchange of bicarbonate out of and chloride into the red cell via band 3 |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Oxygen saturation tells you oxygen delivery."** It tells you the fraction of haemoglobin sites filled; a profoundly anaemic or shocked patient can read 99%.
- **"Carbon dioxide is carried dissolved."** Only ~10% is; most travels as bicarbonate made in the red cell.
- **"Giving oxygen fixes hypoxaemia."** It raises alveolar PO2 in hypoventilation and mismatch, but blood passing a shunt never meets it, and in anaemia or low output the arterial PO2 was not the problem.
