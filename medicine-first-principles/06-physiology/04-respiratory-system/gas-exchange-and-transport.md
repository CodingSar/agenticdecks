## Essence

Oxygen moves from air to mitochondrion down a staircase of partial pressures, and carbon dioxide climbs it in reverse; the lung keeps the top of the staircase high and the carriers in the blood do the rest. Alveolar oxygen is set by how much is breathed in against how much is taken up; diffusion across a membrane less than a micrometre thick brings capillary blood to equilibrium in a quarter of a second; haemoglobin carries seventy times more oxygen than plasma could dissolve, and its sigmoid curve loads fully in the lung yet unloads readily in tissue. Carbon dioxide travels mostly as bicarbonate made by carbonic anhydrase in the red cell. Oxygen delivery, cardiac output times arterial content, is the quantity the whole cardiorespiratory system exists to defend.

## Core tenets

1. **Alveolar oxygen is inspired oxygen minus what metabolism takes, and the alveolar gas equation says how much.** P_AO2 = F_iO2 × (P_B − P_H2O) − P_aCO2 / R ≈ 0.21 × (760 − 47) − 40/0.8 ≈ 100 mmHg (13.3 kPa) at sea level, where R is the respiratory exchange ratio. Alveolar carbon dioxide is set only by production over alveolar ventilation: halve the ventilation and the partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2) doubles, and alveolar oxygen falls by the same 40 mmHg divided by R. Hypoventilation therefore lowers oxygen and raises carbon dioxide together, and the alveolar–arterial oxygen difference (normally 5–15 mmHg, rising with age) stays normal; a widened difference means the lung itself is failing to transfer gas.

2. **Diffusion is fast because the barrier is thin and vast, and carbon dioxide diffuses twenty times faster than oxygen.** Fick: transfer ∝ area × solubility × ΔP / (thickness × √molecular weight). The membrane is ~0.3 µm thick over ~70 m², so a red cell spending ~0.75 s in a pulmonary capillary reaches the alveolar partial pressure of oxygen (PO2) in ~0.25 s; oxygen transfer is normally perfusion-limited, not membrane-limited. Carbon monoxide, bound so avidly that its blood partial pressure stays near zero, is diffusion-limited, which is why it measures diffusing capacity. Thickened membranes (fibrosis, oedema) show their limitation first in exercise, when transit shortens, and at altitude, when the gradient is small.

3. **Haemoglobin's cooperative binding gives a sigmoid curve with the properties the body needs.** Each gram binds 1.34 mL of oxygen when saturated; with 150 g/L, arterial blood carries ~200 mL/L bound and only ~3 mL/L dissolved. Binding to one haem shifts the tetramer to a relaxed state that binds the rest more readily ([protein allostery](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/01-macromolecules/proteins-structure-and-folding.md)), so saturation is ~97% at PO2 100 mmHg, ~90% at 60, ~75% at 40 (mixed venous) and 50% at ~27 (the P50). The flat top means PO2 can fall to 60 before saturation drops below 90% (the usual threshold for respiratory failure); the steep middle means a small fall in tissue PO2 releases much oxygen.

4. **The curve shifts to unload oxygen where it is needed.** Raised carbon dioxide and hydrogen ion (the Bohr effect), temperature and 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG, made in red cells and raised in chronic hypoxia and anaemia) lower affinity and shift the curve right, so working, acidotic, warm tissue extracts more at the same PO2; the lung shifts it back. Fetal haemoglobin binds 2,3-BPG poorly, so its curve lies left (P50 ~19 mmHg) and pulls oxygen across the placenta.

5. **Oxygen content and delivery, not partial pressure, are what tissues receive.** Content = 1.34 × [Hb] × saturation + 0.003 × PO2 (mL/dL); delivery = cardiac output × content ≈ 5 L/min × 200 mL/L ≈ 1,000 mL/min against a resting consumption of 250 mL/min (extraction 25%, mixed venous saturation ~75%). Halve the haemoglobin or the cardiac output and delivery halves with a normal PO2 and a normal pulse oximeter reading. Hypoxia is therefore of four kinds, hypoxaemic, anaemic, stagnant and histotoxic (cyanide blocking the mitochondrion), and only the first shows in the arterial PO2.

6. **Carbon dioxide is carried mostly as bicarbonate, made in the red cell and exchanged for chloride, and deoxygenated haemoglobin carries it better.** Of carbon dioxide entering blood, ~10% stays dissolved, ~20–30% binds haemoglobin as carbamino groups, and ~60–70% is hydrated by carbonic anhydrase to carbonic acid; the bicarbonate leaves the cell through the band 3 exchanger in return for chloride (the chloride shift) while deoxyhaemoglobin buffers the proton. Deoxygenation thus increases the blood's capacity for carbon dioxide (the Haldane effect) and oxygenation in the lung releases it. The carbon dioxide content curve is nearly linear and steep, so venous blood carries only ~40 mL/L more than arterial for a PCO2 rise of ~6 mmHg, and ventilated regions can blow off extra carbon dioxide as they cannot take on extra oxygen ([acid–base physiology](../01-integrative-principles/acid-base-physiology.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Alveolar gas equation | P_AO2 = F_iO2(P_B − P_H2O) − P_aCO2 / R; ~100 mmHg at sea level |
| Alveolar–arterial oxygen difference | Normal 5–15 mmHg; widened by diffusion defect, mismatch or shunt, not by hypoventilation |
| P50 | PO2 at 50% saturation, ~27 mmHg in adult blood; the index of affinity |
| Bohr effect | Acid and carbon dioxide lower haemoglobin's oxygen affinity (right shift) |
| Oxygen delivery (DO2) | Cardiac output × arterial oxygen content, ~1,000 mL/min at rest |
| Haldane effect | Deoxygenation increases carbon dioxide carriage |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Oxygen saturation tells you oxygen delivery."** It tells you the fraction of haemoglobin sites filled; a profoundly anaemic or shocked patient can read 99%.
