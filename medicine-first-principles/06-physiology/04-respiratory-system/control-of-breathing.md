## Essence

Breathing is the output of a feedback loop that holds arterial carbon dioxide within about ±3 mmHg of 40 across a twentyfold range of metabolic demand, and falls back on oxygen as an emergency signal below ~60 mmHg. A rhythm generator in the medulla produces the pattern; central chemoreceptors reading brain pH and peripheral chemoreceptors reading arterial oxygen, carbon dioxide and pH adjust its rate and depth; mechanoreceptors shape each breath; the cortex can override it for speech and breath-holding. The loop has gain and delay, so it can oscillate; it adapts, so chronic hypercapnia can be tolerated; and it is depressed by sleep, opioids and anaesthetics, which is how most deaths from overdose occur.

## Core tenets

1. **The rhythm is generated in the medulla and shaped in the pons; it does not need the cortex or the lungs.** Inspiratory neurons of the pre-Bötzinger complex in the ventrolateral medulla fire in bursts that drive the phrenic (C3–C5) and intercostal motor neurons; neighbouring groups generate active expiration, and pontine nuclei smooth the switch. Opioids act on μ-receptors on these neurons, slowing the rhythm and eventually stopping it, which is why respiratory depression is the lethal effect of overdose and why naloxone reverses it.

2. **Central chemoreceptors read brain pH, which tracks arterial carbon dioxide, and they supply most of the drive.** Chemosensitive neurons near the ventral medullary surface (the retrotrapezoid nucleus among them) respond to hydrogen ion in the surrounding fluid. Carbon dioxide crosses the blood–brain barrier freely and is hydrated to acid there; hydrogen ions and bicarbonate cross poorly, so the arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2) is sensed indirectly within a minute and a metabolic acidosis in blood is sensed weakly at first. Cerebrospinal fluid is poorly buffered, so the pH change per mmHg is large: ventilation rises ~2–3 L/min for each mmHg rise in PCO2, a steep, nearly linear response.

3. **Peripheral chemoreceptors are the only oxygen sensors, and they respond to tension, not content.** Glomus cells of the carotid bodies (glossopharyngeal nerve) and, less, the aortic bodies (vagus) have oxygen-sensitive potassium channels; a falling partial pressure of oxygen (PO2) depolarises them within a second or two. The response is hyperbolic: little until arterial PO2 falls below ~60 mmHg, then steep, and it is potentiated by carbon dioxide and acid, so hypoxia and hypercapnia together drive breathing far more than either alone. Because they sense dissolved oxygen, anaemia and carbon monoxide poisoning do not stimulate them. They also carry ~20–30% of the carbon dioxide response and most of the fast response to metabolic acidosis.

4. **The loop regulates carbon dioxide tightly and oxygen loosely because of the shape of the two responses.** With carbon dioxide the gain is high and linear, so a doubling of production in exercise is matched almost exactly and arterial PCO2 is held near 40. With oxygen the response is flat until 60 mmHg, where haemoglobin saturation begins to fall steeply ([gas exchange](gas-exchange-and-transport.md)), so the body spends no effort defending the plateau and reacts hard once content is threatened. The carbon dioxide response is shifted right and flattened by sleep, opioids, benzodiazepines, anaesthetics and chronic hypercapnia itself; in such a patient hypoxic drive is a larger share of the whole.

5. **The central set point adapts over days, so chronic hypercapnia is tolerated and acute hypercapnia is not.** When arterial PCO2 stays high, cerebrospinal fluid bicarbonate rises, pH around the chemoreceptors returns toward normal and the drive falls back although PCO2 remains raised; the kidney does the same for blood ([acid–base](../01-integrative-principles/acid-base-physiology.md)). The patient with chronic obstructive disease therefore lives at PCO2 60 with a normal pH and little dyspnoea, while an acute rise to 60 in a healthy person causes distress, confusion and coma. Oxygen given to such patients can raise PCO2 further, partly by removing hypoxic drive and more by relieving hypoxic vasoconstriction and through the Haldane effect.

6. **Mechanoreceptors and muscle afferents shape the breath and explain dyspnoea.** Vagal stretch receptors in the airways terminate inspiration when the lung is inflated (the Hering–Breuer reflex, strong in infants). Juxtacapillary (J) receptors in the alveolar wall fire when the interstitium is engorged, producing the rapid shallow breathing of pulmonary oedema and embolism. Afferents from the respiratory muscles report the effort made; dyspnoea arises when the drive sent is mismatched with the ventilation achieved, which is why a paralysed, ventilated patient with a normal PCO2 can feel suffocated and a patient with chronic hypercapnia may not.

7. **The loop has delay and gain, and when either rises it oscillates.** The sensor reads blood that left the lung 10–20 s earlier; in heart failure that time doubles, and the delayed, over-corrected response produces waxing and waning breathing with apnoeic pauses (Cheyne–Stokes respiration). At altitude the hypoxic drive raises gain and periodic breathing appears in sleep. Sleep removes the wakefulness drive, so PCO2 rises 3–5 mmHg, and in rapid eye movement sleep accessory muscles are paralysed, which is when neuromuscular weakness first shows. Hyperventilating before a breath-hold dive removes the carbon dioxide signal without adding oxygen, so the diver loses consciousness before feeling the need to breathe.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Pre-Bötzinger complex | Medullary network generating the inspiratory rhythm |
| Central chemoreceptors | Ventral medullary neurons sensing brain extracellular pH, hence arterial PCO2 |
| Peripheral chemoreceptors | Carotid and aortic bodies; the only oxygen sensors |
| Hypoxic ventilatory response | Hyperbolic rise in ventilation as PaO2 falls below ~60 mmHg; potentiated by hypercapnia |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Low oxygen is the main drive to breathe."** Carbon dioxide is, through brain pH; oxygen matters below ~60 mmHg.
