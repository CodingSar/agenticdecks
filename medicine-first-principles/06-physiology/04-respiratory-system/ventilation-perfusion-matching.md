## Essence

Arterial blood gases are decided not by how much air and blood the lung receives but by how well they are matched, alveolus by alveolus. The whole lung has a ventilation-to-perfusion ratio (V̇/Q̇) of ~0.8, but gravity, disease and posture give individual units ratios from zero (perfused, not ventilated: shunt) to infinity (ventilated, not perfused: dead space), and each unit's gas tensions follow its own ratio. Because the oxygen–haemoglobin curve is flat at the top, well-ventilated units cannot make up for poorly ventilated ones, so mismatch always lowers arterial oxygen; because the carbon dioxide curve is linear, they can compensate for carbon dioxide, so mismatch raises it only when ventilation cannot increase. The lung's one defence is hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, which steers blood away from alveoli that have no air, and it is both the lung's best adaptation and, when the whole lung is hypoxic, the cause of right heart failure.

## Core tenets

1. **Each lung unit's gas tensions are set by its own ventilation–perfusion ratio.** A unit with V̇/Q̇ near 1 reaches PO2 ~100 and PCO2 ~40 mmHg. As its ratio falls, its alveolar gas approaches mixed venous blood (PO2 40, PCO2 46); at zero it *is* venous blood (shunt). As the ratio rises, its gas approaches inspired air (PO2 150, PCO2 0); at infinity it is dead space. The whole lung behaves as the blood-flow-weighted sum of its units, so what matters is the *distribution* of ratios, not the totals.

2. **Gravity makes both ventilation and perfusion greatest at the base, but perfusion more so, so the ratio rises toward the apex.** The pulmonary circulation runs at ~25/10 mmHg (mean ~15), and a 30 cm upright lung spans ~23 mmHg of hydrostatic pressure, so apical vessels are barely perfused and basal ones distended. West's zones: at the apex alveolar pressure can exceed arterial (zone 1, no flow, not present in health but produced by haemorrhage or positive-pressure ventilation); in the middle arterial exceeds alveolar which exceeds venous, so flow depends on the arterial–alveolar difference (zone 2); at the base both vascular pressures exceed alveolar (zone 3). Ventilation also falls from base to apex, but less steeply, so V̇/Q̇ is ~0.6 at the base and ~3 at the apex, with apical PO2 ~130 and basal ~90 mmHg. Tuberculosis favours the oxygen-rich apex; infarcts and oedema favour the base.

3. **Mismatch lowers arterial oxygen more than it raises carbon dioxide, and the shape of the two carriage curves explains why.** Blood from a low-ratio unit leaves undersaturated; blood from a high-ratio unit cannot carry much extra oxygen because its haemoglobin is already ~97% saturated and dissolved oxygen is small. Mixing them gives a content, and so an arterial PO2, lower than normal. Carbon dioxide content is linear in PCO2, so the high-ratio unit's low carbon dioxide offsets the low-ratio unit's excess; and any rise in arterial PCO2 is sensed by the chemoreceptors, which raise total ventilation until it is normal again, at the cost of an even lower PO2 in the low-ratio units' neighbours. Hence mismatch presents as hypoxaemia with a normal or low PCO2, and hypercapnia appears only when ventilation can no longer rise (exhaustion, severe obstruction, depressed drive).

4. **Shunt is the one cause of hypoxaemia that oxygen does not correct.** Blood that passes no ventilated alveolus (collapsed or fluid-filled lung in pneumonia, atelectasis and acute respiratory distress syndrome; intracardiac right-to-left defects; the ~2–5% normal shunt of bronchial and coronary venous blood) never meets inspired gas, however rich. Breathing 100% oxygen raises the PO2 of ventilated units' blood but adds little content, since they were nearly saturated, and the shunted fraction still mixes in. A low-ratio unit, by contrast, is corrected by oxygen because its alveoli do receive some gas. The response to oxygen therefore separates the two, and the shunt fraction can be calculated from arterial, mixed venous and end-capillary contents.

5. **Dead space wastes ventilation, and physiological dead space rises in disease.** Anatomical dead space (~150 mL, the conducting airways) is fixed; alveolar dead space is ventilated units without flow: pulmonary embolism, low cardiac output, overdistension by a ventilator. Physiological dead space is their sum, measured by the Bohr equation V_D/V_T = (P_aCO2 − P_ECO2)/P_aCO2 from arterial and mixed expired carbon dioxide (~0.3 in health). Each breath that goes to dead space moves no gas, so a patient with a large embolus must increase total ventilation to keep PCO2 normal and becomes hypocapnic from the effort, until the effort fails. Hypoxaemia in embolism comes mostly from redistribution of flow to the remaining units, which then have low ratios.

6. **Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction steers blood toward air, uniquely in this circulation.** Systemic arterioles dilate to hypoxia; pulmonary arterioles constrict when alveolar PO2 falls below ~70 mmHg, through inhibition of oxygen-sensitive potassium channels in their smooth muscle. A collapsed or consolidated region thus loses its blood flow to better-ventilated lung, which limits the shunt and is why a pneumonia produces less hypoxaemia than its size would predict. The same reflex closes the fetal pulmonary circulation and opens it at the first breath. When the whole lung is hypoxic (altitude, chronic lung disease, obesity hypoventilation) every vessel constricts, pulmonary pressure rises and the right ventricle, built for a low-pressure circuit, hypertrophies and fails (cor pulmonale). General anaesthetics, vasodilators and sepsis blunt the reflex and worsen oxygenation.

7. **The pulmonary circulation is low-pressure, high-compliance and recruitable, so its resistance falls as flow rises.** Pulmonary vascular resistance is about a tenth of the systemic; as cardiac output triples in exercise, previously closed capillaries open and open ones distend, so pressure rises little. Resistance is lowest at functional residual capacity and rises at both high volumes (alveolar vessels compressed) and low volumes (extra-alveolar vessels narrowed), which is one reason mechanical ventilation at high pressures loads the right heart. Pulmonary capillary pressure is ~7–10 mmHg against a plasma oncotic pressure of ~25, so the Starling balance strongly favours a dry alveolus ([capillary exchange](../03-cardiovascular-system/microcirculation-and-capillary-exchange.md)); oedema forms when left atrial pressure exceeds ~20–25 mmHg or when the barrier leaks.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ventilation–perfusion ratio | Alveolar ventilation / blood flow for a unit; ~0.8 for the whole lung |
| Shunt | Perfused units with no ventilation (ratio 0); hypoxaemia not corrected by oxygen |
| Dead space | Ventilated units with no perfusion (ratio ∞); anatomical plus alveolar = physiological |
| West's zones | Apex-to-base pattern of flow set by alveolar, arterial and venous pressures |
| Bohr equation | V_D/V_T = (P_aCO2 − P_ECO2)/P_aCO2; measures physiological dead space |
| Shunt fraction | Q_s/Q_t = (C_cO2 − C_aO2)/(C_cO2 − C_vO2) from end-capillary, arterial and venous oxygen content |
| Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction | Constriction of pulmonary arterioles to low alveolar PO2, diverting flow to ventilated lung |
| Cor pulmonale | Right ventricular hypertrophy and failure from chronic pulmonary hypertension |
| Recruitment and distension | Opening and widening of pulmonary vessels as flow rises, keeping pressure low |
| Alveolar–arterial difference | Index of mismatch, shunt and diffusion limitation; normal 5–15 mmHg |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Hypoxaemia with a normal PCO2 means the lung is fine and the patient is just hypoventilating."** Hypoventilation raises PCO2; a normal or low PCO2 with hypoxaemia means mismatch or shunt.
- **"Oxygen corrects all hypoxaemia."** It corrects low ratios and hypoventilation; it does nothing for blood that never meets an alveolus.
- **"Pulmonary vessels behave like systemic ones."** They constrict to hypoxia where systemic vessels dilate; this is why altitude causes pulmonary, not systemic, hypertension.
