## Essence

Air moves into the lung because the respiratory muscles enlarge the thorax, the pleural pressure falls, the alveoli expand and their pressure drops a centimetre of water below atmospheric. Expiration at rest is passive: the stretched lung recoils. The lung's elastic recoil comes from its tissue and, more, from the surface tension of the fluid lining 300 million alveoli, which surfactant reduces and stabilises. The work of each breath is spent against that recoil and against the resistance of the airways, and the pattern of breathing, the lung volumes and the flow a person can generate all follow from the balance of compliance and resistance. Lung disease is read in those terms: a stiff lung (restriction) or a narrowed airway (obstruction), each with its own signature on the spirometer.

## Core tenets

1. **Breathing is a pressure gradient of a few cmH2O produced by changing the volume of a sealed box.** The diaphragm (phrenic nerve, C3–C5) does ~70% of resting inspiration, descending ~1–2 cm; external intercostals lift the ribs. Pleural pressure falls from ~−5 to ~−8 cmH2O, alveolar pressure falls ~1 cmH2O below atmospheric, and ~500 mL flows in. Of each 500 mL breath ~150 mL fills conducting airways (anatomical dead space) and does not reach alveoli, so at 12 breaths/min alveolar ventilation is ~4.2 L/min, not 6. Rapid shallow breathing wastes ventilation on dead space; the same minute volume in slow deep breaths delivers more fresh gas.

2. **Lung and chest wall pull in opposite directions, and functional residual capacity is their truce.** The lung recoils inward, the relaxed chest wall springs outward; the volume at which the forces balance (~2.5–3 L, the functional residual capacity) is where quiet breathing starts, and the negative pleural pressure is the tension between them. Open the pleura and both go their own way: the lung collapses and the chest wall expands (pneumothorax). Other volumes: tidal ~0.5 L, residual ~1.2 L (air that cannot be expelled), vital capacity ~4.8 L, total lung capacity ~6 L in a young man, all scaling with height, sex and age.

3. **Compliance is volume change per unit pressure, and most of the lung's stiffness is surface tension.** Lung compliance is ~200 mL/cmH2O; lung plus chest wall ~100. Saline-filled lungs are far more compliant than air-filled ones, showing that the air–liquid interface, not the tissue, provides most of the recoil. By Laplace (P = 2T/r), surface tension T would make a small alveolus need higher pressure than a large one, so small alveoli would empty into large. Surfactant (dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine from type II cells) lowers T, and lowers it more as the alveolus shrinks and the film is compressed, so pressure is equalised across sizes, compliance rises, work falls and fluid is not sucked into the alveolus. It appears late in gestation; without it the premature lung collapses with every breath (respiratory distress syndrome).

4. **Airway resistance lives in the medium bronchi, so the small airways are a silent zone.** Resistance ∝ 1/r⁴ for one tube, but the small airways number tens of thousands in parallel, so their combined resistance is under 20% of the total; most resistance is in the trachea and the first six or seven generations. Hence disease can destroy much of the small-airway tree before spirometry changes. Bronchial smooth muscle contracts to vagal acetylcholine (M3), histamine and leukotrienes, and relaxes to β2-adrenergic stimulation. Lung volume also matters: airways are tethered open by the surrounding parenchyma, so resistance falls at high volume and rises at low volume, which is why obstructed patients breathe at high volumes.

5. **Expiratory flow is limited by dynamic compression, which makes forced expiration a reproducible test.** During a forced expiration pleural pressure becomes positive and compresses the airways downstream of the point where intraluminal pressure has fallen to pleural pressure (the equal pressure point); harder effort compresses them more, so flow at a given lung volume cannot be increased by trying harder. The forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) over the forced vital capacity (FVC) is ~0.75–0.80 in health. Obstruction lowers FEV1 more than FVC (ratio falls) and the flow–volume loop scoops; restriction lowers both with a normal or high ratio. Emphysema loses the tethering and the elastic recoil, so airways collapse at higher volumes and gas is trapped.

6. **The work of breathing is elastic plus resistive, and the body chooses the frequency that minimises it.** Elastic work rises with depth, resistive work with flow (frequency). Healthy adults settle at ~12–15 breaths/min; a stiff lung favours rapid shallow breaths, a narrowed airway slow deep ones. Breathing costs ~3–5% of oxygen consumption at rest but can reach 30% or more in severe disease; when the respiratory muscles' demand outruns their supply they fatigue, ventilation falls and carbon dioxide rises. This is the common final path of obstructive, restrictive and neuromuscular failure, and it is what a ventilator takes over.

7. **Regional ventilation is uneven because the lung hangs in a gravitational field.** Pleural pressure is less negative at the base than the apex (by ~7 cmH2O upright), so basal alveoli are smaller, sit on the steeper part of the compliance curve and receive more of each breath. Units with high resistance or compliance have long time constants (R × C) and fill late, so uneven disease makes ventilation frequency-dependent. At low lung volumes the dependent airways close first (closing volume), trapping gas; this volume rises with age and smoking and in the supine position, which is one reason the elderly and the anaesthetised desaturate.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Tidal volume / dead space | ~500 mL per breath / ~150 mL of it in conducting airways |
| Alveolar ventilation | (Tidal volume − dead space) × frequency, ~4.2 L/min |
| Functional residual capacity | Volume at the end of quiet expiration (~2.5–3 L), where lung and chest wall recoil balance |
| Compliance | ΔV / ΔP; ~200 mL/cmH2O for the lung alone |
| Surfactant | Phospholipid film lowering alveolar surface tension more as alveoli shrink |
| Laplace's law (alveolus) | Pressure = 2 × tension / radius |
| Equal pressure point | Airway site where intraluminal pressure equals pleural pressure; downstream airways compress in forced expiration |
| FEV1 / FVC | Forced expiratory volume in 1 s over forced vital capacity; ~0.75–0.80, lower in obstruction |
| Time constant | Resistance × compliance of a lung unit; sets how fast it fills |
| Closing volume | Volume at which dependent small airways begin to close |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Expiration is active."** At rest it is elastic recoil; expiratory muscles are recruited only in exercise, coughing and obstruction.
- **"Small airways cause most airway resistance."** Their huge parallel number makes their total resistance small; this is why early small-airway disease is silent.
- **"Surfactant makes the lung slippery."** It lowers surface tension at the air–liquid interface, which stabilises alveoli of different sizes and halves the work of breathing.
