## Essence

Air moves into the lung because the respiratory muscles enlarge the thorax, pleural pressure falls, the alveoli expand and their pressure drops a centimetre of water below atmospheric; expiration at rest is the stretched lung recoiling. The recoil comes from tissue and, more, from the surface tension of the fluid lining 300 million alveoli, which surfactant reduces and stabilises. The work of each breath is spent against that recoil and against airway resistance, and lung volumes, breathing pattern and achievable flow all follow from the balance of compliance and resistance. Lung disease is read in those terms: a stiff lung (restriction) or a narrowed airway (obstruction), each with its signature on the spirometer.

## Core tenets

1. **Breathing is a pressure gradient of a few cmH2O produced by changing the volume of a sealed box.** The diaphragm (phrenic nerve, C3–C5) does ~70% of resting inspiration; external intercostals lift the ribs. Pleural pressure falls from ~−5 to ~−8 cmH2O, alveolar pressure falls ~1 cmH2O below atmospheric, and ~500 mL flows in. Of each breath ~150 mL fills conducting airways (anatomical dead space), so at 12 breaths/min alveolar ventilation is ~4.2 L/min, not 6; rapid shallow breathing wastes ventilation on dead space.

2. **Lung and chest wall pull in opposite directions, and functional residual capacity is their truce.** The lung recoils inward, the relaxed chest wall springs outward; the volume at which they balance (~2.5–3 L, the functional residual capacity) is where quiet breathing starts, and the negative pleural pressure is the tension between them. Open the pleura and the lung collapses while the chest wall expands (pneumothorax). Other volumes: tidal ~0.5 L, residual ~1.2 L, vital capacity ~4.8 L, total lung capacity ~6 L in a young man, scaling with height, sex and age.

3. **Compliance is volume change per unit pressure, and most of the lung's stiffness is surface tension.** Lung compliance is ~200 mL/cmH2O; lung plus chest wall ~100. Saline-filled lungs are far more compliant than air-filled ones, so the air–liquid interface, not the tissue, provides most of the recoil. By Laplace (P = 2T/r) a small alveolus would need higher pressure than a large one and empty into it. Surfactant (dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine from type II cells) lowers T, and lowers it more as the alveolus shrinks, so pressure is equalised across sizes, compliance rises and fluid is not sucked into the alveolus. It appears late in gestation; without it the premature lung collapses with every breath (respiratory distress syndrome).

4. **Airway resistance lives in the medium bronchi, so the small airways are a silent zone.** Resistance ∝ 1/r⁴ for one tube, but the small airways number tens of thousands in parallel, so their combined resistance is under 20% of the total; most is in the first six or seven generations. Hence disease can destroy much of the small-airway tree before spirometry changes. Bronchial smooth muscle contracts to vagal acetylcholine (M3), histamine and leukotrienes and relaxes to β2 stimulation. Airways are tethered open by the parenchyma, so resistance falls at high lung volume, which is why obstructed patients breathe at high volumes.

5. **Expiratory flow is limited by dynamic compression, which makes forced expiration a reproducible test.** In forced expiration pleural pressure becomes positive and compresses the airways downstream of the point where intraluminal pressure has fallen to pleural pressure (the equal pressure point); harder effort compresses them more, so flow at a given volume cannot be increased by trying harder. The forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) over the forced vital capacity (FVC) is ~0.75–0.80 in health; obstruction lowers the ratio, restriction lowers both volumes with a normal or high ratio. Emphysema loses tethering and recoil, so airways collapse early and gas is trapped.

6. **The work of breathing is elastic plus resistive, and the body chooses the frequency that minimises it.** Elastic work rises with depth, resistive work with frequency. Healthy adults settle at ~12–15 breaths/min; a stiff lung favours rapid shallow breaths, a narrowed airway slow deep ones. Breathing costs ~3–5% of oxygen consumption at rest but can reach 30% in severe disease; when the respiratory muscles' demand outruns their supply they fatigue, ventilation falls and carbon dioxide rises, the common final path of obstructive, restrictive and neuromuscular failure, and what a ventilator takes over.

7. **Regional ventilation is uneven because the lung hangs in a gravitational field.** Pleural pressure is less negative at the base than the apex (by ~7 cmH2O upright), so basal alveoli are smaller, sit on the steeper part of the compliance curve and receive more of each breath. At low lung volumes the dependent airways close first (closing volume), trapping gas; this volume rises with age, smoking and lying supine, one reason the elderly and the anaesthetised desaturate.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Tidal volume / dead space | ~500 mL per breath / ~150 mL of it in conducting airways |
| Functional residual capacity | Volume at the end of quiet expiration (~2.5–3 L), where lung and chest wall recoil balance |
| Compliance | ΔV / ΔP; ~200 mL/cmH2O for the lung alone |
| FEV1 / FVC | Forced expiratory volume in 1 s over forced vital capacity; ~0.75–0.80, lower in obstruction |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Expiration is active."** At rest it is elastic recoil; expiratory muscles are recruited only in exercise, coughing and obstruction.
