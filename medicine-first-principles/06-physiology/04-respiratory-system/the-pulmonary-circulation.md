## Essence

The lung is the only organ that receives the whole cardiac output, and it does so at a sixth of the systemic pressure. The pulmonary circulation is a short, wide, thin-walled, highly compliant circuit built to spread ~5 L/min over ~70 m² of capillary wall while keeping the alveoli dry; its resistance is a tenth of the systemic circulation's and falls further as flow rises. Two features make it unlike every other vascular bed. Its arterioles constrict to hypoxia rather than dilating, which steers blood away from alveoli without air. And its capillaries sit a fraction of a micrometre from an air space, so a rise in their pressure of a few mmHg, from a failing left heart, floods the lung with fluid. The right ventricle that drives the circuit is thin and tolerant of volume but not of pressure, so any process that raises pulmonary resistance ends by loading, and then failing, the right heart.

## Core tenets

1. **It is a low-pressure, low-resistance, high-compliance circuit.** Pulmonary artery pressure is ~25/10 mmHg (mean ~15) against a left atrial pressure of ~5–8, so the driving pressure for the whole output is ~10 mmHg, compared with ~90 across the systemic circulation; pulmonary vascular resistance is therefore about a tenth of systemic. The arteries have thin walls and little smooth muscle, the capillaries form a near-continuous sheet of blood around each alveolus, and the circuit holds ~500 mL, about a tenth of the blood volume, which the lungs can give up or take in as a reservoir. The right ventricle generates this pressure with a wall a third as thick as the left's ([cardiac cycle](../03-cardiovascular-system/the-cardiac-cycle-and-pump-function.md)).

2. **Resistance falls as flow rises, by recruitment and distension, so exercise barely raises pulmonary pressure.** At rest many apical capillaries carry no flow. As cardiac output triples, closed capillaries open (recruitment) and open ones widen (distension), so resistance falls and mean pressure rises only a few mmHg. The same mechanism lets a lung lose half its vessels (pneumonectomy, or an embolus blocking a main branch) with little rise in pressure at rest, which is why pulmonary hypertension appears late and first in exercise, and why a normal resting pulmonary pressure does not exclude vascular loss.

3. **Lung volume changes resistance, in opposite directions for two kinds of vessel.** Alveolar capillaries are compressed as alveoli expand, so their resistance rises at high lung volume; extra-alveolar vessels are pulled open by the surrounding parenchyma, so their resistance falls as the lung inflates and rises as it collapses. Total resistance is lowest near functional residual capacity and rises at both extremes. Positive-pressure ventilation at high volumes therefore loads the right ventricle, and gross over-inflation can produce zone 1 conditions in which alveolar pressure exceeds arterial and flow stops ([lung mechanics](ventilation-and-lung-mechanics.md)).

4. **Gravity distributes flow, and the zones of West describe it.** A 30 cm upright lung spans ~23 mmHg of hydrostatic pressure against a mean arterial pressure of ~15, so the apex is barely perfused and the base is distended. Where alveolar pressure exceeds arterial there is no flow (zone 1, absent in health, produced by haemorrhage or high airway pressure); where arterial exceeds alveolar but alveolar exceeds venous, flow depends on the arterial–alveolar difference (zone 2, the upper lung); where both vascular pressures exceed alveolar, flow depends on the arterial–venous difference as in any other organ (zone 3, the base). Because ventilation also falls toward the apex but less steeply, the ratio of ventilation to perfusion rises from base to apex ([ventilation–perfusion matching](ventilation-perfusion-matching.md)).

5. **Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction steers blood toward air, and is the defining reflex of this circulation.** Systemic arterioles dilate when oxygen falls; pulmonary arterioles constrict when alveolar oxygen tension falls below ~70 mmHg, because hypoxia inhibits oxygen-sensitive potassium channels in their smooth muscle, depolarising the cell and admitting calcium; acidosis potentiates the response. A consolidated or collapsed region loses its blood flow to ventilated lung, limiting the shunt. The same reflex closes the fetal pulmonary circulation and opens it at the first breath ([fetal and neonatal physiology](../09-reproduction-and-the-life-course/fetal-and-neonatal-physiology.md)). It is also the circuit's weakness: when the whole lung is hypoxic (altitude, chronic lung disease, obesity-hypoventilation) every vessel constricts, pressure rises, and over months the arterioles grow muscle and the right ventricle hypertrophies and fails (cor pulmonale). Anaesthetics, vasodilators and sepsis blunt the reflex and worsen oxygenation.

6. **Pulmonary capillary pressure is kept far below oncotic pressure, so oedema needs a sustained rise or a leak.** Capillary pressure is ~7–10 mmHg against a plasma oncotic pressure of ~25, so the Starling balance strongly favours a dry alveolus, and a slightly negative interstitial pressure plus abundant lymphatics drain what does filter ([microcirculation](../03-cardiovascular-system/microcirculation-and-capillary-exchange.md); [lymphatics](../03-cardiovascular-system/the-lymphatic-system.md)). Fluid first collects in the loose interstitium around bronchi and vessels (interstitial oedema, with rapid shallow breathing from stretched J receptors), and floods the alveoli only when the capacity of the interstitium and lymphatics is exceeded. Acutely that happens when left atrial pressure exceeds ~20–25 mmHg (myocardial infarction, acute mitral regurgitation); in chronic mitral stenosis the lymphatics enlarge and pressures of 30–40 mmHg are tolerated. Oedema with normal pressures means the barrier itself has been injured (acute respiratory distress syndrome, drowning, inhaled toxins), and the fluid is then protein-rich.

7. **Raised pulmonary pressure is read as pre-capillary or post-capillary, and the right ventricle is what fails.** Pulmonary hypertension is a mean pulmonary artery pressure above ~20 mmHg at rest (2022 European definition; 25 mmHg in older texts). If the left atrial pressure is high, the cause is downstream (left heart failure, mitral disease: post-capillary); if it is normal, resistance has risen in the lung's own vessels (chronic hypoxia, thromboembolism, interstitial disease, or the proliferative arteriopathy of pulmonary arterial hypertension: pre-capillary). The thin right ventricle tolerates a gradual rise by hypertrophy but cannot acutely generate a systolic pressure above ~50–60 mmHg, so a massive embolus kills by right ventricular failure, not hypoxaemia. A failing right ventricle dilates, shifts the septum into the left ventricle and starves it of filling, so output falls on both sides at once ([pulmonary vascular disease](../../12-organ-system-specialties/02-pulmonology/pulmonary-vascular-disease-and-respiratory-failure.md), [heart failure](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/heart-failure.md)).

8. **The pulmonary endothelium is a filter and a chemical processor, and the bronchial circulation is a separate systemic supply.** All venous blood passes through the lung, which traps emboli (thrombus, fat, air, tumour cells) before they reach the brain and coronaries, and whose endothelium converts angiotensin I to II, inactivates bradykinin and clears serotonin and noradrenaline; endothelial nitric oxide and prostacyclin keep the vessels dilated and endothelin constricts them, which are the three targets of the drugs used in pulmonary arterial hypertension. The airways themselves are fed by bronchial arteries from the aorta at systemic pressure (~1–2% of cardiac output), which drain partly into pulmonary veins (part of the normal 2–5% anatomical shunt) and keep lung tissue alive beyond an embolus, so infarction after pulmonary embolism is uncommon; they enlarge in chronic inflammation and are the usual source of major haemoptysis.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Pulmonary vascular resistance | (Mean pulmonary artery − left atrial pressure) / cardiac output; ~1/10 of systemic |
| Recruitment and distension | Opening of closed capillaries and widening of open ones as flow or pressure rises; resistance falls |
| Zones of West | Apex-to-base pattern of flow set by the relation of arterial, alveolar and venous pressures |
| Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction | Constriction of pulmonary arterioles to low alveolar oxygen; matches perfusion to ventilation |
| Cor pulmonale | Right ventricular hypertrophy and failure from chronic pulmonary hypertension of lung origin |
| Pulmonary capillary wedge pressure | Catheter estimate of left atrial pressure; separates pre- from post-capillary pulmonary hypertension |
| Cardiogenic vs permeability oedema | Fluid from raised capillary pressure (protein-poor) versus from an injured barrier (protein-rich) |
| Bronchial circulation | Systemic-pressure supply to the airways from the aorta; source of haemoptysis and of the anatomical shunt |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Pulmonary vessels behave like systemic vessels."** They constrict to hypoxia, dilate to rising flow, and are compressed by lung inflation; almost every systemic rule is inverted.
- **"Pulmonary embolism kills by blocking gas exchange."** It kills by acutely loading a right ventricle that cannot generate high pressure; the hypoxaemia is usually modest.
- **"Pulmonary oedema means too much fluid in the body."** It means capillary pressure or permeability in the lung has risen; a patient with a stiff left ventricle can drown in the lungs while total body water is normal.
