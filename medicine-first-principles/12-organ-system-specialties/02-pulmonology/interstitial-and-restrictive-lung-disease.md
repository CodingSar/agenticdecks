# Interstitial and restrictive lung disease

## Essence

Restrictive lung disease is the mirror of obstruction: the lung can empty but cannot fill. Either the lung itself is stiff and small because its interstitium, the thin scaffold between alveolus and capillary, has been thickened by inflammation and scar, or the lung is normal but the bellows around it (chest wall, pleura, respiratory muscles, or a nervous system that drives them) cannot expand it. The interstitial diseases are the specialty's hardest problem because they are many (over 200 named causes), their histology overlaps, and their common endpoint, fibrosis, is the body's repair response turned against the organ. The single idea is that gas exchange needs a membrane less than a micrometre thick and a lung compliant enough to be inflated by a few centimetres of water pressure; thickening the one and stiffening the other produce breathlessness on exertion, hypoxaemia and a small lung on every test.

## Core tenets

1. **Diffusion is fast only because the membrane is thin, so thickening it limits oxygen before carbon dioxide.** Fick's law makes gas transfer proportional to surface area and inversely proportional to thickness ([diffusion](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/diffusion-osmosis-and-transport.md)). The normal alveolar–capillary barrier is about 0.3 micrometres; a red cell crosses the capillary in ~0.75 s and equilibrates with alveolar gas in about a third of that, a reserve that a thickened membrane erodes ([gas exchange](../../06-physiology/04-respiratory-system/gas-exchange-and-transport.md)). On exertion transit time shortens and the reserve is gone: desaturation on walking is the characteristic early sign. Carbon dioxide diffuses about twenty times faster than oxygen and is rarely retained until very late.

2. **A stiff lung is a small lung that is expensive to inflate.** Fibrosis and cellular infiltration lower compliance, so the same inspiratory muscle effort produces a smaller volume ([mechanics](../../06-physiology/04-respiratory-system/ventilation-and-lung-mechanics.md)). The patient takes small, fast breaths, which minimises elastic work. Spirometry shows reduced volumes with a normal or high FEV1/FVC ratio, since the airways are not narrowed and recoil is increased; total lung capacity is reduced. The small, stiff lung also raises pulmonary vascular resistance, so [pulmonary hypertension](pulmonary-vascular-disease-and-respiratory-failure.md) and right heart strain are late consequences.

3. **Fibrosis is repair that never stops.** Repeated epithelial injury to the alveolus, whether from inhaled dust, drugs, radiation, autoimmune attack or an unknown trigger in a genetically susceptible, ageing epithelium, activates fibroblasts and myofibroblasts through transforming growth factor beta and lays down collagen ([repair and fibrosis](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/tissue-repair-regeneration-and-fibrosis.md)). In idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis the process is relentless and inflammation is scanty, which is why corticosteroids do not help and why the antifibrotic drugs that slow it (pirfenidone, nintedanib, which inhibits tyrosine kinases on growth factor receptors) act on fibroblast signalling instead. Median survival from diagnosis is of the order of three to five years untreated, worse than many cancers.

4. **The causes group by what the lung was exposed to, and the history finds most of them.** Occupational and environmental dusts (asbestos, silica, coal, bird and mould antigens in hypersensitivity pneumonitis), drugs (amiodarone, methotrexate, bleomycin), radiation, and connective tissue diseases (systemic sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, myositis) account for a large share; sarcoidosis, a [granulomatous](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/chronic-inflammation-and-granulomas.md) disease of unknown cause, is another. The remainder are idiopathic and classified by pattern. The occupational causes carry legal consequences and latency of decades, which is why the history asks about jobs forty years ago ([the host and its environment](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#the-host-and-its-environment)).

5. **Examination and imaging read the stiff, scarred lung directly.** Fine end-inspiratory crackles at the bases are the sound of collapsed small airways snapping open; finger clubbing accompanies fibrosis for reasons not understood. High-resolution computed tomography is the decisive test: the distribution (basal and subpleural in idiopathic fibrosis, upper-zone in sarcoidosis and most dust diseases) and the pattern (reticulation, ground-glass opacity, honeycombing, traction bronchiectasis) often identify the disease without a biopsy ([computed tomography](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/03-imaging-and-physiological-measurement/radiography-and-computed-tomography.md)). Gas transfer measured with carbon monoxide quantifies the membrane and is the most sensitive functional test. When these disagree, a multidisciplinary meeting of pulmonologist, radiologist and pathologist decides, and surgical biopsy is reserved for cases where the answer would change treatment.

6. **Treatment removes the trigger, suppresses inflammation when there is some, and slows fibrosis when there is not.** Hypersensitivity pneumonitis and drug reactions improve when the exposure stops; sarcoidosis and the connective-tissue-related diseases, which have a real inflammatory component, respond to corticosteroids and immunosuppressants ([anti-inflammatory drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/anti-inflammatory-and-immunomodulatory-drugs.md)); established fibrosis responds to nothing but antifibrotics, which slow the rate of loss of vital capacity by roughly half without reversing it, and transplantation. Oxygen corrects the hypoxaemia and relieves the right heart; pulmonary rehabilitation maintains function. Matching treatment to mechanism depends on distinguishing inflammation from scar, which is the point of the diagnostic effort.

7. **Extrapulmonary restriction is a bellows problem with a normal lung.** Severe kyphoscoliosis, obesity, a large pleural effusion, and neuromuscular diseases such as motor neurone disease and Guillain–Barré syndrome all reduce lung volumes with normal gas transfer per unit of lung. The danger here is ventilatory failure rather than hypoxaemia: weak muscles cannot sustain the minute ventilation and carbon dioxide rises, first during sleep. Nocturnal non-invasive ventilation treats the cause, and vital capacity measured lying down is the test that predicts it ([control of breathing](../../06-physiology/04-respiratory-system/control-of-breathing.md)).

8. **Who else is involved.** [Rheumatology](../09-rheumatology-and-clinical-immunology/connective-tissue-disease-and-vasculitis.md) shares every patient whose fibrosis is part of systemic sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis or myositis, and often started the methotrexate that may be the cause. [Occupational medicine](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/sports-occupational-and-lifestyle-medicine.md) and the law are involved in dust diseases. [Thoracic surgery](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/surgical-subspecialties.md) performs biopsy and transplantation. [Neurology](../10-neurology/demyelinating-and-neuromuscular-disease.md) shares the neuromuscular bellows failures. [Oncology](../07-oncology/README.md) causes some (radiation, bleomycin, immune-checkpoint pneumonitis) and must distinguish fibrosis from cancer on scans. [Palliative care](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/palliative-care-and-symptom-control.md) is needed early in progressive fibrosis.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Interstitium | The connective tissue scaffold between alveolar epithelium and capillary endothelium |
| Restrictive pattern | Reduced lung volumes with a preserved FEV1/FVC ratio |
| Gas transfer (DLCO) | Carbon monoxide uptake as a measure of membrane area and thickness |
| Compliance | Volume change per unit pressure; low in fibrosis |
| Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis | Progressive fibrosis of unknown cause with a basal, subpleural, honeycombing pattern |
| Hypersensitivity pneumonitis | Immune-mediated interstitial inflammation from inhaled organic antigen |
| Sarcoidosis | Multisystem granulomatous disease of unknown cause, usually affecting lungs and lymph nodes |
| Honeycombing | Clustered cystic airspaces of end-stage fibrosis on computed tomography |
| Pneumoconiosis | Lung disease from inhaled mineral dust |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Fibrosis is inflammation, so steroids treat it."** In idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis inflammation is minimal and steroids were shown to harm; antifibrotic drugs act on fibroblast signalling instead.
- **"A normal chest radiograph excludes interstitial disease."** Early disease is often invisible on plain film and needs high-resolution computed tomography and gas transfer.
- **"Restriction means the airways are blocked."** The airways are open; the lung or chest wall cannot expand. The FEV1/FVC ratio is normal or high.
- **"Asbestos disease is history."** Latency is 20–40 years, so disease from exposures of the 1970s–1990s is still presenting in the 2020s.
