# Valvular and structural heart disease

## Essence

Valvular and structural heart disease is cardiology as plumbing. A valve that will not open (stenosis) makes the chamber behind it generate a higher pressure; a valve that will not close (regurgitation) makes it pump a larger volume, part of which goes backwards; a hole between chambers (a septal defect) lets blood take a short cut from high pressure to low. In each case the lesion is a fixed mechanical fault, the physiology follows from fluid mechanics, the heart compensates by hypertrophy or dilatation until it cannot, and the definitive treatment is mechanical: repair, replace or close. The specialty's skill is in timing, because the fault is often silent for decades, and the repair must come after symptoms justify its risk but before the compensating ventricle is irreversibly damaged.

## Core tenets

1. **A stenosis imposes a pressure load; a regurgitation imposes a volume load; the ventricle compensates differently for each.** Flow through a narrowed orifice needs a pressure gradient that rises with the square of velocity ([Bernoulli](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/fluids-pressure-and-flow.md)). Aortic stenosis therefore forces the left ventricle to generate systolic pressures of 200 mmHg or more, and it responds with concentric hypertrophy: thicker walls normalise wall stress (Laplace's law) at the cost of stiffness and oxygen demand. Regurgitation returns blood to the chamber, which dilates to hold the extra volume (eccentric hypertrophy) and uses the [Frank–Starling](../../06-physiology/03-cardiovascular-system/the-cardiac-cycle-and-pump-function.md) reserve to eject it. Both compensations are finite and both end in [heart failure](heart-failure.md).

2. **Murmurs are turbulence, and their timing tells you which valve and which fault.** Blood flowing through a narrowed or leaking orifice exceeds the Reynolds number for laminar flow and vibrates the surrounding structures. Aortic stenosis and mitral regurgitation produce systolic murmurs (the ventricle is ejecting); aortic regurgitation and mitral stenosis produce diastolic ones. The loudness relates poorly to severity: a very tight stenosis with low flow can be quiet. The physical signs that do track severity are the pressure effects: a slow-rising, low-volume pulse in aortic stenosis, a collapsing pulse with wide pulse pressure in aortic regurgitation ([physical examination](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/01-the-clinical-encounter/physical-examination-and-signs.md)).

3. **Echocardiography measures the lesion through Doppler physics.** The Doppler shift gives the velocity of blood through the valve; the simplified Bernoulli equation (gradient in mmHg equals four times velocity squared in metres per second) converts it to a pressure gradient. Conservation of mass (continuity) gives the orifice area from flow upstream and velocity through the valve. Colour Doppler shows a regurgitant jet. The same study measures chamber size and ejection fraction, which is what decides timing of surgery: a dilating or failing ventricle is the signal that compensation is ending ([ultrasound](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/03-imaging-and-physiological-measurement/ultrasound.md)).

4. **The causes are degeneration, rheumatic scarring, infection and congenital malformation, and their balance has shifted.** In high-income countries most aortic stenosis is calcific degeneration of a normal or bicuspid valve in older adults, a process with the lipid deposition and inflammation of [atherosclerosis](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/atherosclerosis.md). Rheumatic heart disease, an [autoimmune](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/04-immunity-in-disease/autoimmunity.md) sequel of streptococcal pharyngitis in which antibodies cross-react with valve tissue, remains the leading cause worldwide (~40 million cases, 2019 estimate) and the classic cause of mitral stenosis. Infective endocarditis is bacterial colonisation of a damaged valve or a prosthesis, seeding emboli and destroying tissue ([pathogenesis](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/01-microbes-and-infection/pathogenesis-virulence-and-toxins.md)). Congenital lesions arise from [faulty septation and valve formation](../../04-genetics-evolution-and-development/03-development/germ-layers-and-organogenesis.md).

5. **Shunts follow pressure, and their direction decides the symptoms.** A septal defect lets blood flow from the high-pressure left heart to the right, overloading the pulmonary circulation; the lungs are flooded but the blood is oxygenated, so the child is breathless, not blue. If pulmonary pressure rises to match or exceed systemic pressure, or if an obstruction to right ventricular outflow exists (tetralogy of Fallot), the shunt reverses and deoxygenated blood reaches the aorta: cyanosis. Long-standing left-to-right shunts remodel the pulmonary arterioles until the raised resistance is fixed, after which closing the defect is too late ([pulmonary vascular disease](../02-pulmonology/pulmonary-vascular-disease-and-respiratory-failure.md)).

6. **Treatment is mechanical, and the decision is about timing.** Drugs cannot widen a stenosis or close a leak; they can lower afterload in regurgitation and treat the resulting failure, but the only treatment that changes the natural history of severe aortic stenosis is replacing the valve, after which the pressure load vanishes and the hypertrophied ventricle partially regresses. Replacement can be surgical or by catheter; a mechanical prosthesis lasts indefinitely but needs lifelong anticoagulation, a biological one needs none but degenerates in 10–20 years ([devices and biomaterials](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/04-non-drug-therapeutics/devices-implants-and-biomaterials.md)). Operating before symptoms exposes the patient to surgical risk for no present gain; operating after the ventricle has failed leaves a damaged pump. Serial echocardiography watches for the moment between.

7. **Who else is involved.** [Cardiac surgery](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/surgical-subspecialties.md) is the partner in every severe lesion, and the "heart team" that chooses between surgical and catheter approaches is the model multidisciplinary meeting. [Infectious diseases](../08-infectious-diseases/README.md) co-manages endocarditis, where the decision to operate on an infected valve is jointly made. [Paediatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/README.md) owns congenital disease until adulthood. [Obstetrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/README.md) is involved because pregnancy raises cardiac output by 40–50% and stenotic lesions tolerate it badly, and because warfarin is teratogenic. [Haematology](../06-haematology/README.md) shares the anticoagulation of mechanical valves.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Stenosis | A valve that opens incompletely, imposing a pressure gradient and pressure load |
| Regurgitation | A valve that closes incompletely, letting blood flow backwards and imposing a volume load |
| Concentric hypertrophy | Wall thickening without dilatation; the response to pressure load |
| Eccentric hypertrophy | Dilatation with proportionate wall growth; the response to volume load |
| Murmur | Audible turbulence from flow through an abnormal orifice |
| Bernoulli gradient | Pressure drop across a valve estimated from Doppler velocity |
| Shunt | Abnormal communication letting blood bypass part of the circulation |
| Eisenmenger syndrome | Shunt reversal after pulmonary vascular resistance becomes fixed |
| Endocarditis | Infection of a valve or endocardial surface, usually bacterial |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A loud murmur means a severe lesion."** Loudness depends on flow and chest wall; critical stenosis with failing output can be nearly silent.
- **"Valve disease should be fixed as soon as it is found."** Most lesions are watched for years; the risk of intervention is justified only once symptoms or ventricular change appear.
- **"Rheumatic heart disease has disappeared."** It has declined in high-income countries but remains the leading cause of valve disease worldwide.
- **"A hole in the heart makes a child blue."** A simple left-to-right shunt causes breathlessness and lung flooding; cyanosis needs right-to-left flow.
