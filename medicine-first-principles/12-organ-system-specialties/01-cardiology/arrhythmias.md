## Essence

An arrhythmia is a failure of the heart's timing system: too slow, too fast, or disorganised. The normal heart is a single oscillator (the sinus node) driving a syncytium through a fixed conduction path with a built-in delay (the atrioventricular node), and the whole design depends on every cell being refractory when the wave has just passed so that the wave cannot turn back on itself. Almost every clinically important arrhythmia is one of three faults: the oscillator or the wire fails (bradycardia and block), an extra oscillator appears (automaticity), or the wave finds a loop it can circle forever (re-entry). The consequences follow from haemodynamics: a heart beating at 30 or 300 per minute pumps poorly, and an atrium that quivers rather than contracts lets blood stand still and clot.

## Core tenets

1. **Normal rhythm is one pacemaker, one path and a universal refractory period.** The sinus node has the fastest intrinsic rate because of its pacemaker current, so it suppresses the slower latent pacemakers below it by firing first ([cardiac electrophysiology](../../06-physiology/03-cardiovascular-system/cardiac-electrophysiology.md)). The atrioventricular node delays conduction so the atria finish before the ventricles start, and is the only normal electrical connection between them. The long plateau of the ventricular action potential makes the cell refractory until contraction is nearly over, which both prevents tetany and prevents a wave from re-exciting tissue it has just left.

2. **Bradycardia is a failed pacemaker or a broken wire.** Sinus node disease gives pauses and slow rates; atrioventricular block gives a ventricular escape rhythm driven by a latent pacemaker at 30–40 per minute, too slow to sustain output on standing. Both come from fibrosis of the conduction tissue with age, from [infarction](ischemic-heart-disease.md) of its blood supply, or from drugs that slow conduction (beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers, digoxin). The treatment is mechanical rather than chemical: a pacemaker replaces the oscillator, and the symptom (syncope, fatigue) resolves because output is restored.

3. **Re-entry needs a loop, one-way block and slow conduction, and it explains most tachycardias.** If tissue around an obstacle (a scar, a valve ring, an accessory pathway) conducts at different speeds in two limbs, a wave can block in one limb, travel down the other, and return up the first after it has recovered, then circle indefinitely. The circuit length divided by conduction velocity must exceed the refractory period, which is why slowing conduction or shortening refractoriness can start a re-entrant rhythm and lengthening refractoriness can stop it. Atrial flutter, most ventricular tachycardia after infarction, and the common supraventricular tachycardias are all re-entry; they start and stop abruptly, as a circuit does.

4. **Atrial fibrillation is many wavelets in a stretched, fibrosed atrium, and its main danger is stroke.** Atrial enlargement from [hypertension](hypertension-and-vascular-disease.md), [valve disease](valvular-and-structural-heart-disease.md) or [heart failure](heart-failure.md), with age-related fibrosis, lets multiple re-entrant wavelets coexist. The atrium stops contracting; the ventricle responds irregularly at whatever rate the atrioventricular node permits. Loss of atrial contraction costs 10–20% of output and matters most in a stiff ventricle. Stasis in the left atrial appendage allows a thrombus to form ([Virchow's triad](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/thrombosis-embolism-and-infarction.md)), and embolism causes about a fifth of ischaemic strokes. Anticoagulation reduces that risk by roughly two-thirds in trials, at the cost of bleeding, so the decision is a harm–benefit calculation per patient.

5. **The Electrocardiogram (ECG) localises the fault by what is missing or widened.** A narrow QRS complex means the ventricles were activated through the normal conduction system, so the rhythm arises above it; a wide one means ventricular origin or a blocked bundle. Absent P waves with an irregular rhythm is fibrillation; P waves that march independently of QRS complexes is complete block; a saw-tooth baseline is flutter. The ECG shows timing, not output, so a patient's blood pressure and consciousness are measured alongside it to decide urgency.

6. **Antiarrhythmic drugs change ion channel kinetics and can therefore cause the arrhythmias they treat.** Sodium-channel blockers slow conduction, potassium-channel blockers (amiodarone, sotalol) prolong refractoriness, and beta-blockers and calcium-channel blockers slow the nodes ([autonomic and cardiovascular drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/autonomic-and-cardiovascular-drugs.md)). Prolonging repolarisation also allows early after-depolarisations and torsades de pointes; slowing conduction in scarred ventricles can create re-entry. Large trials showed sodium-channel blockers given after infarction to suppress ectopy increased mortality, one of medicine's clearest lessons that a surrogate marker is not an outcome ([randomised trials](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/02-evidence-based-medicine/randomized-controlled-trials.md)).

7. **Electricity and ablation treat the circuit directly.** A synchronised shock depolarises the whole heart at once, erasing every wavelet and letting the sinus node resume; it is the only effective treatment for ventricular fibrillation. Catheter ablation maps the circuit and burns or freezes a critical limb so the loop cannot close, which cures many supraventricular tachycardias. An implantable defibrillator detects ventricular fibrillation and shocks it, and lowers mortality in patients with severe ventricular impairment, in whom [sudden death](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/resuscitation-and-cardiac-arrest.md) is otherwise common.

8. **Who else is involved.** [Neurology](../10-neurology/stroke.md) receives the embolic strokes and returns the question of when to anticoagulate after one. [Endocrinology](../05-endocrinology/thyroid-disease.md) is consulted because thyrotoxicosis causes atrial fibrillation. [Nephrology](../03-nephrology/electrolyte-and-acid-base-disorders.md) matters because potassium, magnesium and calcium set resting potential and repolarisation, and dialysis patients die of arrhythmia. [Emergency medicine](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/README.md) and [critical care](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/03-critical-care/README.md) manage the unstable rhythms; [anaesthesia](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/README.md) manages pacemakers and defibrillators around surgery.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Automaticity | Spontaneous depolarisation of a cell to threshold; normal in nodes, abnormal elsewhere |
| Re-entry | A wave circulating around an obstacle through tissue that has recovered; the mechanism of most tachycardias |
| Refractory period | Interval after excitation during which a cell cannot be re-excited |
| Atrioventricular block | Failure of conduction between atria and ventricles, graded by how many impulses fail |
| Atrial fibrillation | Chaotic atrial activity with no effective atrial contraction and an irregular ventricular response |
| Ventricular fibrillation | Chaotic ventricular activity with no output; fatal within minutes unless shocked |
| QT interval | ECG measure of ventricular repolarisation time; prolongation predisposes to torsades de pointes |
| Cardioversion | A synchronised shock that resets the heart to sinus rhythm |
| Ablation | Destruction of a critical part of an arrhythmia circuit by catheter |

## Common misconceptions

- **"An irregular pulse is always abnormal."** Sinus rate normally varies with breathing, especially in the young; irregularity is diagnosed on the ECG, not the wrist.
- **"Suppressing extra beats prevents sudden death."** Ectopic beats are a marker, not a cause; suppressing them with sodium-channel blockers after infarction increased deaths.
- **"A defibrillator restarts a stopped heart."** It stops a fibrillating one; a heart with no electrical activity (asystole) cannot be shocked into rhythm.
