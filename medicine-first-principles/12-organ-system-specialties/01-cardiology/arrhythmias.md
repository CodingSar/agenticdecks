## Essence

An arrhythmia is a failure of the heart's timing system: too slow, too fast, or disorganised. The normal heart is a single oscillator (the sinus node) driving a syncytium through a fixed conduction path with a built-in delay (the atrioventricular node), and the design depends on tissue the wave has just passed being refractory, so the wave cannot turn back. Almost every important arrhythmia is one of three faults: the oscillator or wire fails (bradycardia and block), an extra oscillator appears (automaticity), or the wave finds a loop to circle (re-entry). The consequences follow from haemodynamics: a heart beating at 30 or 300 per minute pumps poorly, and an atrium that quivers rather than contracts lets blood stand still and clot.

## Core tenets

1. **Normal rhythm is one pacemaker, one path and a universal refractory period.** The sinus node has the fastest intrinsic rate, so it suppresses the slower latent pacemakers below it by firing first ([cardiac electrophysiology](../../06-physiology/03-cardiovascular-system/cardiac-electrophysiology.md)). The atrioventricular node delays conduction so the atria finish before the ventricles start, and is the only normal electrical connection between them. The long plateau of the ventricular action potential keeps the cell refractory until contraction is nearly over, which prevents tetany and re-excitation.

2. **Bradycardia is a failed pacemaker or a broken wire.** Sinus node disease gives pauses and slow rates; atrioventricular block gives a ventricular escape rhythm driven by a latent pacemaker at 30–40 per minute, too slow to sustain output on standing. Both come from age-related fibrosis of the conduction tissue, from [infarction](ischemic-heart-disease.md) of its blood supply, or from drugs that slow conduction. The treatment is mechanical: a pacemaker replaces the oscillator and the syncope resolves because output is restored.

3. **Re-entry needs a loop, one-way block and slow conduction, and it explains most tachycardias.** If tissue around an obstacle (a scar, a valve ring, an accessory pathway) conducts at different speeds in two limbs, a wave can block in one, travel down the other, return up the first once it has recovered, and circle indefinitely. The circuit length divided by conduction velocity must exceed the refractory period, so slowing conduction or shortening refractoriness can start re-entry and lengthening refractoriness can stop it. Atrial flutter, post-infarction ventricular tachycardia and the common supraventricular tachycardias are all re-entry; they start and stop abruptly, as a circuit does.

4. **Atrial fibrillation is many wavelets in a stretched, fibrosed atrium, and its main danger is stroke.** Atrial enlargement from [hypertension](hypertension-and-vascular-disease.md), [valve disease](valvular-and-structural-heart-disease.md) or [heart failure](heart-failure.md), with age-related fibrosis, lets multiple re-entrant wavelets coexist. The atrium stops contracting, costing 10–20% of output (more in a stiff ventricle); the ventricle responds irregularly at whatever rate the atrioventricular node permits. Stasis in the left atrial appendage allows a thrombus to form ([Virchow's triad](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/thrombosis-embolism-and-infarction.md)), and embolism causes about a fifth of ischaemic strokes. Anticoagulation cuts that risk by roughly two-thirds in trials, at the cost of bleeding: a harm–benefit calculation per patient.

5. **The Electrocardiogram (ECG) localises the fault by what is missing or widened.** A narrow QRS complex means the ventricles were activated through the normal conduction system, so the rhythm arises above it; a wide one means ventricular origin or a blocked bundle. Absent P waves with an irregular rhythm is fibrillation; P waves marching independently of QRS complexes is complete block. The ECG shows timing, not output, so blood pressure and consciousness decide urgency.

6. **Antiarrhythmic drugs change ion channel kinetics and can therefore cause the arrhythmias they treat.** Sodium-channel blockers slow conduction, potassium-channel blockers prolong refractoriness, and beta-blockers and calcium-channel blockers slow the nodes ([autonomic and cardiovascular drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/autonomic-and-cardiovascular-drugs.md)). Prolonging repolarisation also allows early after-depolarisations and torsades de pointes; slowing conduction in scarred ventricles can create re-entry. Sodium-channel blockers given after infarction to suppress ectopy increased mortality in [randomised trials](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/02-evidence-based-medicine/randomized-controlled-trials.md): a surrogate marker is not an outcome.

7. **Electricity and ablation treat the circuit directly.** A shock depolarises the whole heart at once, erasing every wavelet and letting the sinus node resume; it is the only effective treatment for ventricular fibrillation. Catheter ablation burns or freezes a critical limb of the circuit so the loop cannot close, and cures many supraventricular tachycardias. An implantable defibrillator detects and shocks ventricular fibrillation, and lowers mortality where ventricular function is poor and [sudden death](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/resuscitation-and-cardiac-arrest.md) otherwise common.

8. **Who else is involved.** [Neurology](../10-neurology/stroke.md) receives the embolic strokes and returns the question of when to anticoagulate after one. [Endocrinology](../05-endocrinology/thyroid-disease.md), because thyrotoxicosis causes atrial fibrillation. [Nephrology](../03-nephrology/electrolyte-and-acid-base-disorders.md), because potassium, magnesium and calcium set resting potential and repolarisation, and dialysis patients die of arrhythmia. [Emergency medicine](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/README.md) and [critical care](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/03-critical-care/README.md) manage unstable rhythms; [anaesthesia](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/README.md) manages devices around surgery.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Automaticity | Spontaneous depolarisation of a cell to threshold; normal in nodes, abnormal elsewhere |
| Re-entry | A wave circulating around an obstacle through tissue that has recovered; the mechanism of most tachycardias |
| Refractory period | Interval after excitation during which a cell cannot be re-excited |
| Atrioventricular block | Failure of conduction between atria and ventricles, graded by how many impulses fail |
| Atrial fibrillation | Chaotic atrial activity with no effective atrial contraction and an irregular ventricular response |
| Ventricular fibrillation | Chaotic ventricular activity with no output; fatal within minutes unless shocked |
| QT interval | ECG measure of ventricular repolarisation time; prolongation predisposes to torsades de pointes |

## Common misconceptions

- **"An irregular pulse is always abnormal."** Sinus rate varies with breathing, especially in the young; the rhythm is diagnosed on the ECG, not the wrist.
- **"Suppressing extra beats prevents sudden death."** Ectopic beats are a marker, not a cause; suppressing them after infarction increased deaths.
