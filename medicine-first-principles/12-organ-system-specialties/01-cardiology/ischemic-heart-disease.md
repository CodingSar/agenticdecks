# Ischaemic heart disease

## Essence

Ischaemic heart disease is a supply–demand failure in a muscle that cannot pause. The myocardium extracts about 70% of the oxygen in coronary blood at rest, so it has almost no extraction reserve and must meet any rise in demand by increasing flow. [Atherosclerosis](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/atherosclerosis.md) narrows an epicardial artery slowly, producing predictable pain on exertion (stable angina); rupture of a plaque with [thrombosis](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/thrombosis-embolism-and-infarction.md) stops flow suddenly, producing an acute coronary syndrome and, within hours, infarction. Everything in the specialty's response follows from one fact: ischaemic myocardium is salvageable for a few hours and then becomes scar, so the disease is treated as a race against time in the acute phase and as a slowing of atherosclerosis for the rest of life.

## Core tenets

1. **Coronary flow is the only reserve, and it happens in diastole.** Resting myocardium extracts ~70% of delivered oxygen (skeletal muscle ~25%), so a doubling of demand needs a near-doubling of flow. The left ventricle compresses its own intramural vessels in systole, so flow to it occurs mostly in [diastole](../../06-physiology/03-cardiovascular-system/the-cardiac-cycle-and-pump-function.md). Tachycardia therefore cuts supply (shorter diastole) while raising demand (more beats): the worst of both, and the reason slowing the heart treats angina.

2. **A stenosis matters only once it exhausts autoregulation.** Downstream arterioles dilate to keep resting flow constant until an epicardial narrowing is about 70% of diameter; beyond that, resting flow is maintained but there is nothing left for exercise. Flow through a tube falls with the fourth power of radius ([Poiseuille](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/fluids-pressure-and-flow.md)), so the last few percent of narrowing cost most of the flow. Stable angina is the symptom of a fixed stenosis meeting a demand it cannot serve: predictable, exertional, relieved by rest within minutes.

3. **Acute coronary syndromes are plaque rupture plus thrombus, and the thrombus decides the type.** A thin-capped plaque tears, exposes its lipid core, and platelets then fibrin form a clot. A clot that occludes the vessel completely causes transmural ischaemia, seen on the Electrocardiogram (ECG) as ST-segment elevation; a clot that occludes it partly or intermittently causes subendocardial ischaemia with ST depression or T-wave inversion. The culprit is often a plaque that was not the tightest stenosis; rupture depends on cap thickness and inflammation, not on lumen size, which is why angina severity predicts infarction poorly.

4. **Ischaemia produces its symptoms and signs through metabolic failure, not pain receptors in the lumen.** Within seconds of flow stopping, oxidative phosphorylation fails, Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) falls, the cell switches to glycolysis, and lactate, adenosine and potassium accumulate. Adenosine and protons excite sympathetic afferents that converge on spinal segments T1–T5, which is why the pain is felt in the chest, arm and jaw ([referred pain](../../05-tissues-and-anatomy/02-body-organization/segmental-innervation-dermatomes-and-referred-pain.md)). Potassium leak alters repolarisation and gives the ECG change; loss of contraction in the ischaemic segment gives the new regional wall-motion abnormality on echocardiography and, if large, hypotension and pulmonary oedema.

5. **Troponin is the signature of necrosis, and its time course is the diagnostic logic.** Cardiac troponins are structural proteins released only when the sarcolemma breaks down, so they mark infarction rather than ischaemia. They rise within hours and stay raised for days. A single value therefore cannot separate acute from recent events; a *change* between two measurements does. Because the assays detect tiny amounts, many non-coronary injuries (sepsis, renal failure, pulmonary embolism, tachyarrhythmia) raise troponin, and the test must be read with the pre-test probability from [history and ECG](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/01-the-clinical-encounter/using-tests-pretest-probability-and-thresholds.md).

6. **In infarction, time is muscle: reperfusion reverses the mechanism directly.** Necrosis spreads as a wavefront from the subendocardium outward over about six hours. Opening the artery, by catheter and stent or by a fibrinolytic drug that activates plasminogen, salvages whatever has not yet died; the benefit falls steeply with each hour of delay. Antiplatelet drugs (aspirin, a P2Y12 antagonist such as clopidogrel) and anticoagulants stop the thrombus propagating. The surviving ventricle's function is the main determinant of long-term outcome, which is why infarct size links this leaf to [heart failure](heart-failure.md).

7. **Long-term treatment slows atherosclerosis and reduces demand; it rarely removes the stenosis.** Statins inhibit cholesterol synthesis, lower Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL) and stabilise plaques; the fall in events is proportional to the fall in LDL. Beta-blockers lower heart rate and contractility (demand) and lengthen diastole (supply). Nitrates venodilate, lowering preload and wall stress. Revascularisation relieves angina but, in stable disease, has not consistently reduced death or infarction compared with such therapy, because the plaques that rupture are not the ones that were stented ([randomised trials](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/02-evidence-based-medicine/randomized-controlled-trials.md) settled this against intuition).

8. **Who else is involved.** [Emergency medicine](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/README.md) and prehospital services make the first diagnosis and start the clock. [Cardiac surgery](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/surgical-subspecialties.md) is chosen over stenting for multivessel disease, especially in diabetes. [Endocrinology](../05-endocrinology/README.md) and [primary care](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/README.md) own the risk factors (diabetes, lipids, blood pressure, smoking) that decide the next event. [Neurology](../10-neurology/stroke.md) shares the same atherosclerotic patient and the same antiplatelet decisions, and [rehabilitation](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/rehabilitation-and-disability.md) delivers the exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation that reduces mortality.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Angina | Chest discomfort from reversible myocardial ischaemia; stable if predictable and exertional |
| Acute coronary syndrome | Plaque rupture with thrombosis: unstable angina or myocardial infarction |
| ST-elevation myocardial infarction | Complete thrombotic occlusion with transmural ischaemia; the emergency for reperfusion |
| Troponin | Cardiomyocyte structural protein whose rise and fall marks myocardial necrosis |
| Coronary flow reserve | Ratio of maximal to resting coronary flow; the capacity lost by a stenosis |
| Vulnerable plaque | Thin fibrous cap over a large lipid core with inflammation; prone to rupture regardless of lumen size |
| Reperfusion | Restoring flow to ischaemic myocardium by catheter or fibrinolysis before necrosis is complete |
| Revascularisation | Bypass grafting or stenting to restore flow past a stenosis |

## Common misconceptions

- **"The tightest stenosis is the one that will cause the heart attack."** Rupture depends on plaque composition and inflammation; many infarcts arise from plaques under 50% stenosis, which is why stenting stable lesions does not prevent infarction.
- **"A normal troponin rules out a heart attack."** It rules out necrosis at the time of sampling; a second sample after an interval is needed, and unstable angina by definition has no troponin rise.
- **"Angina means the heart is being damaged."** Stable angina is reversible ischaemia without necrosis; the damage comes from the rupture event, which angina severity predicts poorly.
