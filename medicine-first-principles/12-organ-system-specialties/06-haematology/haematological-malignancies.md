## Essence

Haematological malignancies are cancers of blood-forming and immune cells, and their behaviour follows from the biology of the cell that went wrong. Haematopoiesis is a hierarchy from stem cell to mature cell, and a mutation can arrest a clone at any rung. Where it is frozen determines the disease: an early blast gives acute leukaemia, which kills in weeks by crowding out normal marrow; a mature, still-functional cell gives a chronic leukaemia, lymphoma or myeloma that may smoulder for years. Three consequences unify every form: marrow failure (anaemia, infection, bleeding), infiltration (nodes, spleen, bone) and the clone's products (paraprotein, cytokines). Because the cells are accessible in blood and marrow, these were the first cancers defined by their genetics and treated by drugs aimed at one mutant protein.

## Core tenets

1. **The stage of differentiation at which the clone is arrested predicts the tempo of the disease.** Acute leukaemias are blasts that proliferate but cannot mature, and the marrow fails within weeks. Chronic leukaemias are expanded populations of near-mature cells that function partially, so patients may be well for years. Lymphomas are lymphoid cells arrested in a node rather than in blood; myeloma is a plasma cell that still secretes antibody. Classification ([tumour classification](../../08-pathology/03-neoplasia/tumour-classification-grading-and-staging.md)) uses lineage, maturity and, increasingly, the driver mutation.

2. **These cancers taught us that a single genetic lesion can be the whole disease.** The Philadelphia chromosome, a translocation joining BCR and ABL1, produces a constitutively active tyrosine kinase that alone drives chronic myeloid leukaemia ([oncogenes](../../08-pathology/03-neoplasia/oncogenes-tumour-suppressors-and-genomic-instability.md)). Its inhibitor, imatinib, converted a fatal disease into a chronic one and became the model for targeted therapy. Many lymphomas carry translocations that put a growth or survival gene (MYC, BCL2) under an antibody-gene promoter, a side effect of the deliberate DNA breakage B cells use to make antibodies ([B cells and antibodies](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/03-adaptive-immunity/b-cells-and-antibodies.md)).

3. **Marrow failure produces the presenting triad.** When blasts fill the marrow, normal production stops: anaemia causes fatigue and breathlessness, neutropenia causes infection, and thrombocytopenia causes bruising and bleeding ([anaemias](anaemias.md), [bleeding disorders](bleeding-and-clotting-disorders.md)). A blood count with the three lines falling together, often with circulating blasts, is the first test; marrow biopsy with flow cytometry and cytogenetics makes the diagnosis ([histopathology and molecular diagnostics](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/02-laboratory-medicine/histopathology-cytology-and-molecular-diagnostics.md)).

4. **Infiltration and secretion produce the rest.** Lymphoma presents as painless enlarging nodes with fever, night sweats and weight loss from cytokine release. Myeloma plasma cells secrete a monoclonal immunoglobulin (paraprotein) that damages the kidney, activate osteoclasts to dissolve bone (lytic lesions, hypercalcaemia) and suppress normal antibodies; the tetrad of hypercalcaemia, renal failure, anaemia and bone lesions follows directly from plasma-cell biology ([calcium and bone mineral metabolism](../../06-physiology/07-endocrine-system/calcium-and-bone-mineral-metabolism.md)).

5. **Treatment exploits the fact that haematopoiesis can be destroyed and rebuilt.** Cytotoxic chemotherapy kills dividing cells, malignant and normal alike ([anticancer drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/anticancer-drugs.md)); in acute leukaemia the strategy is to empty the marrow and let normal stem cells regrow faster than leukaemic ones. Where that fails, the marrow is replaced by stem-cell transplantation from the patient (autologous) or a donor (allogeneic); a donor graft also brings T cells that attack residual leukaemia, at the price of graft-versus-host disease ([transplant and tumour immunology](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/04-immunity-in-disease/transplant-and-tumour-immunology.md)).

6. **Immunotherapy works best here because the target is a lineage, not a tumour.** All B cells express CD19 and CD20, and a patient can live without B cells on antibody replacement, so monoclonal antibodies such as rituximab and chimeric antigen receptor T cells (CAR-T) eliminate a whole lineage to remove the clone within it ([biologics and advanced therapies](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/biologics-and-advanced-therapies.md)). Solid tumours lack such clean lineage markers, which is why CAR-T succeeded here first.

7. **Measurable residual disease has replaced "remission" as the thing that matters.** Morphological remission (under 5% blasts) can hide a billion malignant cells. Flow cytometry and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) detect one cell in ten thousand to a million, and residual disease measured this way predicts relapse better than any clinical feature and now guides escalation ([molecular techniques](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/03-genetic-information-flow/molecular-techniques.md)).

8. **Who else is involved.** Haematological malignancy borders [oncology](../07-oncology/README.md), which shares its drugs and trial culture; [infectious diseases](../08-infectious-diseases/README.md), because neutropenic fever is the commonest cause of death during treatment; [transfusion medicine](transfusion-medicine.md), which supports months of marrow suppression; [nephrology](../03-nephrology/README.md) over myeloma kidney and tumour lysis; [palliative care](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/README.md), since many patients are old; and [paediatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/README.md), where acute lymphoblastic leukaemia is the commonest and one of the most curable childhood cancers.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Blast | Immature haematopoietic precursor; over 20% in marrow defines acute leukaemia |
| Clonal | Derived from one ancestral cell, shown by a shared mutation or rearrangement |
| Philadelphia chromosome | t(9;22) translocation producing the BCR-ABL1 fusion kinase |
| Lymphoma | Malignancy of lymphocytes presenting mainly in nodes and tissues; Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin types |
| Myeloma | Malignancy of plasma cells secreting a monoclonal immunoglobulin |
| Paraprotein | Monoclonal immunoglobulin or light chain in serum or urine |
| Stem-cell transplantation | Replacing marrow after high-dose therapy, from self (autologous) or donor (allogeneic) |
| Graft-versus-host disease | Donor T cells attacking recipient tissues; the price of graft-versus-leukaemia |
| Measurable residual disease | Malignant cells detected by flow cytometry or PCR below the morphological threshold |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Leukaemia means a high white cell count."** Many present with a low count; the diagnosis is that the cells are abnormal, not that there are many.
- **"A chronic leukaemia is a mild one."** Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia can be watched for decades, but chronic myeloid leukaemia untreated transforms into acute leukaemia within a few years; "chronic" describes tempo, not threat.
- **"Lymphoma is a cancer of the lymph nodes."** It is a cancer of lymphocytes, which can arise in gut, skin, brain or marrow; nodes are simply where lymphocytes live.
