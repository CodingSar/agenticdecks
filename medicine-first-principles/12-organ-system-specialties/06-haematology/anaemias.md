## Essence

Anaemia is a haemoglobin concentration too low to carry the oxygen the tissues demand, and it is a sign, not a diagnosis. Red cells are made in the marrow at roughly 2 million per second, live about 120 days, and are destroyed in the spleen; their number is a steady state between production and loss. Every anaemia is therefore one of three failures: the marrow makes too few cells (lack of iron, vitamin B12 or folate; marrow suppression or infiltration; too little erythropoietin), cells are destroyed too early (haemolysis), or cells are lost (bleeding). The clinician's method is to ask which of the three has failed, using cell size and the reticulocyte count as the first two questions, because each failure has a different cause upstream and a different fix.

## Core tenets

1. **Anaemia is a production–destruction imbalance, and the reticulocyte count tells you which side has failed.** Reticulocytes are red cells released within the last day or so. A marrow responding properly to anaemia raises the reticulocyte fraction several-fold; a low or normal count in an anaemic patient means the marrow itself is the problem (hypoproliferative), while a high count means cells are being lost or destroyed and the marrow is compensating. This one measurement halves the differential before any cause is considered.

2. **Cell size encodes the mechanism of underproduction.** Haemoglobin synthesis and nuclear maturation proceed on different schedules. Too little haemoglobin per cell (iron deficiency, thalassaemia, chronic inflammation) yields small, pale cells (microcytic, low Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV)). Impaired Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) synthesis from B12 or folate deficiency delays nuclear division while cytoplasm keeps growing, yielding large cells (macrocytic) and hypersegmented neutrophils. A normal-sized cell with low reticulocytes points to marrow suppression, kidney disease or early iron deficiency. Mechanism is read directly from morphology.

3. **Iron deficiency is a supply-chain disease, so the question is always where the iron went.** The body has no excretory route for iron; it loses about 1–2 mg a day through shed cells and controls balance only at absorption, gated by hepcidin ([red cells, haemoglobin and iron](../../06-physiology/08-blood/red-cells-haemoglobin-and-iron.md)). Deficiency therefore means chronic blood loss (a bleeding gut lesion, heavy menstruation), inadequate intake or absorption (coeliac disease, gastric surgery), or the demands of pregnancy and growth. Low ferritin confirms the deficit; the clinically load-bearing step is finding the leak, which is why iron deficiency in an older adult is a gastroenterology problem until proven otherwise.

4. **Inflammation causes anaemia by hiding iron, not by lacking it.** Interleukin-6 raises hepcidin, which degrades ferroportin and locks iron inside macrophages and enterocytes ([cytokines](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/02-innate-immunity/cytokines-interferons-and-nk-cells.md)). Serum iron falls while ferritin (an acute-phase protein) stays normal or high. This anaemia of chronic disease is an evolved strategy that starves bacteria of iron ([evolutionary medicine](../../04-genetics-evolution-and-development/02-evolution/evolutionary-medicine.md)); giving oral iron does little because absorption is the blocked step. Treating the inflammation releases the iron.

5. **Haemolysis is a shortened red-cell lifespan, and its signature is the debris of destruction.** Whether the defect is in the membrane (hereditary spherocytosis), the enzymes (glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency), the haemoglobin (sickle cell disease), or external (autoantibodies, mechanical valves, microangiopathy), destroyed cells release lactate dehydrogenase and haemoglobin, which is converted to unconjugated bilirubin and binds up haptoglobin. The triad of raised reticulocytes, raised bilirubin and low haptoglobin says "haemolysis"; a direct antiglobulin test then separates immune from non-immune causes ([hypersensitivity](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/04-immunity-in-disease/hypersensitivity-and-allergy.md)). Sickle cell disease illustrates how one amino-acid substitution polymerises haemoglobin at low oxygen tension, making cells rigid, obstructing microvessels and causing both haemolysis and painful infarction ([single-gene disorders](../../08-pathology/04-genetic-metabolic-and-environmental-disease/single-gene-and-chromosomal-disorders.md)).

6. **Symptoms come from oxygen delivery and the compensation for its loss.** Delivery is cardiac output times arterial oxygen content, and content is almost all haemoglobin ([gas exchange and transport](../../06-physiology/04-respiratory-system/gas-exchange-and-transport.md)). As haemoglobin falls, the body raises cardiac output, redistributes flow and shifts the oxygen dissociation curve rightwards via 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate. Fatigue, breathlessness on exertion, palpitations and a flow murmur are the compensation; angina or heart failure appear when the heart cannot sustain it. A slow anaemia is tolerated to remarkably low values; the same value reached in hours by bleeding is shock, because volume is lost along with cells ([haemorrhage and shock](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/hemorrhage-shock-and-sepsis.md)).

7. **Treatment replaces the missing input or removes the destroyer; transfusion buys time, not cure.** Iron, B12 or folate replacement works only when deficiency is the mechanism, and the response (a reticulocyte surge within a week) confirms the diagnosis. Recombinant erythropoietin treats the anaemia of kidney failure because that is the missing hormone. Immune haemolysis responds to immunosuppression, and the spleen is removed when it is the site of destruction. Transfusion restores oxygen carriage immediately but carries its own costs ([transfusion medicine](transfusion-medicine.md)), so it is reserved for symptomatic or rapidly falling anaemia.

8. **Who else is involved.** Iron deficiency sends the patient to [gastroenterology](../04-gastroenterology-and-hepatology/README.md) for endoscopy and to [gynaecology](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/README.md) for menstrual loss; anaemia of kidney disease is managed with [nephrology](../03-nephrology/README.md); marrow failure overlaps with [haematological malignancies](haematological-malignancies.md) and [oncology](../07-oncology/README.md); sickle cell disease involves [pain medicine](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/pain.md), [paediatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/README.md) and [obstetrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/README.md); and anaemia is a routine finding in [geriatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/README.md), where it is often multifactorial.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Reticulocyte | Newly released red cell still containing RNA; its count measures marrow output |
| MCV | Mean corpuscular volume; average red-cell size, classifying anaemia as micro-, normo- or macrocytic |
| Ferritin | Iron storage protein; low means iron deficiency, but it rises with inflammation |
| Hepcidin | Liver hormone that blocks iron absorption and release by degrading ferroportin |
| Haemolysis | Premature red-cell destruction, intravascular or in the spleen |
| Haptoglobin | Plasma protein that binds free haemoglobin; consumed in haemolysis |
| Direct antiglobulin test | Detects antibody or complement on the patient's red cells |
| Megaloblastic | Large cells with immature nuclei from impaired DNA synthesis (B12, folate) |
| Anaemia of chronic disease | Hypoproliferative anaemia from inflammation-driven iron sequestration |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A normal ferritin excludes iron deficiency."** Ferritin rises with inflammation, infection and liver disease; an inflamed patient can have iron deficiency with a normal ferritin, and transferrin saturation or the response to iron settles it.
- **"Anaemia means give iron."** Iron given to a patient with B12 deficiency, haemolysis or marrow failure does nothing and delays the real diagnosis; iron given to a patient with thalassaemia trait causes overload.
- **"A haemoglobin value alone tells you how sick the patient is."** The rate of fall matters more than the level: a haemoglobin of 70 g/L reached over months is compensated; the same value an hour after a bleed is a surgical emergency.
