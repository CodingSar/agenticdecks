## Essence

Transfusion is a transplant of liquid tissue from one person to another, and everything about it follows from that. The donor's cells carry antigens the recipient may recognise as foreign; the donor's plasma carries antibodies that may recognise the recipient; and the product can carry anything the donor's blood carried, from viruses to iron. Transfusion medicine is the discipline of making this transplant safe: matching antigens to prevent immune destruction, screening and processing to prevent transmission, separating whole blood into components so that each patient gets only what is missing, and, increasingly, not transfusing at all when the body can compensate. Its central lesson is that blood is a drug with a narrow therapeutic window, prescribed by indication rather than by number.

## Core tenets

1. **Compatibility is about antibodies already present, not antigens alone.** The ABO system matters most because people make antibodies against the A or B antigens they lack without ever being exposed, since similar sugars occur on gut bacteria ([blood groups](../../06-physiology/08-blood/blood-groups-and-transfusion-compatibility.md)). Transfusing A cells into an O recipient triggers complement-mediated intravascular haemolysis within minutes, with shock, kidney injury and disseminated intravascular coagulation ([hypersensitivity type II](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/04-immunity-in-disease/hypersensitivity-and-allergy.md)). Rh (D) and the minor antigens cause trouble only after sensitisation by a previous transfusion or pregnancy, so the antibody screen looks for what this patient has already made.

2. **Whole blood is separated because each component has a different job, shelf life and risk.** Red cells carry oxygen and keep for weeks refrigerated; platelets stop bleeding and last days at room temperature; plasma supplies clotting factors and is frozen; cryoprecipitate concentrates fibrinogen; and fractionation yields albumin, immunoglobulin and factor concentrates ([blood products](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/04-non-drug-therapeutics/fluids-nutrition-and-blood-products.md)). Giving a bleeding patient only red cells dilutes their clotting factors and platelets, so massive haemorrhage is treated with components in ratio, the lesson of military surgery ([trauma and haemorrhage control](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/trauma-and-haemorrhage-control.md)).

3. **The commonest serious transfusion harms are now errors and immune reactions, not infection.** Donor screening, nucleic acid testing and pathogen reduction have driven transmission of hepatitis B and C and the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) to well under one per million units in high-income countries (2020s). Bacterial contamination of platelets, stored warm, remains the largest infectious risk. Giving the wrong unit to the wrong patient, a bedside identification failure, remains the leading cause of fatal ABO incompatibility, which is why transfusion safety is as much a systems problem as a biological one ([patient safety](../../15-medicine-in-society/01-health-systems/patient-safety-and-quality-improvement.md)).

4. **Transfusion-related lung injury and circulatory overload are mechanistically distinct and treated oppositely.** Transfusion-associated circulatory overload is hydrostatic pulmonary oedema from volume given faster than a failing heart or kidney can handle, and diuresis helps ([oedema](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/edema-and-fluid-imbalance.md)). Transfusion-related acute lung injury is inflammatory oedema caused by donor antibodies against recipient leucocyte antigens activating neutrophils in the lung; it needs supportive ventilation, and diuresis worsens it. Using plasma from male or never-pregnant donors, who lack such antibodies, has cut its incidence sharply.

5. **Alloimmunisation is the long-term cost of repeated transfusion.** Each unit exposes the recipient to foreign red-cell, platelet and leucocyte antigens. Patients transfused for life (sickle cell disease, thalassaemia, myelodysplasia) develop antibodies that make future matching harder and cause delayed haemolysis; platelet refractoriness follows antibodies to human leucocyte antigens (HLA). Extended antigen matching and leucocyte depletion of products reduce but do not abolish this ([specificity and recognition](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#specificity-and-recognition)).

6. **Iron overload is arithmetic.** Each red-cell unit contains roughly 200–250 mg of iron and the body has no way to excrete it ([red cells, haemoglobin and iron](../../06-physiology/08-blood/red-cells-haemoglobin-and-iron.md)). After a few dozen units, iron deposits in liver, heart and endocrine glands cause cirrhosis, cardiomyopathy and diabetes. Chelation therapy is the treatment, and avoiding unnecessary transfusion the prevention.

7. **Restrictive transfusion is usually as safe as liberal, because the body compensates for anaemia.** Randomised trials in critical care, cardiac surgery and gastrointestinal bleeding showed no benefit, and sometimes harm, from transfusing to higher haemoglobin thresholds ([randomised controlled trials](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/02-evidence-based-medicine/randomized-controlled-trials.md)). Oxygen delivery has reserve, and each unit carries risk, so transfusion is indicated by symptoms, ongoing loss and physiological need, not by a laboratory number alone. Patient blood management (treating anaemia before surgery, minimising loss, tolerating lower values) now reduces transfusion rather than refining it.

8. **Who else is involved.** Transfusion is a service to every bleeding specialty: [surgery](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/README.md) and [anaesthesia](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/README.md) consume most products; [obstetrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/README.md) depends on it for postpartum haemorrhage and for anti-D prophylaxis against haemolytic disease of the newborn; [neonatology](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/the-newborn-and-neonatology.md) exchanges blood in severe jaundice; [haematological malignancy](haematological-malignancies.md) and [oncology](../07-oncology/README.md) need weeks of platelet support; [infectious diseases](../08-infectious-diseases/README.md) and [public health](../../15-medicine-in-society/03-populations-and-the-future/global-health-and-pandemics.md) govern donor screening as new pathogens emerge.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ABO system | Carbohydrate antigens with naturally occurring antibodies; the main barrier to transfusion |
| Rh D | Protein antigen that provokes antibodies after exposure; central to haemolytic disease of the newborn |
| Crossmatch | Testing recipient plasma against donor cells for incompatibility |
| Antibody screen | Testing recipient plasma for antibodies against common red-cell antigens |
| Component therapy | Transfusing red cells, platelets, plasma or cryoprecipitate separately by indication |
| Alloimmunisation | Antibody formation against foreign antigens on transfused cells |
| Transfusion-associated circulatory overload | Hydrostatic pulmonary oedema from volume |
| Transfusion-related acute lung injury | Inflammatory pulmonary oedema from donor antibodies |
| Leucodepletion | Removing white cells from products to reduce reactions, alloimmunisation and some transmission |
| Patient blood management | Strategies to optimise a patient's own blood and minimise transfusion |

## Common misconceptions

- **"O negative is universally safe."** O negative red cells lack A, B and D antigens, but recipients may carry antibodies to other antigens, and O plasma contains anti-A and anti-B; universal donor applies to cells in an emergency, not to plasma or to the sensitised patient.
- **"The main risk of transfusion is catching a virus."** In well-regulated systems the infectious risk is now far smaller than the risks of error, circulatory overload and immune reactions.
- **"A low haemoglobin is an indication to transfuse."** It is an indication to find out why and whether the patient is compensating; the number alone does not decide.
