# Skin lesion morphology and diagnosis

## Essence

The skin is the only organ examined directly, in its entirety, with the naked eye, and dermatology is built on a disciplined way of describing what is seen. A lesion's morphology (flat or raised, solid or fluid-filled, its size, colour, surface and edge), its arrangement and its distribution over the body each encode where in the skin the pathology sits and what process produced it. A blister means the layers have separated, and the level of the split names the disease; a scaly plaque means the epidermis is proliferating too fast; a dermatomal band means a nerve. Thousands of named diseases reduce to a few dozen reaction patterns, and the method is to describe first, then infer the level and the process, then confirm by biopsy when the stakes demand it. Because the skin also mirrors systemic disease and drug reactions, this method is a window onto the rest of medicine.

## Core tenets

1. **Morphology encodes depth, and depth encodes mechanism.** The skin is a layered organ: a keratinised epidermis a tenth of a millimetre thick, a dermis of collagen with vessels, nerves and appendages, and subcutaneous fat ([the skin as barrier and sensor](../../06-physiology/10-musculoskeletal-system-and-skin/the-skin-as-barrier-and-sensor.md), [epithelium](../../05-tissues-and-anatomy/01-tissues/epithelium.md)). A macule (flat colour change) is pigment or vessel change without altered thickness; a papule or plaque (raised, solid) is thickening of epidermis, infiltration of dermis, or both; a vesicle or bulla (fluid-filled) is a split between or within layers; a nodule is deep dermal or subcutaneous. Scale means disordered keratinisation in the epidermis; crust is dried exudate; lichenification is epidermal thickening from rubbing. The primary lesion, before scratching and secondary change, is the thing to find.

2. **The level of a blister is diagnostic, and immunology explains it.** Pemphigus vulgaris autoantibodies target desmogleins, the desmosomal adhesion molecules between keratinocytes, so the split is within the epidermis: the blister roof is a few cells thick, breaks at once, and the patient presents with painful erosions and mouth ulcers ([cell adhesion](../../03-cell-biology/03-cells-in-community/cell-adhesion-and-the-extracellular-matrix.md), [autoimmunity](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/04-immunity-in-disease/autoimmunity.md)). Bullous pemphigoid antibodies target hemidesmosomal proteins anchoring epidermis to dermis, so the blister is tense, roofed by the whole epidermis, and survives. Burns, friction and toxin-mediated infections split at characteristic levels too. Direct immunofluorescence on biopsy shows where the antibody sits and confirms the diagnosis.

3. **Distribution is the second axis, and it tells you the cause.** Photo-exposed sites (face, V of neck, backs of hands) implicate ultraviolet light, in lupus, porphyria, drug photosensitivity and skin cancer. Flexures suggest atopic eczema; extensor surfaces, psoriasis; a single dermatome, herpes zoster ([segmental innervation and dermatomes](../../05-tissues-and-anatomy/02-body-organization/segmental-innervation-dermatomes-and-referred-pain.md)); a contact pattern (under a watch, where a plant brushed), allergic or irritant contact dermatitis; symmetry suggests an internal cause and asymmetry an external one. Arrangement adds more: linear lesions follow scratching or embryological lines; grouped vesicles are herpetic; annular lesions with an active edge suggest fungus or a migrating process.

4. **Colour is physics and chemistry read through the skin.** Red is dilated or inflamed dermal vessels and blanches on pressure; purple that does not blanch is blood outside vessels (purpura), which points to platelet, coagulation or vessel-wall disease ([bleeding and clotting disorders](../06-haematology/bleeding-and-clotting-disorders.md), [vasculitis](../09-rheumatology-and-clinical-immunology/connective-tissue-disease-and-vasculitis.md)). Brown is melanin in the epidermis; blue-grey is melanin deep in the dermis, scattered by the Tyndall effect ([waves, sound, light and radiation](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/waves-sound-light-and-radiation.md)); yellow is lipid or bilirubin; white is absent melanin (vitiligo) or absent blood. Every colour sign must be read against the patient's baseline skin tone, because erythema and jaundice are harder to see and purpura easier to miss in darker skin, a persistent source of diagnostic inequity.

5. **The skin reports internal disease and drug reactions, often first.** Pruritus without rash suggests cholestasis, kidney failure, iron deficiency, lymphoma or thyroid disease. Specific eruptions announce systemic disease: the malar rash of lupus, the heliotrope rash and knuckle papules of dermatomyositis, acanthosis nigricans in insulin resistance, erythema nodosum in sarcoidosis and inflammatory bowel disease, necrobiosis lipoidica in diabetes. Drug eruptions range from the benign maculopapular rash a week or two after a new drug to the T-cell-mediated emergencies of Stevens–Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis, in which the epidermis dies in sheets, and drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms ([adverse drug reactions](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/02-pharmacodynamics/adverse-drug-reactions-and-pharmacovigilance.md), [hypersensitivity](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/04-immunity-in-disease/hypersensitivity-and-allergy.md)). The timing from drug start to rash is the key history.

6. **Biopsy is cheap and definitive, so the threshold for it is low when the differential includes cancer or a blistering disease.** A punch or shave biopsy takes minutes and shows the level and type of inflammation, the presence of atypical cells and, with immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry, the antibody or lineage involved ([histopathology](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/02-laboratory-medicine/histopathology-cytology-and-molecular-diagnostics.md)). Dermoscopy, a hand-held magnifier with polarised light, improves the sensitivity and specificity of melanoma diagnosis over the naked eye and reduces unnecessary excisions ([diagnostic accuracy studies](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/02-evidence-based-medicine/diagnostic-accuracy-studies.md)). Skin scrapings and swabs confirm fungi, scabies and bacteria. Clinical photography, compared over time, is the test for change in a pigmented lesion.

7. **Who else is involved.** Most skin disease is seen in [primary care](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/README.md), where rashes are among the commonest presentations and where teledermatology with photographs now triages referrals. The dermatologist is the consultant who reads the skin for [rheumatology](../09-rheumatology-and-clinical-immunology/README.md) (lupus, dermatomyositis, vasculitis), [infectious diseases](../08-infectious-diseases/README.md) (the rashes of systemic infection), [haematology](../06-haematology/README.md) (purpura, cutaneous lymphoma), [oncology](../07-oncology/README.md) (drug rashes, paraneoplastic signs), [endocrinology](../05-endocrinology/README.md) and [gastroenterology](../04-gastroenterology-and-hepatology/README.md) (the skin markers of their diseases). Severe drug eruptions are managed with [critical care](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/03-critical-care/README.md) and burns units; [paediatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/README.md) shares the childhood exanthems and birthmarks; histopathologists are the other half of every difficult diagnosis.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Macule and patch | Flat change in colour, small and large respectively |
| Papule, plaque and nodule | Raised solid lesions: small, broad and flat-topped, or deep |
| Vesicle and bulla | Fluid-filled blisters, small and large |
| Scale, crust and lichenification | Excess keratin, dried exudate and thickened skin markings |
| Purpura | Non-blanching discolouration from extravasated blood |
| Primary versus secondary lesion | The original lesion versus changes from scratching, infection or healing |
| Distribution | Where on the body lesions occur; encodes cause (light, contact, nerve, flexure) |
| Dermoscopy | Magnified polarised-light examination improving pigmented-lesion diagnosis |
| Direct immunofluorescence | Biopsy staining showing where antibody is bound in the skin |
| Koebner phenomenon | New lesions at sites of trauma, seen in psoriasis and lichen planus |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A rash is a diagnosis."** It is a description that has not yet been made; the same red scaly plaque may be psoriasis, eczema, fungus or a cutaneous lymphoma, and the method exists to tell them apart.
- **"Blanching is a trivial sign."** It is the fastest way to tell inflamed vessels from leaked blood, and non-blanching purpura in a feverish patient is meningococcal sepsis until proven otherwise.
- **"Skin signs look the same in everyone."** Erythema, cyanosis and jaundice are muted in darker skin and purpura is easier to miss; textbooks built on pale skin have taught a biased pattern recognition.
