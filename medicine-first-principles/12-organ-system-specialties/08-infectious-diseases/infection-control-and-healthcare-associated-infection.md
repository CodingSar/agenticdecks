## Essence

A hospital concentrates the sickest, most immunosuppressed people in one building, pierces their barriers with cannulas, catheters and tubes, bathes them in antibiotics that clear their protective flora, and moves staff between them hundreds of times a day. It is the ideal environment for transmitting infection, and roughly one in fifteen to twenty inpatients in high-income countries acquires one (2010s–2020s). Infection control is the application of the chain of infection to this setting: every healthcare-associated infection has a reservoir, a route and a susceptible host, and each is a point of attack. The central discovery, made by Semmelweis in 1847 and relearned every generation, is that the hands of staff are the main route and that the solution is behavioural and organisational, not pharmacological.

## Core tenets

1. **Healthcare-associated infection is a breach of barriers, and the devices are the breach.** Skin and mucosa are the first line of immunity ([barriers and pattern recognition](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/02-innate-immunity/barriers-and-pattern-recognition.md)). A urinary catheter gives bacteria a highway past the urethra; a central venous line delivers skin flora into the bloodstream; an endotracheal tube bypasses the cough and lets oropharyngeal secretions pool over the larynx into the lungs; a surgical incision opens tissue to the air. The four big categories, catheter-associated urinary infection, line-associated bloodstream infection, ventilator-associated pneumonia and surgical-site infection, are each named for their device, and the most effective prevention is to remove the device as soon as it is not needed.

2. **Biofilm turns a device into a sanctuary.** Bacteria on a foreign surface switch to a sessile state, secrete a polysaccharide matrix and divide slowly ([devices, implants and biomaterials](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/04-non-drug-therapeutics/devices-implants-and-biomaterials.md)). Antibiotics penetrate biofilm poorly and act weakly on non-dividing cells, so the minimum concentration to kill rises a hundred-fold or more. An infected prosthetic joint, heart valve or line therefore usually needs the device removed; antibiotics alone suppress and relapse. This is why source control is a principle and not a preference ([fever and sepsis](fever-and-sepsis.md)).

3. **Hands are the vector, and hand hygiene is the single most effective intervention.** Staff touch a colonised patient, then a surface, then the next patient; organisms such as methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* and vancomycin-resistant enterococci survive on skin and dry surfaces for hours to days ([transmission and the chain of infection](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/01-microbes-and-infection/transmission-and-the-chain-of-infection.md)). Alcohol hand rub before and after every contact interrupts the route. Compliance in unobserved practice is typically well below 100%, and raising it is a problem of design (rub at every bedside), culture and feedback rather than of knowledge ([behaviour change](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/04-non-drug-therapeutics/behaviour-change-and-the-placebo-effect.md)).

4. **Precautions are matched to the route of transmission.** Standard precautions assume every patient may carry something. Contact precautions (gloves, gown, single room) are added for organisms spread by touch; droplet precautions (mask within a metre or two) for pathogens in large respiratory droplets that fall quickly; airborne precautions (negative-pressure room, filtering respirator) for tuberculosis, measles and varicella, whose small particles stay suspended and travel on air currents ([waves, sound, light and radiation](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/waves-sound-light-and-radiation.md) covers the aerosol physics only in passing; the practical point is particle size and settling time). The COVID-19 pandemic showed that the droplet–airborne boundary is a continuum, and that ventilation is an infection-control measure.

5. **Antibiotic pressure creates the organisms that infection control then fights.** The resistant organisms that define hospital infection, methicillin-resistant staphylococci, extended-spectrum beta-lactamase producers, carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales and *Clostridioides difficile*, exist because hospitals are where antibiotic use is most intense ([antimicrobial stewardship](antimicrobial-stewardship.md)). Stewardship reduces the reservoir; infection control reduces transmission; neither works alone. Screening admissions for carriage and isolating or decolonising carriers lowers the number of infected patients only if the hospital's own selection pressure is also controlled.

6. **Surveillance is what makes an outbreak visible before it is obvious.** Two cases of the same unusual organism on one ward are a signal; without routine counting they are noise. Infection-control teams track rates of device-associated infection per thousand device-days, feed them back to units, and investigate clusters using the epidemiological method of case definition, time-place-person description and typing of isolates by whole-genome sequencing, which can now show transmission chains between individual patients ([surveillance and outbreak investigation](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/01-epidemiology/surveillance-and-outbreak-investigation.md), [molecular techniques](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/03-genetic-information-flow/molecular-techniques.md)).

7. **Bundles work because reliability, not novelty, prevents infection.** Central-line infection rates fell by two thirds or more in large programmes that simply ensured five known steps (hand hygiene, full barrier precautions, skin antisepsis, avoiding the femoral site, daily review of need) were done every time, with a checklist and the authority to stop a procedure. The lesson belongs to [patient safety and quality improvement](../../15-medicine-in-society/01-health-systems/patient-safety-and-quality-improvement.md): most hospital infection is not a mystery but a failure to apply what is known.

8. **Who else is involved.** Infection control touches everyone who touches a patient, but its sharpest interfaces are with [critical care](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/03-critical-care/README.md), where devices and resistant organisms concentrate; [surgery](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/README.md) and [anaesthesia](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/README.md), whose surgical-site infection rates are the measure of their asepsis, prophylaxis and glucose control; [haematology](../06-haematology/README.md) and [oncology](../07-oncology/README.md), whose neutropenic patients need protective isolation; [neonatology](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/README.md), where outbreaks in units of tiny patients are devastating; [geriatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/README.md), because care homes are the community reservoir; and [public health](../../15-medicine-in-society/03-populations-and-the-future/global-health-and-pandemics.md), which during a pandemic turns the whole of society into a ward.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Healthcare-associated infection | Infection acquired during care that was not present or incubating at admission |
| Chain of infection | Reservoir, portal of exit, route, portal of entry, susceptible host |
| Biofilm | Matrix-enclosed bacterial community on a surface, highly tolerant of antibiotics |
| Device-days | Denominator for device-associated infection rates |
| Standard, contact, droplet and airborne precautions | Tiers of barrier measures matched to the route of spread |
| Colonisation | Carriage of an organism without disease; the reservoir for transmission |
| Decolonisation | Topical treatment to eliminate carriage, for example nasal mupirocin for *S. aureus* |
| Care bundle | A small set of evidence-based steps performed reliably together |
| Surgical-site infection | Infection of the wound or operated space within weeks of surgery |
| Outbreak | More cases than expected of one infection in a place and time |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Hospital infections are caused by dirty hospitals."** Visible dirt matters less than invisible hands and devices; a spotless ward with poor hand hygiene transmits more than a shabby one with good compliance.
- **"Resistant organisms are more virulent."** They are usually equally virulent but harder to treat; their danger is the delay before an effective drug is found.
- **"Prophylactic antibiotics prevent device infections."** Beyond a single peri-operative dose, prolonged prophylaxis selects resistant flora without reducing infection; removal of the device does.
