## Essence

Antimicrobials are the only drugs whose use by one patient reduces their value for everyone else, because every course selects for resistant organisms that spread. Stewardship is the discipline of getting the maximum benefit from these drugs while slowing the loss of their effectiveness: the right drug for the right diagnosis at the right dose and duration, and no drug when none is needed. It is applied evolutionary biology and applied Bayesian reasoning. The core tension is that a doctor facing an uncertain patient is rewarded for treating and never sees the resistance created, so stewardship must be built into systems rather than left to good intentions.

## Core tenets

1. **Resistance is natural selection and the antibiotic is the selective pressure.** Bacteria replicate in minutes and swap genes across species on plasmids, so any population of billions contains resistant variants before exposure ([host-pathogen coevolution and resistance](../../04-genetics-evolution-and-development/02-evolution/host-pathogen-coevolution-and-resistance.md), [bacteria](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/01-microbes-and-infection/bacteria.md)). Each course kills the susceptible majority and leaves the resistant minority room to grow, in the patient's microbiome as much as in the pathogen ([the human microbiome](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/01-microbes-and-infection/the-human-microbiome.md)); the resistant commensal then colonises the next patient. Resistance therefore tracks total consumption at the level of hospital, country and farm.

2. **Mechanisms of resistance follow from mechanisms of action.** Beta-lactamases hydrolyse the beta-lactam ring; altered penicillin-binding proteins give methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*; efflux pumps expel tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones; ribosomal methylation blocks macrolides; carbapenemases now threaten the drugs of last resort ([antimicrobials](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/antimicrobials.md)). Knowing the mechanism predicts cross-resistance within a class.

3. **Most antibiotic prescriptions treat conditions that antibiotics cannot help.** Most respiratory infections in the community are viral, and trials show antibiotics shorten sore throat, otitis and bronchitis by a day or less while causing diarrhoea, rash and resistance ([harm and benefit](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#harm-and-benefit)). The decision is Bayesian: pre-test probability of a bacterial cause, the value of a test, and a treatment threshold ([using tests and thresholds](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/01-the-clinical-encounter/using-tests-pretest-probability-and-thresholds.md)). Delayed prescriptions and explaining the natural history reduce use without harm.

4. **In hospital, the strategy is to start broad when the patient is sick and narrow fast.** Sepsis demands empirical cover for the likely organisms ([fever and sepsis](fever-and-sepsis.md)). The stewardship step comes at 48–72 hours: review cultures, course and source, then stop, narrow, switch to oral or set a stop date. Most excess hospital use is failure to perform this review, not the initial decision. Microbiology supports it with selective reporting and rapid molecular identification ([microbiological diagnosis](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/02-laboratory-medicine/microbiological-and-serological-diagnosis.md)).

5. **Pharmacokinetics determine whether a dose kills or merely selects.** Beta-lactams kill in proportion to the time the concentration stays above the minimum inhibitory concentration, so frequent or continuous dosing matters; aminoglycosides and fluoroquinolones kill in proportion to peak concentration, so a large infrequent dose is better and also spares the kidney ([elimination, half-life and dosing](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/01-pharmacokinetics/elimination-half-life-and-dosing.md), [dose-response](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#dose-response)). Sub-therapeutic exposure, from under-dosing, poor penetration or biofilm, is the regime that most efficiently breeds resistance.

6. **Shorter courses are usually as effective, and the "finish the course" rule has little evidence.** Randomised trials in pneumonia, urinary infection, cellulitis and intra-abdominal infection have repeatedly shown short courses non-inferior to long ones ([randomised controlled trials](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/02-evidence-based-medicine/randomized-controlled-trials.md)). Duration should be set by the infection's biology (endocarditis and bone infection need weeks because bacteria in biofilm divide slowly) and by response, not by a round number.

7. **Collateral damage falls on the microbiome, and *Clostridioides difficile* is its signature.** Broad-spectrum agents empty the gut of competitors, letting *C. difficile* spores germinate and produce toxin; the resulting colitis is a hospital epidemic disease driven by prescribing ([the gut barrier and microbiome](../../06-physiology/06-gastrointestinal-system/the-gut-barrier-and-microbiome.md)). Restricting the most disruptive classes reduces its incidence more than any infection-control measure.

8. **Who else is involved.** Stewardship is a system property. [Primary care](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/README.md) writes most prescriptions; [surgery](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/README.md) and [anaesthesia](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/README.md) use prophylaxis that must be single-dose and timed; [critical care](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/03-critical-care/README.md) is where the broadest agents and most resistant organisms meet; [haematology](../06-haematology/README.md) and [oncology](../07-oncology/README.md) need prolonged prophylaxis; [paediatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/README.md) prescribes for the age group with most viral infections. Pharmacists, microbiologists and [quality programmes](../../15-medicine-in-society/01-health-systems/patient-safety-and-quality-improvement.md) run the audit and feedback; agriculture and [global health](../../15-medicine-in-society/03-populations-and-the-future/global-health-and-pandemics.md) set the background rate of resistance no hospital can escape.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Antimicrobial resistance | Ability of a microbe to survive a drug that once inhibited it; acquired by mutation or gene transfer |
| Minimum inhibitory concentration | Lowest drug concentration that prevents visible growth; the laboratory measure of susceptibility |
| Empirical versus targeted therapy | Treatment by probability before results, versus by identified organism and sensitivity |
| De-escalation | Narrowing or stopping antibiotics once cultures and course are known |
| Time- versus concentration-dependent killing | Whether efficacy tracks duration above MIC or peak concentration |
| Collateral damage | Resistance and *C. difficile* infection induced in non-target organisms |
| Prophylaxis | Antibiotic given to prevent rather than treat infection, as before surgery |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Resistance develops in the patient who takes the antibiotic."** It mostly develops in their commensal flora and spreads to others; the prescriber rarely sees the harm.
- **"Stopping early breeds resistance."** Longer exposure selects more; the evidence favours stopping when the infection is treated.
- **"New antibiotics will solve the problem."** The pipeline is thin because the economics are poor, and every new drug meets resistance within years of use; conservation is the only renewable strategy.
