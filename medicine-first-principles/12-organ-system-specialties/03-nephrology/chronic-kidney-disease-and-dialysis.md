## Essence

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is the loss of nephrons over months to years, with no capacity to make new ones. It affects roughly one adult in ten worldwide (2017 estimate), is caused mostly by diabetes and hypertension, and is silent until most of the organ is gone, because the surviving nephrons filter harder to compensate. That compensation is also the mechanism of progression: hyperfiltration damages the survivors, so CKD advances even after the original cause is removed. As filtration falls, the kidney's other jobs fail in sequence (blood pressure, potassium and acid, erythropoietin, vitamin D and phosphate), and the patient dies of cardiovascular disease long before, or instead of, reaching the point where dialysis or a transplant must take over the excretory function.

## Core tenets

1. **Nephron loss is hidden by hyperfiltration, and hyperfiltration is what makes it progress.** When nephrons are lost, angiotensin II constricts the efferent arterioles of the survivors and each filters more, so total filtration and creatinine barely change until about half are gone ([filtration](../../06-physiology/05-renal-system/glomerular-filtration-and-clearance.md)). The raised glomerular pressure injures the survivors' capillaries and podocytes, protein leaks into the filtrate, and the glomeruli scar (glomerulosclerosis) while the tubules and interstitium fibrose around them ([fibrosis](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/tissue-repair-regeneration-and-fibrosis.md)). Brenner's hypothesis, that the compensation drives the decline, explains why drugs that lower glomerular pressure slow CKD of every cause.

2. **Two numbers stage the disease and predict its course: the filtration rate and the albumin leak.** The estimated glomerular filtration rate, calculated from creatinine with age and sex, grades severity from mildly reduced (above 60 mL/min) to kidney failure (below 15). Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio grades the glomerular injury. Both predict progression and cardiovascular death independently, so a patient with moderate filtration and heavy albuminuria is at far higher risk than one with the same filtration and none ([risk prediction](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/03-screening-and-prevention/risk-prediction-and-stratification.md)). Diabetes and hypertension cause two-thirds of kidney failure in high-income countries; glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease and obstruction most of the rest ([diabetes](../05-endocrinology/diabetes-mellitus.md), [hypertension](../01-cardiology/hypertension-and-vascular-disease.md)).

3. **Each lost function produces a predictable complication.** Sodium retention raises blood pressure, which accelerates the loss. Potassium and acid accumulate below about 20–30 mL/min. Peritubular cells stop making erythropoietin, so a normocytic anaemia develops ([red cells](../../06-physiology/08-blood/red-cells-haemoglobin-and-iron.md)). The kidney cannot excrete phosphate or hydroxylate vitamin D, so calcium falls, parathyroid hormone rises (secondary hyperparathyroidism), bone is resorbed and vessels calcify ([calcium metabolism](../../06-physiology/07-endocrine-system/calcium-and-bone-mineral-metabolism.md)). Retained uraemic toxins cause nausea, itch, neuropathy and pericarditis. Each is detectable on routine blood tests before the patient feels it, which is why CKD is monitored by chemistry rather than symptoms.

4. **Cardiovascular disease, not uraemia, is the usual cause of death.** Hypertension, volume overload, anaemia, vascular calcification, inflammation and dyslipidaemia combine so that a patient with moderate CKD is many times more likely to die of heart disease than to reach dialysis ([atherosclerosis](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/atherosclerosis.md)). CKD is therefore treated as a cardiovascular risk state: blood pressure control, statins and the drugs below reduce death more than they delay dialysis.

5. **Treatment lowers glomerular pressure and removes the cause.** Angiotensin-converting-enzyme inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers dilate the efferent arteriole, reduce hyperfiltration and proteinuria, and slow progression; the small initial fall in filtration they cause is the mechanism working, not harm. Sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors restore tubuloglomerular feedback by delivering more sodium to the macula densa, which constricts the afferent arteriole, and they slow CKD in people with and without diabetes in [randomised trials](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/02-evidence-based-medicine/randomized-controlled-trials.md). Glycaemic and blood pressure control treat the cause; dietary protein and salt restriction reduce the load; drugs that need the kidney are re-dosed ([elimination](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/01-pharmacokinetics/elimination-half-life-and-dosing.md)). Erythropoietin, iron, phosphate binders and active vitamin D replace the lost functions.

6. **Dialysis replaces filtration by diffusion across a membrane; it is partial, intermittent and not a cure.** Blood on one side of a semipermeable membrane and a dialysate of chosen composition on the other let small solutes (urea, potassium, acid) diffuse down their gradients while a pressure gradient removes water ([diffusion and osmosis](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/diffusion-osmosis-and-transport.md)). Haemodialysis does this through an extracorporeal circuit, typically four hours three times a week, so between sessions solutes and fluid accumulate and the patient lives in a sawtooth; peritoneal dialysis uses the patient's own peritoneum continuously. Neither replaces the endocrine functions or the continuous fine control of a kidney, and neither delivers more than about 10–15% of normal clearance; five-year survival on dialysis is of the order of 40–50% (high-income countries, 2010s–2020s), worse than many cancers, and driven by cardiovascular disease.

7. **A transplant restores the organ, at the price of immunosuppression.** A kidney from a living or deceased donor gives near-normal function and the best survival, because it restores the endocrine and continuous functions dialysis cannot. The recipient's immune system recognises the donor's major histocompatibility antigens and must be suppressed for life (calcineurin inhibitors such as tacrolimus, antiproliferatives, corticosteroids) ([transplant immunology](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/04-immunity-in-disease/transplant-and-tumour-immunology.md)), which trades rejection for infection, cancer and the nephrotoxicity of the drugs themselves. Donor organs are scarce, so allocation is a problem in [ethics and health systems](../../15-medicine-in-society/01-health-systems/health-economics-and-priority-setting.md) as much as in medicine.

8. **Who else is involved.** [Cardiology](../01-cardiology/README.md) shares the patient who dies of heart disease and the drugs that protect both organs. [Endocrinology](../05-endocrinology/README.md) manages the diabetes that caused it and the bone disease that follows. [Transplant and vascular surgery](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/surgical-subspecialties.md) provide the graft and the fistula. [Haematology](../06-haematology/anaemias.md) is consulted for anaemia that does not respond to erythropoietin. [Primary care](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/README.md) manages the majority of mild CKD and the [self-management](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/chronic-disease-and-self-management.md) that dialysis demands; [geriatrics and palliative care](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/death-dying-and-end-of-life-decisions.md) share the decision, in frail older people, that conservative management without dialysis gives a similar length of life with a better quality.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Estimated glomerular filtration rate | Filtration estimated from creatinine, age and sex; the main staging variable |
| Albuminuria | Albumin in urine; a marker of glomerular injury and an independent predictor of progression and death |
| Hyperfiltration | Raised single-nephron filtration in surviving nephrons; compensation that drives progression |
| Glomerulosclerosis | Scarring of glomeruli; the final common lesion of nephron loss |
| Secondary hyperparathyroidism | Parathyroid overactivity driven by low calcium, high phosphate and low active vitamin D in CKD |
| Uraemia | The syndrome of retained nitrogenous waste in kidney failure |
| Renal replacement therapy | Dialysis (haemodialysis or peritoneal) or transplantation |
| Arteriovenous fistula | Surgically joined artery and vein providing high-flow access for haemodialysis |
| Conservative kidney management | Treatment of kidney failure without dialysis, chosen when dialysis would not add life or quality |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Kidney disease ends in dialysis."** Most patients with CKD die of cardiovascular disease with their kidneys still working; fewer than one in fifty reach dialysis.
- **"A small rise in creatinine after starting an ACE inhibitor means it is harming the kidney."** A modest fall in filtration is the drug lowering glomerular pressure, which is how it protects the kidney over years.
- **"Dialysis replaces the kidney."** It replaces a fraction of one function intermittently; patients on dialysis remain anaemic, hypertensive, bone-diseased and at high cardiovascular risk.
- **"Older patients should always be offered dialysis."** In the frail over-80s, conservative management often gives similar survival with fewer hospital days; the choice is the patient's.
