## Essence

Electrolyte and acid–base disorders are failures of regulation, and the regulated variables are few: the sodium concentration (which is really the body's water), the potassium concentration (which is really the ratio of potassium inside cells to outside), the calcium concentration, and the hydrogen ion concentration. Each has a controller (antidiuretic hormone, aldosterone and insulin, parathyroid hormone, the lung and kidney together) and an effector, and each disorder is a controller firing wrongly or an effector that cannot obey. Most are caused outside the kidney, by vomiting, diarrhoea, drugs, hormones, tumours and the brain, and the kidney is the organ that fails to correct them. The approach is the same for all: decide what the plasma value means, decide what the controller should be doing, and measure the urine to see whether it is.

## Core tenets

1. **A sodium disorder is a water disorder, and the danger is in the brain.** Plasma sodium is set by the ratio of body sodium to body water, and water is controlled by thirst and antidiuretic hormone acting on the collecting duct ([volume regulation](../../06-physiology/05-renal-system/sodium-water-and-volume-regulation.md)). Hyponatraemia almost always means the kidney is retaining water because antidiuretic hormone is being released when it should not be: by pain, nausea, drugs, lung and brain disease, or a tumour (the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis), or appropriately, by the low effective volume of heart failure and cirrhosis. Water enters brain cells by osmosis; the brain adapts over days by extruding solute, so a sodium that fell over weeks is tolerated and one that fell over hours causes seizures ([fluid compartments](../../06-physiology/01-integrative-principles/body-fluid-compartments-and-electrolytes.md)). The same adaptation makes rapid correction of chronic hyponatraemia dangerous: the shrinking brain demyelinates. Hypernatraemia is water deficit in someone who cannot drink; the treatment is water, slowly.

2. **The urine says whether the kidney is the problem.** In hyponatraemia, a dilute urine means the kidney is excreting water correctly and the patient drank more than it could pass; a concentrated urine means antidiuretic hormone is active. Urine sodium then separates low effective volume (sodium retained, urine sodium low) from inappropriate antidiuresis (volume normal, urine sodium freely excreted). The same logic applies to every electrolyte: the plasma value says a disorder exists, the urine value says whether the kidney is causing it or failing to fix it.

3. **Potassium is almost all intracellular, so small shifts across membranes change the plasma value as much as large losses do.** Of about 3,500 mmol of body potassium, only ~60 mmol are in the plasma. Insulin, beta-2 adrenergic stimulation and alkalosis drive potassium into cells; acidosis, cell breakdown (haemolysis, tumour lysis, crushed muscle) and insulin deficiency drive it out ([renal potassium handling](../../06-physiology/05-renal-system/potassium-calcium-phosphate-and-renal-acid-base.md)). The kidney excretes the daily load through aldosterone-driven secretion in the collecting duct, which requires sodium delivery and flow. Hyperkalaemia therefore comes from kidney failure, aldosterone deficiency or blockade (angiotensin blockers, potassium-sparing diuretics), and shift; hypokalaemia from vomiting and diarrhoea, diuretics, aldosterone excess and shift.

4. **The potassium concentration sets the resting membrane potential, so its disorders are arrhythmias.** The ratio of intracellular to extracellular potassium fixes the resting potential by the Nernst relation ([membrane potential](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/membrane-potential-and-the-action-potential.md)). Hyperkalaemia depolarises cardiac cells, inactivates sodium channels and slows conduction: peaked T waves, a widening QRS, then a sine wave and asystole. Hypokalaemia hyperpolarises and prolongs repolarisation, causing ectopy and torsades, and weakens muscle. The emergency treatment of hyperkalaemia follows the mechanism in order: calcium to stabilise the membrane, insulin with glucose and a beta-agonist to shift potassium into cells, then removal by the kidney, binders or dialysis ([arrhythmias](../01-cardiology/arrhythmias.md)).

5. **Acid–base disorders are classified by which system failed and whether the other compensated.** The lung controls carbon dioxide and the kidney controls bicarbonate; pH follows their ratio ([acid–base physiology](../../06-physiology/01-integrative-principles/acid-base-physiology.md), [buffers](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/02-chemistry-of-life/acids-bases-and-buffers.md)). A primary change in one is met by a compensating change in the other, predictable in size and never complete, so the arterial pH, carbon dioxide and bicarbonate together identify the primary disorder and whether compensation is appropriate; a compensation of the wrong size means a second disorder is present. Respiratory disorders are the lung's ([respiratory failure](../02-pulmonology/pulmonary-vascular-disease-and-respiratory-failure.md)); metabolic disorders are the kidney's, the gut's, or the metabolism's.

6. **The anion gap separates acid added from bicarbonate lost.** Plasma is electrically neutral, so sodium minus chloride minus bicarbonate (normally ~8–12 mmol/L) measures the unmeasured anions. When an acid is added, its anion fills the gap: lactate in shock, ketoacids in [diabetic ketoacidosis](../05-endocrinology/diabetes-mellitus.md), sulphate and phosphate in kidney failure, and the metabolites of methanol, ethylene glycol and salicylate in [poisoning](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/poisoning-and-overdose.md). When bicarbonate is lost (diarrhoea) or cannot be regenerated (renal tubular acidosis), chloride takes its place and the gap is normal. Metabolic alkalosis comes from losing acid (vomiting) or retaining bicarbonate under aldosterone and chloride depletion, and persists only while the kidney is prevented from excreting the excess, usually by volume depletion. Treating an acid–base disorder means treating its cause; bicarbonate itself is rarely the answer.

7. **Calcium is held by parathyroid hormone and vitamin D, and its disorders present as nerves and bones.** Ionised calcium sets the threshold of excitable membranes: low calcium causes tetany, paraesthesia and seizures; high calcium causes weakness, confusion, polyuria (it blocks the collecting duct's response to antidiuretic hormone), stones and a short QT interval. Hypercalcaemia is usually parathyroid adenoma in outpatients and malignancy in inpatients; hypocalcaemia follows parathyroid loss, vitamin D deficiency, kidney failure and acute pancreatitis ([bone and calcium disorders](../05-endocrinology/bone-and-calcium-disorders.md)). Magnesium and phosphate travel with them: magnesium deficiency causes refractory hypokalaemia and hypocalcaemia, and the re-feeding of a starved patient drops phosphate dangerously as cells take it up.

8. **Who else is involved.** Every specialty causes these disorders and nephrology corrects them. [Endocrinology](../05-endocrinology/README.md) owns the hormonal causes; [gastroenterology](../04-gastroenterology-and-hepatology/README.md) supplies vomiting, diarrhoea and cirrhosis; [oncology](../07-oncology/README.md) supplies hypercalcaemia, tumour lysis and inappropriate antidiuresis; [neurology](../10-neurology/README.md) and [psychiatry](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/03-psychiatry/README.md) share the seizures and the drugs that disturb sodium. [Critical care](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/03-critical-care/README.md) and [emergency medicine](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/README.md) manage the acute ones hour by hour, and [geriatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/README.md) sees hyponatraemia as the commonest electrolyte disorder of the old, usually from a drug.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Effective circulating volume | The part of extracellular volume that perfuses the baroreceptors; what the kidney actually senses |
| Antidiuretic hormone | Hypothalamic hormone that makes the collecting duct reabsorb water; the controller of plasma sodium |
| Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis | Water retention from antidiuretic hormone released without an osmotic or volume stimulus |
| Osmotic demyelination | Brain injury from correcting chronic hyponatraemia too fast |
| Transcellular shift | Movement of potassium between cells and plasma under insulin, catecholamines and pH |
| Anion gap | Sodium minus chloride minus bicarbonate; raised when an acid has been added |
| Compensation | The predictable respiratory or renal response to a primary acid–base disorder; never complete |
| Tetany | Spontaneous muscle contraction from low ionised calcium lowering the excitation threshold |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Low sodium means the patient needs salt."** It almost always means excess water; giving salt to a patient with inappropriate antidiuresis or heart failure makes things worse.
- **"A normal pH means no acid–base disorder."** Two opposing disorders can cancel; the carbon dioxide, bicarbonate and anion gap must be read together.
- **"Hyperkalaemia should be treated by removing potassium."** The first step is protecting the heart (calcium) and shifting potassium into cells; removal comes after.
