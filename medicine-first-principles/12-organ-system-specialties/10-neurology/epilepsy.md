## Essence

A seizure is a transient episode of abnormal, excessive, synchronised neuronal firing; epilepsy is the enduring tendency to have them. The cortex is a network balanced between excitation (glutamate) and inhibition (gamma-aminobutyric acid, GABA), and a seizure is what happens when, in a patch of cortex or across the whole brain, that balance tips and neurons recruit their neighbours into a self-sustaining oscillation. Anything that lowers the threshold (a scar, a malformation, a channel mutation, a drug withdrawal, low sodium, fever in a child) can produce one. The clinical work is first to decide whether the event was a seizure at all, since faints and psychogenic attacks mimic it; second to decide whether it began focally or generally, because that tells the cause and chooses the drug; and third to weigh treatment, which suppresses excitability at the cost of sedation and teratogenicity, against the risk of recurrence.

## Core tenets

1. **A seizure is a failure of the excitation–inhibition balance, so every cause acts on that balance.** Neurons fire when depolarisation reaches threshold; inhibitory interneurons normally prevent runaway recruitment ([membrane potential and the action potential](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/membrane-potential-and-the-action-potential.md), [synaptic transmission](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/synaptic-transmission-and-neurotransmitters.md)). Mutations in sodium, potassium or GABA-receptor channels lower the threshold genetically; a cortical scar, tumour or malformation creates a locally disinhibited circuit; hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia, alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal and some drugs lower the threshold everywhere. A seizure in an adult with no history is therefore a search for a structural or metabolic cause, not a diagnosis.

2. **Seizures are either focal or generalised in onset, and the distinction organises everything.** A focal seizure starts in one cortical region and its first symptom reveals the site: a rising epigastric sensation, fear or déjà vu from the temporal lobe; jerking of one limb from the motor cortex; flashing lights from the occipital cortex. It may stay focal, with or without loss of awareness, or spread to a bilateral convulsion. A generalised seizure engages both hemispheres from the start through thalamocortical circuits: absences (brief staring with 3-per-second spike-and-wave on the electroencephalogram), myoclonic jerks, tonic-clonic convulsions. Focal onset points to a lesion and to drugs acting on sodium channels; generalised onset points to a genetic epilepsy and to broad-spectrum drugs ([nonlinear dynamics and networks](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/03-systems-and-models/nonlinear-dynamics-oscillations-and-networks.md)).

3. **The diagnosis is clinical, and the main competitors are syncope and psychogenic attacks.** A witnessed account is the key test: a tonic-clonic seizure has a sudden onset, stiffening then rhythmic jerking for a minute or two, cyanosis, tongue biting on the side, incontinence, and a confused drowsy recovery over many minutes. Syncope has a prodrome of light-headedness, pallor, brief limpness with a few jerks, and rapid recovery ([hemodynamics and blood pressure regulation](../../06-physiology/03-cardiovascular-system/hemodynamics-and-blood-pressure-regulation.md)); cardiac arrhythmia must be excluded with an electrocardiogram because it is the fatal mimic ([arrhythmias](../01-cardiology/arrhythmias.md)). Psychogenic non-epileptic attacks are prolonged, fluctuating, with eyes closed and resistance to opening, and are a dissociative response, not pretence ([the mental state and psychiatric formulation](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/03-psychiatry/the-mental-state-and-psychiatric-formulation.md)).

4. **Tests support the clinical diagnosis; they do not make it.** The interictal electroencephalogram (EEG) is normal in about half of people with epilepsy and shows minor abnormalities in a few percent of people without it, so it confirms a syndrome rather than rules epilepsy in or out ([using tests](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/01-the-clinical-encounter/using-tests-pretest-probability-and-thresholds.md), [electrophysiological testing](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/03-imaging-and-physiological-measurement/electrocardiography-and-electrophysiological-testing.md)). Video-EEG capturing an attack is definitive. Magnetic resonance imaging finds the structural cause of focal epilepsy (hippocampal sclerosis, cortical dysplasia, tumour, old infarct). Blood tests find the metabolic provocations.

5. **Anti-seizure drugs raise the threshold by damping excitability, and their side effects follow.** Sodium-channel blockers (carbamazepine, lamotrigine) limit rapid repetitive firing; GABA enhancers (benzodiazepines, valproate in part) strengthen inhibition; levetiracetam modulates synaptic vesicle release; others act on calcium channels or glutamate receptors ([central nervous system drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/central-nervous-system-drugs.md)). Because they damp neurons everywhere, sedation, dizziness and cognitive slowing are the common cost; several induce or inhibit hepatic enzymes and interact with other drugs ([drug metabolism and interactions](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/01-pharmacokinetics/drug-metabolism-and-interactions.md)), and valproate causes malformations and neurodevelopmental harm in the fetus ([teratogens](../../04-genetics-evolution-and-development/03-development/congenital-malformations-and-teratogens.md)). About two thirds of patients become seizure-free on drugs; of the rest, those with a single resectable focus can be cured by surgery.

6. **Status epilepticus is a seizure that has lost its off-switch, and it damages the brain by its own metabolism.** Most seizures stop within two minutes because inhibitory mechanisms recover; after about five minutes, GABA receptors are internalised and the seizure becomes self-sustaining and progressively resistant to benzodiazepines. Continuous firing outstrips oxygen and glucose supply, producing excitotoxic neuronal death, acidosis, hyperthermia and rhabdomyolysis ([cell injury](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/cell-injury-adaptation-and-death.md), [supply and demand](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#supply-and-demand)). Treatment is therefore escalated by the clock, and the mortality is from the cause (encephalitis, stroke, poisoning) as much as the seizure.

7. **Who else is involved.** A first seizure presents to [emergency medicine](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/README.md), where poisoning and withdrawal are common causes ([poisoning and overdose](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/poisoning-and-overdose.md)); status epilepticus goes to [critical care](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/03-critical-care/README.md). [Cardiology](../01-cardiology/README.md) excludes arrhythmic syncope. [Neurosurgery](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/surgical-subspecialties.md) resects foci and tumours. [Paediatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/README.md) owns febrile seizures and the childhood epilepsy syndromes; [obstetrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/README.md) shares the pregnant woman whose drugs are teratogenic and whose seizures threaten the fetus; [psychiatry](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/03-psychiatry/README.md) manages non-epileptic attacks and the depression that is twice as common in epilepsy; [primary care](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/README.md) and the law share the questions of driving, employment and sudden unexpected death in epilepsy ([consent, capacity and confidentiality](../../15-medicine-in-society/02-ethics-law-and-the-profession/consent-capacity-and-confidentiality.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Seizure | Transient symptoms from abnormal excessive synchronous neuronal activity |
| Epilepsy | Enduring predisposition to seizures; in practice two unprovoked seizures or one with high recurrence risk |
| Focal onset | Seizure beginning in one hemisphere; first symptom localises it |
| Generalised onset | Seizure engaging both hemispheres from the start via thalamocortical circuits |
| Aura | The conscious first part of a focal seizure |
| Provoked seizure | Seizure from an acute cause (metabolic, toxic, fever) without enduring epilepsy |
| Electroencephalogram | Scalp recording of cortical electrical activity; supports syndrome diagnosis |
| Status epilepticus | Seizure beyond about five minutes or recurrent seizures without recovery; a medical emergency |
| Psychogenic non-epileptic attack | Dissociative episode resembling a seizure without abnormal electrical activity |
| Sudden unexpected death in epilepsy | Death during or after a seizure, usually tonic-clonic, from cardiorespiratory failure |

## Common misconceptions

- **"One seizure is epilepsy."** About half of people with a single unprovoked seizure never have another; treatment after one is a judgement on recurrence risk, usually informed by imaging and EEG.
- **"A normal EEG excludes epilepsy."** It is normal between attacks in about half of patients; the diagnosis is made on the history.
- **"Restrain the patient and put something in the mouth."** Neither helps; protect the head, time the seizure, and treat with a benzodiazepine only if it exceeds five minutes.
