## Essence

A seizure is a transient episode of abnormal, excessive, synchronised neuronal firing; epilepsy is the enduring tendency to have them. The cortex is balanced between excitation (glutamate) and inhibition (gamma-aminobutyric acid, GABA), and a seizure is what happens when that balance tips and neurons recruit their neighbours into a self-sustaining oscillation. Anything that lowers the threshold (a scar, a channel mutation, drug withdrawal, low sodium, fever in a child) can produce one. The clinical work is to decide whether the event was a seizure at all, since faints and psychogenic attacks mimic it; whether it began focally or generally, which tells the cause and chooses the drug; and whether treatment, which suppresses excitability at the cost of sedation and teratogenicity, is worth the risk of recurrence.

## Core tenets

1. **A seizure is a failure of the excitation–inhibition balance, so every cause acts on that balance.** Neurons fire when depolarisation reaches threshold; inhibitory interneurons normally prevent runaway recruitment ([membrane potential and the action potential](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/membrane-potential-and-the-action-potential.md), [synaptic transmission](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/synaptic-transmission-and-neurotransmitters.md)). Channel mutations lower the threshold genetically; a scar, tumour or malformation creates a locally disinhibited circuit; hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia, alcohol withdrawal and some drugs lower it everywhere. A first seizure in an adult is therefore a search for a cause, not a diagnosis.

2. **Seizures are either focal or generalised in onset, and the distinction organises everything.** A focal seizure starts in one region and its first symptom reveals the site: déjà vu or a rising epigastric sensation from the temporal lobe, jerking of one limb from the motor cortex, flashing lights from the occipital cortex; it may stay focal or spread to a bilateral convulsion. A generalised seizure engages both hemispheres from the start through thalamocortical circuits: absences, myoclonic jerks, tonic-clonic convulsions. Focal onset points to a lesion and to drugs acting on sodium channels; generalised onset points to a genetic epilepsy and to broad-spectrum drugs ([nonlinear dynamics and networks](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/03-systems-and-models/nonlinear-dynamics-oscillations-and-networks.md)).

3. **The diagnosis is clinical, and the main competitors are syncope and psychogenic attacks.** A witnessed account is the key test: a tonic-clonic seizure has sudden onset, stiffening then rhythmic jerking for a minute or two, lateral tongue biting and a confused recovery over many minutes. Syncope has a prodrome of light-headedness, pallor, brief limpness and rapid recovery ([hemodynamics and blood pressure regulation](../../06-physiology/03-cardiovascular-system/hemodynamics-and-blood-pressure-regulation.md)); cardiac arrhythmia must be excluded with an electrocardiogram because it is the fatal mimic ([arrhythmias](../01-cardiology/arrhythmias.md)). Psychogenic non-epileptic attacks are prolonged and fluctuating, with eyes closed, and are a dissociative response, not pretence ([the mental state and psychiatric formulation](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/03-psychiatry/the-mental-state-and-psychiatric-formulation.md)).

4. **Tests support the clinical diagnosis; they do not make it.** The interictal electroencephalogram (EEG) is normal in about half of people with epilepsy and mildly abnormal in a few percent without it, so it confirms a syndrome rather than ruling epilepsy in or out ([using tests](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/01-the-clinical-encounter/using-tests-pretest-probability-and-thresholds.md), [electrophysiological testing](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/03-imaging-and-physiological-measurement/electrocardiography-and-electrophysiological-testing.md)). Video-EEG capturing an attack is definitive; magnetic resonance imaging finds the structural cause of focal epilepsy.

5. **Anti-seizure drugs raise the threshold by damping excitability, and their side effects follow.** Sodium-channel blockers (carbamazepine, lamotrigine) limit repetitive firing; GABA enhancers (benzodiazepines) strengthen inhibition; levetiracetam modulates vesicle release ([central nervous system drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/central-nervous-system-drugs.md)). Because they damp neurons everywhere, sedation and cognitive slowing are the cost; several induce hepatic enzymes ([drug metabolism and interactions](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/01-pharmacokinetics/drug-metabolism-and-interactions.md)), and valproate harms the fetus ([teratogens](../../04-genetics-evolution-and-development/03-development/congenital-malformations-and-teratogens.md)). About two thirds become seizure-free on drugs; of the rest, those with a single resectable focus can be cured by surgery.

6. **Status epilepticus is a seizure that has lost its off-switch, and it damages the brain by its own metabolism.** Most seizures stop within two minutes as inhibition recovers; after about five minutes, GABA receptors are internalised and the seizure becomes self-sustaining and resistant to benzodiazepines. Continuous firing outstrips oxygen and glucose supply, producing excitotoxic neuronal death, acidosis and hyperthermia ([cell injury](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/cell-injury-adaptation-and-death.md), [supply and demand](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#supply-and-demand)). Treatment is escalated by the clock, and mortality is from the cause as much as the seizure.

7. **Who else is involved.** A first seizure presents to [emergency medicine](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/README.md), where poisoning and withdrawal are common causes ([poisoning and overdose](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/poisoning-and-overdose.md)); status epilepticus goes to [critical care](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/03-critical-care/README.md). [Cardiology](../01-cardiology/README.md) excludes arrhythmic syncope; [neurosurgery](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/surgical-subspecialties.md) resects foci. [Paediatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/README.md) owns febrile seizures and the childhood syndromes; [obstetrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/README.md) the pregnant woman on teratogenic drugs; [psychiatry](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/03-psychiatry/README.md) non-epileptic attacks and the depression twice as common in epilepsy; [primary care](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/README.md) and the law the questions of driving and sudden unexpected death in epilepsy ([consent, capacity and confidentiality](../../15-medicine-in-society/02-ethics-law-and-the-profession/consent-capacity-and-confidentiality.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Seizure | Transient symptoms from abnormal excessive synchronous neuronal activity |
| Epilepsy | Enduring predisposition to seizures; in practice two unprovoked seizures or one with high recurrence risk |
| Focal onset | Seizure beginning in one hemisphere; first symptom localises it |
| Generalised onset | Seizure engaging both hemispheres from the start via thalamocortical circuits |
| Electroencephalogram | Scalp recording of cortical electrical activity; supports syndrome diagnosis |
| Status epilepticus | Seizure beyond about five minutes or recurrent seizures without recovery; a medical emergency |
| Psychogenic non-epileptic attack | Dissociative episode resembling a seizure without abnormal electrical activity |
| Sudden unexpected death in epilepsy | Death during or after a seizure, usually tonic-clonic, from cardiorespiratory failure |

## Common misconceptions

- **"One seizure is epilepsy."** About half of people with a single unprovoked seizure never have another; treatment after one is a judgement on recurrence risk, usually informed by imaging and EEG.
- **"A normal EEG excludes epilepsy."** It is normal between attacks in about half of patients; the diagnosis is made on the history.
- **"Restrain the patient and put something in the mouth."** Neither helps; protect the head, time the seizure, and treat with a benzodiazepine only if it exceeds five minutes.
