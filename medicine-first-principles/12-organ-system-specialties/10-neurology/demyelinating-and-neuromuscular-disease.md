## Essence

Movement requires a chain: upper motor neuron, lower motor neuron, myelin that insulates both, the neuromuscular junction, and muscle. Sensation runs the same chain in reverse. This leaf covers diseases that break the chain at a link other than the neuron's cell body: the myelin of the central nervous system (multiple sclerosis), the myelin and axons of peripheral nerves (neuropathies, including Guillain–Barré syndrome), the junction (myasthenia gravis) and the muscle (myopathies and dystrophies). Two ideas organise them. First, the level of the lesion can be deduced at the bedside from the pattern of weakness, reflexes, sensory loss and fatigability, because each link fails in a characteristic way. Second, many of these diseases are autoimmune attacks on a single molecular target, and because the target is known and the structures can repair, they are among the most treatable conditions in neurology, in contrast to the degenerations.

## Core tenets

1. **Myelin makes conduction fast and economical, so demyelination causes slowing, block and fatigue.** Saltatory conduction between nodes of Ranvier allows a myelinated axon to conduct at up to 100 m/s with sodium channels only at the nodes ([nervous tissue](../../05-tissues-and-anatomy/01-tissues/nervous-tissue.md), [membrane potential and the action potential](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/membrane-potential-and-the-action-potential.md)). Strip the myelin and the internode leaks current: conduction slows, fails at higher frequencies or temperatures (which is why multiple sclerosis symptoms worsen in heat), or blocks altogether. Nerve conduction studies and evoked potentials measure this slowing directly ([electrophysiological testing](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/03-imaging-and-physiological-measurement/electrocardiography-and-electrophysiological-testing.md)). Remyelination restores function, which is why relapses recover; axonal loss, which accumulates with repeated attacks, does not.

2. **Multiple sclerosis is T-cell-mediated inflammation of central myelin, scattered in space and time.** Autoreactive lymphocytes cross the blood–brain barrier and attack oligodendrocyte myelin in focal plaques, favouring optic nerves, brainstem, cerebellum, periventricular white matter and spinal cord ([autoimmunity](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/04-immunity-in-disease/autoimmunity.md), [barriers and compartments](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#barriers-and-compartments)). The clinical rule of dissemination in space and time follows: episodes of optic neuritis, diplopia, limb weakness or sensory loss in different places at different times, each evolving over days and recovering over weeks. Magnetic resonance imaging shows the plaques and, with gadolinium, which are active; cerebrospinal fluid shows oligoclonal immunoglobulin bands ([magnetic resonance imaging](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/03-imaging-and-physiological-measurement/magnetic-resonance-imaging.md)). Risk combines HLA alleles, low vitamin D and latitude, smoking, and Epstein–Barr virus infection, which recent cohort data make nearly necessary ([the host and its environment](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#the-host-and-its-environment)). Most patients begin with relapses and remissions; over decades, many enter a progressive phase in which slow axonal loss outpaces inflammation.

3. **Disease-modifying therapy in multiple sclerosis works by keeping lymphocytes out of the brain, and the more completely, the better.** Interferon-beta and glatiramer modestly reduce relapses; natalizumab blocks the integrin lymphocytes need to cross the endothelium; fingolimod traps them in lymph nodes; ocrelizumab depletes B cells; alemtuzumab depletes lymphocytes wholesale ([biologics](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/biologics-and-advanced-therapies.md), [anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/anti-inflammatory-and-immunomodulatory-drugs.md)). Higher-efficacy agents reduce relapses by around two thirds or more and slow disability, at the price of infection, including reactivation of JC virus causing progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy. These drugs do little for the progressive phase, which is neurodegeneration rather than inflammation.

4. **Peripheral neuropathy is length-dependent when metabolic and patchy when inflammatory or vasculitic.** The longest axons fail first in a metabolic insult, so diabetes, alcohol, B12 deficiency and many drugs produce symmetrical glove-and-stocking sensory loss starting in the toes ([diabetes mellitus](../05-endocrinology/diabetes-mellitus.md)). Inflammatory neuropathies attack nerves at random: Guillain–Barré syndrome is an acute immune attack on peripheral myelin or axons, usually triggered by a preceding infection whose antigens resemble nerve gangliosides (molecular mimicry, classically *Campylobacter*), producing ascending weakness and areflexia over days that can paralyse the diaphragm; intravenous immunoglobulin or plasma exchange removes the antibodies, and ventilation supports the patient until remyelination occurs ([respiratory failure and mechanical ventilation](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/03-critical-care/respiratory-failure-and-mechanical-ventilation.md)). Mononeuritis multiplex, individual nerves failing one after another, is the signature of vasculitis ([connective tissue disease and vasculitis](../09-rheumatology-and-clinical-immunology/connective-tissue-disease-and-vasculitis.md)).

5. **Myasthenia gravis is antibody blockade of the neuromuscular junction, and its hallmark is fatigability.** Antibodies against the acetylcholine receptor (or, in a minority, muscle-specific kinase) reduce the number of functional receptors and trigger complement damage to the postsynaptic membrane ([synaptic transmission](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/synaptic-transmission-and-neurotransmitters.md), [hypersensitivity type II](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/04-immunity-in-disease/hypersensitivity-and-allergy.md)). The safety factor of transmission is lost, so repeated use exhausts the junction: ptosis and diplopia worsen through the day, chewing tires, and in crisis the respiratory muscles fail. The thymus is often abnormal and its removal helps. Treatment follows the mechanism: acetylcholinesterase inhibitors such as pyridostigmine prolong the transmitter's action, immunosuppression reduces antibody, plasma exchange or immunoglobulin removes it in crisis. Many drugs, including aminoglycosides and some anaesthetic agents, impair the junction and can precipitate crisis ([general and regional anaesthesia](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/general-and-regional-anaesthesia.md)).

6. **Muscle disease is proximal, symmetrical weakness without sensory loss, and the creatine kinase is the clue.** Myopathies, whether inflammatory (dermatomyositis, polymyositis), metabolic, drug-induced (statins, glucocorticoids) or genetic (the dystrophies), weaken the shoulder and hip girdles first, so patients struggle to rise from a chair or lift the arms, while reflexes and sensation stay normal ([skeletal muscle contraction and energetics](../../06-physiology/10-musculoskeletal-system-and-skin/skeletal-muscle-contraction-and-energetics.md)). Damaged fibres leak creatine kinase into blood; electromyography shows small, short motor units; biopsy and genetic testing name the disease. Duchenne muscular dystrophy, an X-linked absence of dystrophin that leaves the sarcolemma unable to withstand contraction, is the model genetic myopathy and a target of exon-skipping and gene therapies ([single-gene disorders](../../08-pathology/04-genetic-metabolic-and-environmental-disease/single-gene-and-chromosomal-disorders.md)).

7. **Who else is involved.** Respiratory failure from Guillain–Barré syndrome, myasthenic crisis or advanced muscular dystrophy belongs to [critical care](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/03-critical-care/README.md) and long-term to [pulmonology](../02-pulmonology/README.md) for home ventilation. [Anaesthesia](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/README.md) must know about myasthenia and dystrophies because muscle relaxants and some anaesthetics are hazardous. [Ophthalmology](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/05-ophthalmology-and-ent/README.md) sees optic neuritis and the diplopia of myasthenia first. [Rheumatology](../09-rheumatology-and-clinical-immunology/README.md) shares myositis and vasculitic neuropathy; [endocrinology](../05-endocrinology/README.md) the commonest neuropathy, diabetic; [infectious diseases](../08-infectious-diseases/README.md) the complications of lymphocyte depletion; [paediatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/README.md) and clinical genetics the dystrophies; [urology](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/surgical-subspecialties.md) and [rehabilitation](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/rehabilitation-and-disability.md) the bladder, spasticity and mobility of multiple sclerosis; [obstetrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/README.md) the young women who form most of the multiple sclerosis and myasthenia population.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Demyelination | Loss of myelin with preserved axon; slows or blocks conduction and is reversible |
| Saltatory conduction | Jumping of the action potential between nodes of Ranvier along a myelinated axon |
| Dissemination in space and time | Lesions in different sites at different times; the diagnostic principle of multiple sclerosis |
| Oligoclonal bands | Immunoglobulin bands in cerebrospinal fluid not present in serum; intrathecal antibody synthesis |
| Length-dependent neuropathy | Symmetrical distal sensory loss as the longest axons fail first |
| Molecular mimicry | Immune response to a microbe cross-reacting with a self-antigen, as in Guillain–Barré syndrome |
| Fatigability | Weakness that worsens with repeated use; the sign of junction failure |
| Safety factor | Excess of transmitter and receptors at the junction that makes transmission reliable |
| Creatine kinase | Muscle enzyme released into blood when fibres are damaged |
| Proximal myopathic weakness | Girdle weakness without sensory loss or reflex change |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Multiple sclerosis is a wheelchair disease."** With modern therapy most patients remain ambulant for decades; the prognosis has changed more than almost any chronic neurological disease.
- **"Weakness with normal sensation must be in the brain."** It is the pattern of junction and muscle disease; the level is told by fatigability, reflexes and distribution, not by assumption.
- **"Guillain–Barré syndrome is a mild self-limiting illness."** It recovers in most, but a quarter need ventilation and a few percent die; the vital capacity, not the limb power, is what is watched.
