## Essence

A stroke is a sudden focal loss of brain function caused by a blocked or burst blood vessel. The brain consumes a fifth of the body's oxygen with almost no stored fuel, so neurons downstream of an occluded artery stop firing within seconds and begin to die within minutes ([supply and demand](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#supply-and-demand)). Because each artery supplies a known territory and each territory has a known function, the deficit tells the clinician which vessel is blocked. About 85% of strokes are ischaemic (a thrombus or embolus), the rest haemorrhagic; they look identical at the bedside, are treated in opposite ways, and are separated only by imaging. The organising idea of modern stroke care is that the infarct core is surrounded by a penumbra of threatened but salvageable tissue, and that reopening the vessel within hours saves it, so stroke is a time-critical emergency in which every minute of delay costs neurons.

## Core tenets

1. **Deficit maps onto vascular territory, so the examination localises the lesion.** The middle cerebral artery supplies the lateral hemisphere: its occlusion causes contralateral face and arm weakness and sensory loss, with aphasia if the dominant hemisphere, neglect if the non-dominant ([motor control](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/motor-control.md), [higher functions](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/higher-functions-memory-language-and-consciousness.md)). The anterior cerebral artery supplies the medial hemisphere, so leg weakness predominates. The posterior circulation supplies brainstem, cerebellum and occipital lobes, producing vertigo, diplopia, ataxia, crossed signs and visual field loss ([head, neck and spine](../../05-tissues-and-anatomy/03-regional-anatomy/head-neck-and-spine.md)). Small deep perforating arteries cause lacunar strokes with pure motor or sensory deficits. The sudden onset is what says "vascular"; the pattern says "where".

2. **Ischaemic stroke has three main mechanisms, and the mechanism decides secondary prevention.** Large-artery atherosclerosis in the carotid or intracranial vessels sheds thrombus ([atherosclerosis](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/atherosclerosis.md)); cardiac embolism, mostly from atrial fibrillation, throws clot from a fibrillating atrium ([arrhythmias](../01-cardiology/arrhythmias.md)); small-vessel disease from hypertension and diabetes occludes perforators ([thrombosis, embolism and infarction](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/thrombosis-embolism-and-infarction.md)). Finding the cause (carotid imaging, cardiac rhythm monitoring, echocardiography) is why the workup continues after the acute phase: carotid stenosis is treated by endarterectomy, atrial fibrillation by anticoagulation, small-vessel disease by blood pressure and antiplatelet therapy. A transient ischaemic attack is the same process that resolved; it carries a high early risk of completed stroke and is treated with the same urgency.

3. **The penumbra is the therapeutic target, and collateral flow determines how long it survives.** Within the core, blood flow is below the threshold for membrane pump failure and cells die quickly ([cell injury](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/cell-injury-adaptation-and-death.md)). Around it, collateral flow from neighbouring arteries keeps tissue above the death threshold but below the firing threshold: silent but alive. Without reperfusion the core grows into the penumbra over hours; with it, the penumbra recovers. Perfusion imaging can show the mismatch between core and penumbra and select patients for reperfusion beyond the fixed time windows ([compensation and decompensation](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#compensation-and-decompensation)).

4. **Imaging first, because the treatments for ischaemia and haemorrhage are opposites.** Non-contrast computed tomography shows blood immediately as a bright area and so excludes haemorrhage within minutes, while early infarction is often invisible ([radiography and computed tomography](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/03-imaging-and-physiological-measurement/radiography-and-computed-tomography.md)). Computed tomography angiography shows the occluded vessel; diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging shows infarcted tissue within minutes of onset ([magnetic resonance imaging](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/03-imaging-and-physiological-measurement/magnetic-resonance-imaging.md)). No antithrombotic treatment is given until bleeding is excluded.

5. **Reperfusion reverses the mechanism, and its benefit decays by the minute.** Intravenous thrombolysis with a plasminogen activator such as alteplase dissolves fibrin; mechanical thrombectomy pulls a large-vessel clot out through a catheter ([autonomic and cardiovascular drugs](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/autonomic-and-cardiovascular-drugs.md), [devices](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/04-non-drug-therapeutics/devices-implants-and-biomaterials.md)). Randomised trials show that the number of patients who recover independence per hundred treated falls steadily with time from onset, and that thrombectomy for large-vessel occlusion is among the most effective treatments in medicine ([randomised controlled trials](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/02-evidence-based-medicine/randomized-controlled-trials.md)). The cost is haemorrhage into the infarct, which is why imaging and time limits exist ([harm and benefit](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#harm-and-benefit)).

6. **Haemorrhagic stroke injures by pressure and by blood itself.** Intracerebral haemorrhage, usually from hypertensive damage to small arteries or from amyloid-weakened vessels in the old, tears tissue, compresses its surroundings and raises intracranial pressure within a rigid skull; the haematoma often expands over the first hours, so lowering blood pressure and reversing anticoagulation early limits growth ([bleeding and clotting disorders](../06-haematology/bleeding-and-clotting-disorders.md)). Subarachnoid haemorrhage from a ruptured aneurysm presents as a sudden severe headache ([headache](headache.md)) and threatens rebleeding and delayed vasospasm; the aneurysm is secured by coiling or clipping.

7. **Recovery is plasticity, and it is driven by rehabilitation.** Neurons do not regenerate, but surviving cortex reorganises, and the penumbra that was stunned recovers over days to weeks. Most functional gain occurs within three months and depends on early, intensive, task-specific practice; swallowing assessment, prevention of aspiration, thrombosis and pressure injury, and treatment of post-stroke depression determine survival as much as the acute phase ([rehabilitation and disability](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/rehabilitation-and-disability.md), [injury and repair](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#injury-and-repair)).

8. **Who else is involved.** Stroke is the paradigm of a shared emergency. [Emergency medicine](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/README.md) and prehospital services recognise it; [radiology](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/03-imaging-and-physiological-measurement/README.md) and interventional neuroradiology image and reperfuse; [neurosurgery](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/surgical-subspecialties.md) evacuates haematomas, decompresses malignant oedema and secures aneurysms; [cardiology](../01-cardiology/README.md) finds and treats atrial fibrillation and patent foramen ovale and shares hypertension; vascular surgery operates on the carotid; [haematology](../06-haematology/README.md) advises on anticoagulation and thrombophilia in the young; [critical care](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/03-critical-care/README.md) manages the unconscious patient; [rehabilitation medicine](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/rehabilitation-and-disability.md), [geriatrics](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/README.md) and [primary care](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/05-primary-care-and-integration/README.md) own the months that follow and the risk-factor control that prevents the next one.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ischaemic stroke | Infarction from arterial occlusion by thrombus or embolus |
| Haemorrhagic stroke | Bleeding into brain (intracerebral) or around it (subarachnoid) |
| Transient ischaemic attack | Focal ischaemic deficit that resolves, usually within an hour; a warning of stroke |
| Penumbra | Hypoperfused but viable tissue around the infarct core, salvageable by reperfusion |
| Collateral circulation | Alternative arterial supply that sustains the penumbra |
| Thrombolysis | Drug dissolution of fibrin clot with a plasminogen activator |
| Thrombectomy | Mechanical catheter removal of a large-vessel clot |
| Lacunar stroke | Small deep infarct from occlusion of a perforating artery |
| Neglect and aphasia | Non-dominant and dominant hemisphere cortical deficits respectively |
| Neuroplasticity | Reorganisation of surviving cortex that underlies recovery |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A deficit that resolved needs no urgent action."** A transient ischaemic attack carries a risk of completed stroke of several percent within days; it is the best opportunity for prevention.
- **"Lower the blood pressure in acute stroke."** In ischaemic stroke, raised pressure may be maintaining the penumbra through collaterals; aggressive lowering can extend the infarct. The rule differs in haemorrhage.
- **"Stroke is a disease of the old."** A quarter of strokes occur under 65, and in the young the causes (dissection, patent foramen ovale, thrombophilia, drugs) are different and need a different search.
