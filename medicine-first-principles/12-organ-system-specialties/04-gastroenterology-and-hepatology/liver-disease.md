## Essence

The liver is the organ with the greatest reserve and the greatest capacity to regenerate, and liver disease is the story of both being exhausted. A handful of insults (alcohol, excess fat under insulin resistance, the hepatitis B and C viruses, autoimmunity, iron and copper overload, drugs) injure hepatocytes by different mechanisms but produce one response: inflammation, then fibrosis, then cirrhosis, in which regenerating nodules are walled off by scar and the architecture that lets blood meet hepatocyte is destroyed. Cirrhosis fails in two directions at once. The scarred liver resists portal blood flow, so pressure rises and blood finds collateral paths (varices) and fluid leaks into the abdomen (ascites); and the shrinking hepatocyte mass stops making albumin and clotting factors and stops clearing ammonia and drugs. Because the liver does so many jobs, its failure is systemic, and hepatology interfaces with nearly every other specialty.

## Core tenets

1. **One response to many injuries: fibrosis is the liver's repair, and cirrhosis is repair that has destroyed the architecture.** Hepatocyte death, from any cause, activates the hepatic stellate cells that normally store vitamin A into myofibroblasts that lay down collagen ([fibrosis](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/tissue-repair-regeneration-and-fibrosis.md)). While the insult continues, scar bridges between portal tracts and central veins, and surviving hepatocytes regenerate inside the scar as nodules without a normal sinusoidal supply. Fibrosis regresses if the cause is removed early; established cirrhosis mostly does not. The commonest causes worldwide are hepatitis B, hepatitis C, alcohol and metabolic-dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, with proportions that vary sharply by region (2020s); hepatitis B alone infects ~250 million people.

2. **Each cause has its own mechanism of hepatocyte injury.** Ethanol is oxidised to acetaldehyde, which binds proteins and generates oxidative stress, and its metabolism diverts the liver into making fat. Insulin resistance floods the liver with fatty acids and drives lipogenesis; the fat itself is benign until lipotoxicity and inflammation turn steatosis into steatohepatitis ([metabolic syndrome](../05-endocrinology/obesity-lipids-and-metabolic-syndrome.md)). Hepatitis B and C [viruses](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/01-microbes-and-infection/viruses.md) damage mainly through the T-cell response against infected cells, which is why the virus persists when the response is weak and the liver is destroyed when it is strong. Autoimmune hepatitis and primary biliary cholangitis are [autoimmune](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/04-immunity-in-disease/autoimmunity.md) attacks on hepatocytes and small bile ducts. Haemochromatosis and Wilson's disease are inherited failures to excrete iron and copper. Paracetamol in overdose saturates conjugation and the excess is oxidised to a reactive metabolite that depletes glutathione and kills the centrilobular zone ([drug metabolism](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/01-pharmacokinetics/drug-metabolism-and-interactions.md), [poisoning](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/poisoning-and-overdose.md)).

3. **Portal hypertension is a pressure gradient looking for a path, and it produces varices and ascites.** Scar and regenerating nodules compress sinusoids, and the hepatic resistance to the ~1.2 L/min of portal flow rises; nitric oxide deficiency inside the liver and excess outside it worsen the mismatch. Portal pressure above about 10 mmHg over the hepatic vein opens collaterals to the systemic veins at the oesophagus, stomach, rectum and umbilicus ([gradients and flow](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#gradients-and-flow)). Oesophageal varices are thin-walled and bleed catastrophically ([GI bleeding](acid-peptic-disease-and-gi-bleeding.md)). The same pressure, with low albumin and splanchnic vasodilatation, pushes fluid into the peritoneum ([oedema](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/edema-and-fluid-imbalance.md)); the kidney reads the arterial underfilling as hypovolaemia, retains sodium avidly and makes the ascites worse, by the same misfiring reflex as in [heart failure](../01-cardiology/heart-failure.md). Spontaneous infection of ascitic fluid and the hepatorenal syndrome (a functional [acute kidney injury](../03-nephrology/acute-kidney-injury.md) from extreme renal vasoconstriction) are its complications.

4. **Hepatocellular failure shows in what the liver no longer makes or clears.** Albumin falls (oedema, ascites); clotting factors fall and the prothrombin time lengthens, though bleeding risk is balanced by the loss of anticoagulant proteins as well ([haemostasis](../../06-physiology/08-blood/haemostasis-and-coagulation.md)); bilirubin is not conjugated and excreted (jaundice); ammonia and other gut-derived toxins escape through portosystemic shunts to the brain (hepatic encephalopathy, with confusion, a flapping tremor and coma); oestrogens are not cleared (gynaecomastia, spider naevi); and drugs cleared by the liver accumulate. Serum albumin, bilirubin and prothrombin time, with ascites and encephalopathy, make up the Child–Pugh and MELD scores that grade severity and prioritise transplantation ([prognosis](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/01-the-clinical-encounter/prognosis-and-shared-decision-making.md)).

5. **Liver tests measure injury and function separately, and imaging stages the scar.** The aminotransferases leak from damaged hepatocytes and measure injury (very high in viral and paracetamol hepatitis, modest in cirrhosis, where few cells are left to leak); alkaline phosphatase and gamma-glutamyl transferase rise with bile duct obstruction or injury (cholestasis); bilirubin, albumin and prothrombin time measure function ([clinical chemistry](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/02-laboratory-medicine/clinical-chemistry-and-reference-ranges.md)). Serology, iron and copper studies and autoantibodies name the cause. Ultrasound shows a shrunken nodular liver, splenomegaly and ascites; elastography measures liver stiffness as a proxy for fibrosis and has largely replaced biopsy for staging. Compensated cirrhosis is often silent and found incidentally, which is why abnormal liver tests in an asymptomatic patient are pursued.

6. **Treatment removes the cause, lowers portal pressure, and replaces the organ.** Stopping alcohol, weight loss, antiviral therapy (direct-acting antivirals cure hepatitis C in over 95%; nucleoside analogues suppress hepatitis B), venesection for iron, chelation for copper, and immunosuppression for autoimmune hepatitis all halt the injury and allow fibrosis to regress if the architecture is not yet lost. Non-selective beta-blockers (propranolol) lower portal pressure by reducing splanchnic flow and prevent variceal bleeding; band ligation obliterates varices; a transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt decompresses the portal system at the cost of more encephalopathy. Diuretics (spironolactone, which opposes the aldosterone excess) and paracentesis treat ascites; lactulose and rifaximin lower gut ammonia production. Transplantation is the only treatment for end-stage disease and for acute liver failure, with survival over 70% at five years (2010s–2020s); donor scarcity makes selection an [ethical allocation](../../15-medicine-in-society/01-health-systems/health-economics-and-priority-setting.md) problem.

7. **Cirrhosis is a precancerous state.** Decades of hepatocyte death and regeneration under inflammation accumulate mutations, so hepatocellular carcinoma arises in a cirrhotic liver at 1–4% per year, and hepatitis B can cause it even without cirrhosis through viral integration ([causes of cancer](../../08-pathology/03-neoplasia/causes-of-cancer.md)). Surveillance ultrasound every six months in cirrhotics finds tumours small enough to resect, ablate or transplant, and is one of the few cancer surveillance programmes with a plausible survival benefit in a high-risk group ([common solid tumours](../07-oncology/common-solid-tumours.md)).

8. **Who else is involved.** [Haematology](../06-haematology/bleeding-and-clotting-disorders.md) is involved because the liver makes the clotting factors and the spleen traps platelets, and because the coagulopathy of cirrhosis is both bleeding- and thrombosis-prone. [Transplant surgery](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/surgical-subspecialties.md) is the end point, and [critical care](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/03-critical-care/README.md) manages acute liver failure and variceal haemorrhage. [Nephrology](../03-nephrology/acute-kidney-injury.md) co-manages hepatorenal syndrome. [Infectious diseases](../08-infectious-diseases/hiv-and-chronic-viral-infections.md) shares viral hepatitis and HIV co-infection; [endocrinology](../05-endocrinology/obesity-lipids-and-metabolic-syndrome.md) shares fatty liver, now the commonest liver disease; [psychiatry and addiction services](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/03-psychiatry/addiction-and-substance-use.md) share alcohol dependence, without whose treatment the liver cannot recover. [Oncology](../07-oncology/README.md) treats the hepatocellular carcinoma. [Public health](../../15-medicine-in-society/03-populations-and-the-future/global-health-and-pandemics.md) prevents most of it: hepatitis B vaccination, screening of blood, needle programmes and alcohol pricing.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Steatosis | Fat accumulation in hepatocytes; reversible, but the first step of metabolic and alcoholic liver disease |
| Cirrhosis | Diffuse fibrosis with regenerating nodules and loss of normal lobular architecture |
| Portal hypertension | Raised pressure in the portal venous system from hepatic resistance to flow |
| Varices | Dilated portosystemic collateral veins, chiefly oesophageal, that bleed |
| Ascites | Free fluid in the peritoneal cavity from portal hypertension and sodium retention |
| Hepatic encephalopathy | Brain dysfunction from gut-derived toxins bypassing a failing liver |
| Cholestasis | Impaired bile flow; raised alkaline phosphatase and conjugated bilirubin |
| Decompensation | The appearance of ascites, variceal bleeding, encephalopathy or jaundice in a cirrhotic |
| MELD score | Prognostic score from bilirubin, creatinine, prothrombin time and sodium; used to rank transplant candidates |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Only heavy drinkers get cirrhosis."** Fatty liver from obesity and insulin resistance is now the commonest cause of chronic liver disease in many countries, and viral hepatitis leads worldwide.
- **"Normal liver tests mean a normal liver."** Compensated cirrhosis commonly has near-normal aminotransferases; function tests and imaging are needed.
- **"A long prothrombin time means a cirrhotic will bleed."** Pro- and anticoagulant factors fall together; cirrhotics also clot, and the prothrombin time predicts bleeding poorly.
- **"Jaundice means liver failure."** Obstruction of the bile duct by a stone or tumour causes jaundice with a normal liver; the pattern of liver tests and an ultrasound separate them.
