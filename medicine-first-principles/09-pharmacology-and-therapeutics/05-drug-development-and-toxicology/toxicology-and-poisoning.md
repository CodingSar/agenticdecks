## Essence

Paracelsus's rule, that the dose makes the poison, is the whole of toxicology in one sentence: every substance has a dose–response curve for harm, and a poison is any substance taken to the point on that curve where harm begins. Toxicology studies that curve for drugs, chemicals, metals, gases, plants and venoms, and poisoning is what happens when a person reaches it, by accident, intent or occupation. The mechanisms are those of pharmacology at excess: receptors overwhelmed, enzymes inhibited, metabolism saturated so that toxic pathways open, and cells deprived of oxygen or energy. The single idea is that treatment follows from mechanism and kinetics: reduce what is absorbed, increase what is eliminated, block or reverse the toxic action with an antidote where one exists, and support the organs until the body clears the rest, which it almost always does.

## Core tenets

1. **Toxicity is dose–response, and the curve's threshold, slope and reversibility decide the danger.** The [quantal curve](../02-pharmacodynamics/dose-response-and-therapeutic-index.md) gives a median lethal dose and, more usefully, a threshold below which no effect is seen, used to set occupational and environmental limits with safety factors for inter-individual and inter-species variation. Agents without a threshold (genotoxic carcinogens, by assumption ionising radiation) are regulated by acceptable risk rather than safe dose. Reversibility matters as much as potency: an organophosphate's inhibition of acetylcholinesterase "ages" into a permanent bond within hours, which is why its antidote (pralidoxime) must be given early, and a chronic exposure (lead, cadmium) accumulates damage that acute measures cannot undo.

2. **Overdose breaks the kinetic assumptions that hold at therapeutic doses.** Metabolism saturates, so elimination becomes zero-order and the half-life lengthens with dose; minor pathways that are irrelevant at normal doses carry a toxic share; absorption continues for hours from a mass of tablets or a modified-release preparation, so concentrations peak late; protein binding saturates, raising the free fraction. Paracetamol is the model case: glucuronidation and sulphation cope with therapeutic doses, but in overdose the CYP2E1 pathway produces N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine faster than glutathione can conjugate it, and hepatocytes die over the next two to three days while the patient feels well. The antidote, acetylcysteine, replenishes glutathione, and its efficacy depends on being given within about eight hours, before the damage is done.

3. **The toxidrome is the mechanism written on the patient.** A cluster of signs identifies the receptor system involved before the agent is known. Anticholinergic: hot, dry, dilated pupils, delirium, retention (antihistamines, tricyclics). Cholinergic: salivation, lacrimation, urination, diarrhoea, bronchorrhoea, bradycardia, small pupils, weakness (organophosphates). Sympathomimetic: agitation, tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, dilated pupils (cocaine, amfetamines). Opioid: coma, respiratory depression, pinpoint pupils. Sedative: coma with preserved pupils and breathing. Serotonergic: clonus, hyperreflexia, hyperthermia. Reading the toxidrome applies the receptor pharmacology of [autonomic](../03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/autonomic-and-cardiovascular-drugs.md) and [central nervous system drugs](../03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/central-nervous-system-drugs.md) in reverse.

4. **Decontamination and enhanced elimination work only when kinetics allow, and the kinetics are the ones already learned.** Activated charcoal adsorbs most organic molecules in the gut if given within about an hour, before absorption is complete, but not metals, alcohols or corrosives. Urinary alkalinisation traps weak acids (salicylate) in the ionised form in the tubule so they cannot be reabsorbed, the [ion-trapping](../01-pharmacokinetics/absorption-and-bioavailability.md) principle. Haemodialysis removes small, water-soluble, poorly protein-bound drugs with a small volume of distribution (lithium, salicylate, methanol, metformin) and does nothing for the lipid-soluble, tissue-bound majority (tricyclics, digoxin, calcium-channel blockers). Repeated-dose charcoal interrupts enterohepatic recycling for a few drugs. Each measure is the [elimination leaf](../01-pharmacokinetics/elimination-half-life-and-dosing.md) applied to a patient who has taken too much.

5. **Antidotes act by five mechanisms, and most poisonings have none.** Competitive antagonism at the receptor (naloxone for opioids, flumazenil for benzodiazepines, atropine for the muscarinic effects of organophosphates). Binding the toxin so it cannot act (chelators for lead, iron and mercury; antivenom; digoxin-specific antibody fragments; hydroxocobalamin for cyanide). Blocking a toxic metabolic pathway (fomepizole inhibits alcohol dehydrogenase so that methanol and ethylene glycol are not oxidised to formic and oxalic acids). Replenishing a depleted defence (acetylcysteine; methylene blue for methaemoglobinaemia; high-flow oxygen displacing carbon monoxide from haemoglobin by competition). Bypassing the blocked step (glucagon or high-dose insulin for beta-blocker and calcium-channel-blocker overdose, raising cardiac cAMP or substrate independently of the blocked receptor). For everything else, supportive care while the drug is cleared is the treatment, and it is usually sufficient, as [emergency medicine](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/04-emergency-medicine/poisoning-and-overdose.md) and [critical care](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/03-critical-care/organ-support-and-icu-syndromes.md) practise it.

6. **Cellular poisons attack energy production, and they kill by hypoxia without lack of oxygen.** Carbon monoxide binds haemoglobin with ~250 times the affinity of oxygen and shifts the dissociation curve, so oxygen carriage fails at a normal arterial oxygen tension; it also binds cytochrome oxidase. Cyanide blocks cytochrome oxidase directly, so the mitochondria cannot use the oxygen delivered, venous blood stays red and lactate rises. Methaemoglobin-forming oxidants (nitrites, some local anaesthetics) lock iron in the ferric state. Salicylate uncouples oxidative phosphorylation, producing heat and lactate alongside its respiratory stimulation; the resulting mixed respiratory alkalosis and metabolic acidosis is the textbook case of [acid–base](../../06-physiology/01-integrative-principles/acid-base-physiology.md) reasoning in poisoning, and the anion gap and osmolar gap are the laboratory clues to an unmeasured toxin.

7. **Chronic and environmental toxicity are the same mechanisms at low dose over long times, and they are found by epidemiology rather than at the bedside.** Lead inhibits haem synthesis and damages the developing brain with no threshold yet found; mercury and arsenic bind sulphydryl groups; asbestos fibres cause mesothelioma decades later; tobacco smoke's combination of carcinogens, carbon monoxide and nicotine is the largest single toxic exposure in human history. Occupational limits, biomonitoring (blood lead, urinary metabolites) and the recognition that susceptibility varies with age (the fetus and child are most sensitive), genetics and co-exposure are the content of [environmental and toxic injury](../../08-pathology/04-genetic-metabolic-and-environmental-disease/environmental-and-toxic-injury.md) and of [environmental health](../../15-medicine-in-society/03-populations-and-the-future/environmental-and-planetary-health.md). Toxicology also supplies drug development's preclinical safety studies, in [the previous leaf](from-target-to-approval.md).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Median lethal dose (LD50) | Dose killing half of an exposed population; a point on the quantal toxicity curve |
| Threshold and safety factor | Dose below which no effect is observed, divided by factors for variability to set an exposure limit |
| Toxidrome | A recognisable cluster of signs identifying the receptor system a poison acts on |
| Zero-order kinetics in overdose | Constant elimination rate when metabolism is saturated, so half-life rises with dose |
| Decontamination | Reducing absorption, mainly with activated charcoal within the first hour |
| Enhanced elimination | Urinary alkalinisation, haemodialysis or repeated charcoal for drugs whose kinetics permit it |
| Antidote | Agent reversing or preventing a toxic action by antagonism, binding, pathway blockade, replenishment or bypass |
| Anion gap and osmolar gap | Laboratory differences revealing unmeasured acids or osmoles, such as toxic alcohols |
| Cellular hypoxia | Failure of oxygen use despite adequate delivery, as with cyanide and carbon monoxide |
| Biomonitoring | Measuring a toxin or its metabolite in blood or urine to assess chronic exposure |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Natural substances are safer than synthetic ones."** Botulinum toxin, ricin, aconite and amatoxins are among the most lethal substances known; the dose and the mechanism decide, not the origin.
- **"Every poisoning has an antidote."** Only a few dozen do; most are treated by supporting breathing, circulation and the kidneys while the body eliminates the agent.
- **"A patient who feels well after an overdose is safe."** Paracetamol, iron, toxic alcohols, mushrooms and modified-release preparations have latent periods of hours to days before organ damage appears.
