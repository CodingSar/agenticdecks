## Essence

Endocrine drugs are the simplest class to reason about and the easiest to get wrong, because every hormone sits in a feedback loop. There are only four things a drug can do to an axis: replace a hormone that is missing, block a hormone that is excessive (at its synthesis, its release or its receptor), stimulate the gland, or exploit the loop itself by feeding a signal back to the controller. The single idea is that the body measures the hormone's effect and adjusts its own secretion, so giving a hormone suppresses the gland that makes it, blocking one lifts the brake on its controller, and the full consequences of any endocrine drug include what the loop does in response. Metabolic drugs act on the same loops through the enzymes and transporters of fuel handling.

## Core tenets

1. **Replacement therapy aims at physiological levels and is judged by the feedback signal, not the dose.** Levothyroxine for hypothyroidism is titrated until thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) returns to the reference range, because the pituitary is a better sensor of thyroid status than any dose table; its long half-life (~7 days) means weeks to steady state and allows once-daily dosing. Hydrocortisone for adrenal failure must mimic the diurnal rhythm and be increased in illness, because the lost gland would have done so. Insulin for type 1 diabetes replaces a hormone whose secretion normally tracks glucose minute by minute, so the replacement must be split into basal and meal-time components, and its [therapeutic index](../02-pharmacodynamics/dose-response-and-therapeutic-index.md) is narrow in both directions. Replacement is lifelong when the gland is destroyed, and it suppresses whatever residual function remains.


2. **Excess is blocked at synthesis, release or receptor, and the loop responds by raising the stimulus.** Thionamides (carbimazole, propylthiouracil) inhibit thyroid peroxidase and so hormone synthesis; stored hormone keeps levels up for weeks, so onset is slow, and the pituitary raises TSH as levels fall, enlarging the gland. Adrenal steroidogenesis inhibitors (metyrapone) block cortisol synthesis and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) rises in response. Receptor antagonists (spironolactone at the mineralocorticoid receptor; antiandrogens) block the effect while leaving, or raising, the level. Dopamine agonists (cabergoline) suppress prolactin because dopamine is prolactin's physiological inhibitor.


3. **Continuous stimulation of a pulsatile receptor switches it off, which turns an agonist into a functional antagonist.** Gonadotrophin-releasing hormone (GnRH) is secreted in pulses, and the pituitary responds only to pulses. A long-acting GnRH agonist (leuprorelin, goserelin) first stimulates and then, by desensitising and downregulating the receptor, abolishes luteinising hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone secretion and so gonadal steroid production. This is why the same molecule treats prostate cancer, endometriosis and precocious puberty, and why pulsatile GnRH by pump treats infertility while continuous GnRH causes it. It is the cleanest clinical instance of [receptor desensitisation](../02-pharmacodynamics/tolerance-dependence-and-withdrawal.md).


4. **Hormonal contraception and hormone therapy use the feedback loop directly.** Combined oestrogen–progestogen contraceptives hold the hypothalamus and pituitary under negative feedback so that the luteinising hormone surge, and hence ovulation, does not occur; progestogens also thicken cervical mucus and thin the endometrium. The oestrogen component raises hepatic synthesis of clotting factors, which is the mechanism of its venous thrombosis risk, and the lipid, blood-pressure and breast effects follow from the same receptor acting in other tissues. Menopausal hormone therapy replaces the lost oestrogen at lower doses with the same trade-offs. Selective oestrogen receptor modulators (tamoxifen, raloxifene) are agonists in bone and antagonists in breast, showing that a receptor's effect depends on the co-regulators present in each tissue.


5. **Drugs for type 2 diabetes act on distinct steps of glucose handling, and the mechanism predicts whether they can cause hypoglycaemia.** Metformin reduces hepatic glucose output, partly through adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and mitochondrial effects, and does not raise insulin, so it does not cause hypoglycaemia alone; its hazard is lactic acidosis when renal failure lets it accumulate. Sulfonylureas (gliclazide) close the beta-cell ATP-sensitive potassium channel and force insulin release regardless of glucose, so they can cause hypoglycaemia. Incretin-based drugs (glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) agonists such as semaglutide; dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors that prolong endogenous GLP-1) enhance insulin release only when glucose is high, slow gastric emptying and suppress appetite, hence both the low hypoglycaemia risk and the weight loss that has made them [obesity](../../12-organ-system-specialties/05-endocrinology/obesity-lipids-and-metabolic-syndrome.md) drugs. Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors (empagliflozin) make the kidney excrete glucose, lowering glucose, weight and blood pressure insulin-independently, with the osmotic and ketotic consequences that follow from losing ~70 g of glucose a day in urine, and with benefits in [heart failure](../../12-organ-system-specialties/01-cardiology/heart-failure.md) and kidney disease that were discovered in trials rather than predicted. Thiazolidinediones (pioglitazone) activate the nuclear receptor PPAR-gamma to improve insulin sensitivity at the cost of fluid retention.


6. **Bone drugs act on the remodelling cycle, and antiresorptives and anabolics work in opposite directions on the same cells.** Bisphosphonates (alendronic acid) bind hydroxyapatite and are taken up by osteoclasts, in which they inhibit farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase and trigger apoptosis; they persist in bone for years, and their rare complications (osteonecrosis of the jaw, atypical femoral fractures) follow from suppressing the remodelling that repairs microdamage. Denosumab is an antibody against RANK ligand, the signal that makes osteoclasts; its effect reverses rapidly when stopped, with rebound fracture risk. Teriparatide is a fragment of parathyroid hormone that, given intermittently, is anabolic, whereas the continuous elevation seen in hyperparathyroidism destroys bone: the pattern of the signal, not its level, decides the response, as in tenet 3. Vitamin D and calcium are substrates; calcimimetics (cinacalcet) sensitise the parathyroid calcium sensor.


## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Replacement therapy | Giving a missing hormone at physiological levels, titrated to the feedback signal |
| Negative feedback suppression | Reduction of a gland's own secretion when its hormone, or a drug mimicking it, is given |
| Thionamide | Inhibitor of thyroid peroxidase; blocks synthesis but not release of stored hormone |
| GnRH agonist paradox | Continuous agonist stimulation abolishes gonadotrophin secretion by desensitisation |
| Selective receptor modulator | Ligand that is agonist in some tissues and antagonist in others, depending on co-regulators |
| Incretin | Gut hormone (GLP-1, GIP) that augments glucose-dependent insulin release |
| ATP-sensitive potassium channel | Beta-cell sensor coupling glucose metabolism to insulin release; closed by sulfonylureas |
| SGLT2 | Proximal tubular glucose transporter; its inhibition causes glycosuria |
| Antiresorptive and anabolic | Bone drugs reducing osteoclast activity or stimulating osteoblast activity respectively |

## Common misconceptions

- **"More thyroid hormone makes a hypothyroid patient feel better."** Above replacement it suppresses TSH, causes atrial fibrillation and bone loss, and is thyrotoxicosis by prescription; the target is a normal TSH.
- **"A GnRH agonist stimulates the gonads."** Only for the first one to two weeks; continuous exposure then switches the axis off, which is the intended effect in prostate cancer.
- **"All diabetes drugs cause hypoglycaemia."** Only those that release or supply insulin independently of glucose do; metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors and incretin drugs alone rarely do.
