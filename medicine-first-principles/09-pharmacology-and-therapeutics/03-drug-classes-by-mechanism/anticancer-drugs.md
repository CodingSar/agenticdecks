## Essence

A cancer cell is a human cell, so there is no wall or ribosome to attack; the differences are quantitative (it divides more, repairs DNA worse, depends on particular signals) or specific to the mutations that made it. Anticancer drugs exploit those differences in three generations of logic. Cytotoxics damage DNA or the mitotic apparatus of any dividing cell and rely on cancer cells dividing more and recovering less than the marrow, gut and hair follicles that share the damage. Targeted drugs block the mutated or over-expressed protein on which a particular tumour depends. Hormonal and immune therapies remove a growth signal or release the immune system. The single idea is fractional kill: each dose kills a fixed proportion of a tumour population, not a fixed number, so cure demands repeated cycles timed to the recovery of normal tissue, and the survivors of each cycle are selected for resistance.

## Core tenets

1. **Cytotoxic drugs kill a constant fraction of cells per dose, so curing a large tumour needs many cycles.** Skipper's log-kill hypothesis: a dose that kills 99% of cells reduces 10¹² cells (about 1 kg) to 10¹⁰ and 10⁴ to 10², so the same treatment that cures microscopic disease leaves a visible tumour visibly present. Cycles are spaced to let marrow recover (typically two to four weeks), during which the tumour regrows; the net slope decides cure. This arithmetic is why adjuvant therapy after surgery, when the residual population is smallest, cures some cancers that the same drugs cannot cure when bulky.

2. **Cytotoxics act at defined points in the cell cycle, and the toxicity profile follows from the normal tissues that are dividing.** Antimetabolites (fluorouracil, methotrexate, cytarabine) block nucleotide synthesis or substitute for nucleotides in S phase. Alkylating agents (cyclophosphamide, cisplatin) cross-link DNA in any phase and cause breaks on replication. Topoisomerase inhibitors (doxorubicin, etoposide, irinotecan) trap the enzymes that relieve torsional strain and leave double-strand breaks. Antimitotics stabilise microtubules (paclitaxel) or prevent their assembly (vincristine), arresting cells in mitosis. All of them hit the marrow (neutropenia at the nadir, around day 10–14), the gut mucosa (mucositis, diarrhoea), hair follicles and gonads; each adds organ-specific damage (cisplatin to the kidney and ear, doxorubicin to the heart, vincristine to peripheral nerves) that limits cumulative dose.

3. **Selectivity of cytotoxics comes from defective checkpoints, not faster division alone.** A normal cell with DNA damage halts at a [checkpoint](../../03-cell-biology/02-the-cell-life-cycle/the-cell-cycle-and-its-checkpoints.md) and repairs; a cancer cell with mutant p53 or defective repair proceeds and dies. Tumours with homologous-recombination defects (BRCA mutations) are exquisitely sensitive to platinum and to poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors, which block the remaining repair pathway: synthetic lethality, in which two individually tolerable losses are jointly fatal. The rapidly dividing marrow survives because its checkpoints work.

4. **Targeted drugs block the driver on which the tumour has become dependent, and the response lasts as long as the dependence.** Oncogene addiction: a tumour driven by the BCR-ABL fusion kinase collapses when imatinib occupies its ATP site; one driven by an EGFR mutation responds to EGFR inhibitors; one over-expressing HER2 responds to trastuzumab; a BRAF-mutant melanoma to BRAF inhibitors. The target must be found by [molecular diagnostics](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/02-laboratory-medicine/histopathology-cytology-and-molecular-diagnostics.md) before the drug is chosen, which is the [companion diagnostic](../02-pharmacodynamics/pharmacogenomics-and-individual-variation.md) principle. Toxicity is on-target in normal tissues using the same pathway (skin rash with EGFR inhibitors, cardiac dysfunction with HER2 antibodies) rather than on dividing cells.

5. **Resistance is evolution within the patient, and its mechanisms are predictable from the drug's mechanism.** A tumour of 10⁹ cells with genomic instability contains cells with every single-point mutation; a drug removes the sensitive majority and the resistant clone expands, exactly as with [antimicrobials](antimicrobials.md). Cytotoxic resistance arises by efflux pumps (P-glycoprotein), enhanced repair and loss of apoptosis; targeted-drug resistance by a second mutation in the target (the T790M gatekeeper mutation in EGFR; T315I in BCR-ABL), amplification of the target, or activation of a bypass pathway. Combination therapy, as in tuberculosis, lowers the probability of pre-existing double resistance, and sequential targeted drugs are designed against the known escape mutations.

6. **Hormone-dependent tumours are treated by removing the hormone or blocking its receptor.** Breast cancers expressing the oestrogen receptor respond to receptor antagonists and modulators (tamoxifen) or to aromatase inhibitors (anastrozole) that stop peripheral oestrogen synthesis after the menopause; prostate cancer responds to androgen deprivation by gonadotrophin-releasing hormone analogues (which, by continuous stimulation, desensitise the pituitary, an instance of [tolerance](../02-pharmacodynamics/tolerance-dependence-and-withdrawal.md)) and to androgen receptor antagonists. The tumour eventually escapes by receptor mutation or ligand-independent signalling, but years of control are common, with the costs of hormone deficiency: osteoporosis, vasomotor symptoms, metabolic change.

7. **Immunotherapy treats the tumour by treating the immune system.** Checkpoint inhibitors (antibodies to PD-1, PD-L1 and CTLA-4) release T-cells that the tumour had silenced, producing durable remissions in melanoma, lung and renal cancers that no cytotoxic achieved, at the cost of autoimmunity. Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cells are the patient's own lymphocytes engineered to recognise a surface antigen (CD19 on B-cell leukaemias and lymphomas), an [advanced therapy](biologics-and-advanced-therapies.md). Response correlates with tumour mutational burden, because more mutations yield more neo-antigens for T-cells to see; this links treatment back to the [causes of cancer](../../08-pathology/03-neoplasia/causes-of-cancer.md).

8. **The therapeutic index of cytotoxics is near one, so dosing is pushed to the limit of normal-tissue tolerance and supported by rescue.** Doses are set by body surface area (a rough proxy for clearance, see [scale](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/scale-and-allometry.md)), escalated in trials to the maximum tolerated dose, and supported by granulocyte colony-stimulating factor to shorten neutropenia, antiemetics acting on 5-HT3 and NK1 receptors to control the vomiting that chemoreceptor-trigger-zone stimulation produces, and in the extreme by stem-cell rescue after marrow-ablative doses. The oncologist's calculus of harm and benefit is the [cancer care](../../12-organ-system-specialties/07-oncology/principles-of-cancer-care.md) leaf's subject.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Log-kill (fractional kill) | Each dose kills a fixed fraction of tumour cells, so cell number falls exponentially with cycles |
| Cell-cycle specificity | Whether a drug acts only in one phase (S-phase antimetabolites, M-phase antimitotics) or in any |
| Nadir | The lowest point of blood counts after a cycle, typically 10–14 days |
| Dose-limiting toxicity | The adverse effect that caps the dose, cumulative or per cycle |
| Oncogene addiction | Dependence of a tumour on one driver, making it vulnerable to a single targeted drug |
| Synthetic lethality | Lethality from losing two pathways together when losing either alone is survivable |
| Companion diagnostic | The molecular test that must be positive before a targeted drug is used |
| Gatekeeper mutation | Second mutation in a kinase that blocks drug binding without losing function |
| Immune checkpoint inhibitor | Antibody removing an inhibitory signal on T-cells so that they attack the tumour |
| Maximum tolerated dose | Highest dose with acceptable toxicity, found in phase I trials by escalation |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Chemotherapy works because cancer cells divide faster."** Many tumours divide more slowly than marrow or gut; selectivity comes mainly from the cancer cell's defective checkpoints and repair, which turn damage into death.
- **"Targeted therapy has no side effects."** Its toxicity is on-target in normal tissues that use the same pathway (skin, heart, gut), not on dividing cells; it is different, not absent.
- **"A complete response means cure."** Imaging detects about 10⁹ cells; a complete response can leave 10⁸ cells, which is why therapy continues after the scan is clear and why relapse follows.
