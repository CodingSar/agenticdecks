## Essence

Drugs that act on the brain and spinal cord do so at synapses and ion channels, the same sites that physiology uses to compute. A drug that enters the central nervous system (CNS) must be lipid-soluble enough to cross the blood–brain barrier and must then change one of a few things: the amount of a transmitter in the cleft (by blocking its reuptake or breakdown, or driving its release), the activity of its receptor (agonist, antagonist, allosteric modulator), or the excitability of the membrane itself (sodium, calcium or potassium channels). The single idea is the balance of excitation and inhibition: glutamate excites, gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) inhibits, and the monoamines (dopamine, noradrenaline, serotonin) and acetylcholine modulate. Sedatives, anaesthetics and anticonvulsants tilt the balance towards inhibition; stimulants, antidepressants and antipsychotics adjust the modulators; and because each transmitter serves many circuits, every CNS drug acts on circuits it was not meant for.

## Core tenets

1. **Enhancing GABA-A inhibition produces, in order of dose, anxiolysis, sedation, anticonvulsant effect, anaesthesia and respiratory depression.** The GABA-A receptor is a chloride channel. Benzodiazepines (diazepam) bind an allosteric site and increase the frequency of channel opening in the presence of GABA, so they have a ceiling and are rarely fatal alone; barbiturates and propofol increase the duration of opening and at high concentration open the channel directly, so they have no ceiling and can stop breathing. Ethanol and volatile anaesthetics act partly at the same receptor. The order of effects with rising dose is the same for all, which is why combining them is dangerous and why their [withdrawal](../02-pharmacodynamics/tolerance-dependence-and-withdrawal.md) is excitatory.

2. **General anaesthesia is a dose-dependent, reversible loss of consciousness produced by many unrelated molecules, which argues for a few shared targets.** Potency of volatile agents correlates with lipid solubility (the Meyer–Overton relation), but the modern view is that they act on specific proteins: GABA-A and glycine receptors (enhanced), two-pore potassium channels (opened) and N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors (blocked, the main target of ketamine and nitrous oxide). Onset and offset of inhaled agents follow their blood–gas partition coefficient; onset and offset of intravenous agents follow [redistribution](../01-pharmacokinetics/distribution-and-protein-binding.md). This is the pharmacology behind [general anaesthesia](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/general-and-regional-anaesthesia.md).

3. **Anticonvulsants reduce the spread of synchronous firing by one of three mechanisms.** Use-dependent sodium-channel block (phenytoin, carbamazepine, lamotrigine) binds the inactivated channel preferentially, so rapidly firing neurons are silenced while normal ones are spared. Enhanced GABA inhibition (benzodiazepines, valproate in part, vigabatrin by inhibiting GABA transaminase). Reduced excitation: T-type calcium-channel block in thalamic neurons (ethosuximide, for absence seizures), blockade of synaptic vesicle protein 2A (levetiracetam) or of glutamate receptors. The mechanism predicts the seizure type a drug helps and the type it can worsen, which [epilepsy](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/epilepsy.md) uses.

4. **Opioids mimic the endogenous peptides at mu receptors, inhibiting pain transmission at every level and breathing at the brainstem.** Mu-receptor activation (morphine, fentanyl) opens potassium channels and closes calcium channels, hyperpolarising neurons and reducing transmitter release in the dorsal horn, the periaqueductal grey and the medullary respiratory centre. Analgesia and respiratory depression come from the same receptor, so there is no opioid that separates them; partial agonists (buprenorphine) and antagonists (naloxone) are the [receptor theory](../02-pharmacodynamics/receptor-theory-agonists-and-antagonists.md) examples. Constipation (enteric mu receptors) and miosis show little tolerance; analgesia and respiratory depression show much.

5. **Monoamine drugs change the amount of dopamine, noradrenaline or serotonin in the cleft, and the clinical effect lags the chemical one by weeks.** Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (fluoxetine) and serotonin–noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors block the transporters within hours, but antidepressant effect takes two to six weeks, which implies that the therapeutic change is downstream adaptation (receptor desensitisation, synaptic plasticity, neurogenesis) rather than the transmitter level itself. Monoamine oxidase inhibitors block breakdown; tricyclics block reuptake and, through muscarinic, histamine and alpha-1 blockade, cause the side effects that limit them. Stimulants (amfetamine, methylphenidate) release or block reuptake of dopamine and noradrenaline. The "chemical imbalance" model is a mnemonic, not a mechanism, as [mood disorders](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/03-psychiatry/mood-and-anxiety-disorders.md) discusses.

6. **Antipsychotics are dopamine D2 antagonists, and their adverse effects map onto the four dopamine pathways.** Blocking D2 in the mesolimbic pathway reduces positive symptoms of [psychosis](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/03-psychiatry/psychosis.md); blocking it in the nigrostriatal pathway produces parkinsonism and, after years, tardive dyskinesia; blocking it in the tuberoinfundibular pathway raises prolactin; blocking it in the mesocortical pathway may worsen negative symptoms. "Atypical" agents (clozapine, olanzapine) add serotonin 5-HT2A antagonism and dissociate faster from D2, producing fewer motor effects but more weight gain and metabolic disturbance through histamine and serotonin receptors. Antiemetics (metoclopramide, domperidone) are D2 antagonists at the chemoreceptor trigger zone and share the motor and prolactin effects to the degree that they cross the blood–brain barrier.

7. **Neurodegenerative disease is treated by replacing or protecting the missing transmitter, which works until the neurons that make it are gone.** Parkinson's disease is treated with levodopa, the precursor that crosses the blood–brain barrier when dopamine cannot, given with a peripheral decarboxylase inhibitor (carbidopa) so that it is converted inside the brain; dopamine agonists and monoamine oxidase B inhibitors supplement it. Alzheimer's disease is treated with acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil) that raise acetylcholine in a shrinking cholinergic system. Both are symptomatic; neither slows the loss of neurons, which is why benefit fades, and why the [neurodegeneration](../../12-organ-system-specialties/10-neurology/neurodegenerative-disease.md) leaf separates symptom from disease modification.

8. **Local anaesthetics block voltage-gated sodium channels from inside the axon, so they must cross the membrane un-ionised and act ionised.** Lidocaine is a weak base; its un-ionised fraction crosses the nerve sheath and membrane, then the ionised form blocks the channel from the cytoplasmic side. In inflamed, acidic tissue less is un-ionised and the block fails. Small, myelinated and rapidly firing fibres (pain) are blocked before large motor fibres (use-dependence and diameter), which is the basis of [regional anaesthesia](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/general-and-regional-anaesthesia.md). Systemic absorption blocks the same channels in heart and brain, so toxicity is seizures and arrhythmia.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Blood–brain barrier | Tight-junctioned endothelium with efflux pumps; CNS drugs must be lipid-soluble, small and not pumped out |
| GABA-A receptor | Ligand-gated chloride channel; the main fast inhibitory receptor and target of sedatives and anaesthetics |
| NMDA receptor | Glutamate-gated calcium-permeable channel; target of ketamine and part of anaesthesia and memory |
| Use-dependent block | Preferential binding to channels that open frequently, sparing normally firing cells |
| Monoamine transporter | Presynaptic reuptake pump for serotonin, noradrenaline or dopamine; target of most antidepressants and stimulants |
| Dopamine pathways | Mesolimbic, mesocortical, nigrostriatal and tuberoinfundibular; each predicts one effect of D2 blockade |
| Minimum alveolar concentration | Concentration of inhaled anaesthetic preventing movement in 50% of patients; the quantal ED50 of anaesthesia |
| Mu opioid receptor | G-protein-coupled receptor mediating opioid analgesia, euphoria, respiratory depression and constipation |
| Extrapyramidal effects | Motor side effects (parkinsonism, dystonia, akathisia, tardive dyskinesia) of nigrostriatal D2 blockade |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Antidepressants work by correcting a serotonin deficiency."** The transporter is blocked within hours but benefit takes weeks; the therapeutic change is downstream plasticity, and low serotonin has not been shown to cause depression.
- **"Benzodiazepines and barbiturates are interchangeable sedatives."** Benzodiazepines need GABA to act and have a ceiling; barbiturates and propofol can open the channel alone and have none, which is the difference between a rarely fatal overdose and a fatal one.
- **"A local anaesthetic numbs by acting on the skin surface."** It must cross into the axon; that is why it fails in acidic infected tissue and why a topical preparation needs a lipid-soluble base.
