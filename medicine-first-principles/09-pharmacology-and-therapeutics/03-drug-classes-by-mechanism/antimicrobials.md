## Essence

An antimicrobial is a poison with a difference: it must kill or arrest a microbe inside a human while leaving the human alone. The design principle, named selective toxicity by Ehrlich, is to attack a structure or enzyme the pathogen has and the host lacks, or has in a sufficiently different form. Bacteria offer the peptidoglycan wall, a distinct ribosome and their own folate synthesis; fungi offer ergosterol; viruses offer polymerases and proteases of their own; parasites offer unique metabolic steps. The single idea that makes antimicrobials unlike every other drug class is that the target is a population of organisms that reproduces and evolves, so use of the drug selects for its own failure, and every antimicrobial is a shared and depleting resource.

## Core tenets

1. **Selective toxicity comes from targets the host does not have.** Beta-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems) acylate the transpeptidases that cross-link peptidoglycan, so the growing wall fails and the cell lyses under its own osmotic pressure; human cells have no wall. Glycopeptides (vancomycin) bind the D-alanyl-D-alanine terminus of the wall precursor. Aminoglycosides (gentamicin), tetracyclines and macrolides (clarithromycin) bind the bacterial 70S ribosome at sites that differ from the human 80S ribosome. Sulphonamides and trimethoprim block folate synthesis, which bacteria must perform and humans bypass by eating folate. Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin) trap DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, bacterial enzymes with human homologues different enough to spare. The narrower the difference, the greater the host toxicity: aminoglycosides also damage mitochondrial ribosomes (a bacterial relic) in the ear and kidney.

2. **Bactericidal and bacteriostatic are properties of a drug–organism pair, and the distinction matters most where the host cannot help.** Wall and DNA inhibitors generally kill; most protein-synthesis inhibitors only arrest growth and rely on the immune system to clear the organism. In a neutropenic patient, in endocarditis (bacteria shielded in a vegetation) and in meningitis (few phagocytes), a bactericidal drug is required. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) measured in the laboratory is the concentration that stops visible growth; it is an in-vitro number that must be related to the concentration the drug achieves at the site of infection.

3. **The shape of the concentration–time curve relative to the MIC decides how to dose.** Beta-lactams kill in proportion to the time the free concentration stays above the MIC, with little gain from higher peaks, so they are given often or by infusion. Aminoglycosides and fluoroquinolones kill in proportion to the peak-to-MIC ratio and show a post-antibiotic effect (growth stays suppressed after the drug has fallen), so a single large daily dose is both more effective and, for aminoglycosides, less toxic to the kidney. Vancomycin is governed by the area under the curve divided by MIC. This is the [pharmacokinetics](../01-pharmacokinetics/elimination-half-life-and-dosing.md) of the previous parent applied to a target that has its own threshold.

4. **The drug must reach the organism, and barriers and biofilms are where it fails.** Polar drugs (aminoglycosides, glycopeptides) do not cross the blood–brain barrier or enter cells; intracellular pathogens (Legionella, Chlamydia, Mycobacterium) need drugs that concentrate in cells (macrolides, fluoroquinolones, rifampicin). Abscesses have no blood supply and an acid, anaerobic interior that inactivates aminoglycosides; they need drainage. Bacteria on prosthetic material form biofilms in which growth is slow (so wall inhibitors have nothing to act on) and a polysaccharide matrix limits penetration, so infected implants usually need removal, a problem shared with [devices](../04-non-drug-therapeutics/devices-implants-and-biomaterials.md).

5. **Resistance arises by mutation or gene transfer, is selected by exposure and spreads between organisms and patients.** Mechanisms: enzymes that destroy the drug (beta-lactamases, now including carbapenemases), altered targets (penicillin-binding protein 2a in meticillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*; mutated gyrase), reduced entry or active efflux, and bypass pathways. Plasmids carry resistance genes between species, so resistance selected in the gut flora by a drug given for a chest infection can reach a later pathogen. The rate of emergence depends on the mutation rate, the population size and the selection pressure; this is [host–pathogen coevolution](../../04-genetics-evolution-and-development/02-evolution/host-pathogen-coevolution-and-resistance.md) on a timescale of months. Combination therapy works against tuberculosis and HIV because the probability of simultaneous resistance mutations to two or three drugs is the product of small numbers.

6. **Antivirals must target viral enzymes or entry, and they work best early, because the virus borrows most of its machinery from the cell.** Nucleoside analogues (aciclovir, phosphorylated only by the herpes thymidine kinase, so it is activated only in infected cells; the HIV reverse-transcriptase inhibitors; sofosbuvir for hepatitis C) terminate viral chain elongation. Protease inhibitors stop polyprotein cleavage; integrase inhibitors stop HIV inserting its genome; neuraminidase inhibitors stop influenza leaving the cell; entry inhibitors and monoclonal antibodies block attachment. Because viruses replicate before symptoms, antivirals shorten rather than abort most acute infections; for chronic infections (HIV, hepatitis B) they suppress replication for life, which is the premise of [HIV care](../../12-organ-system-specialties/08-infectious-diseases/hiv-and-chronic-viral-infections.md).

7. **Antifungals and antiparasitics face a eukaryotic target, so the margin is narrower.** Azoles (fluconazole) inhibit the synthesis of ergosterol, the fungal membrane sterol; polyenes (amphotericin B) bind it and punch holes, with the kidney as collateral because cholesterol is similar enough; echinocandins block synthesis of beta-glucan in the fungal wall, a structure humans lack, and so are the best tolerated. Antimalarials exploit the parasite's digestion of haemoglobin (chloroquine, artemisinins); antihelminthics paralyse the worm's muscle (praziquantel, ivermectin) or starve it (benzimidazoles on tubulin).

8. **Every antimicrobial course acts on the patient's microbiome as well as on the pathogen.** Broad-spectrum agents clear the colonic anaerobes that keep *Clostridioides difficile* in check, so colitis follows; they select resistant colonisers that cause the next infection; and they disturb a [microbiome](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/01-microbes-and-infection/the-human-microbiome.md) whose roles in metabolism and immunity are still being mapped. The cost of an antibiotic is paid by the patient and by everyone who shares their bacteria, which is the rationale for [stewardship](../../12-organ-system-specialties/08-infectious-diseases/antimicrobial-stewardship.md).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Selective toxicity | Harming the pathogen more than the host by targeting a structure the host lacks or has in different form |
| Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) | Lowest concentration preventing visible growth in vitro; the reference point for dosing and resistance breakpoints |
| Bactericidal / bacteriostatic | Killing versus arresting growth; depends on drug, organism and concentration |
| Time-dependent and concentration-dependent killing | Efficacy tracking time above MIC (beta-lactams) or peak-to-MIC ratio (aminoglycosides) |
| Post-antibiotic effect | Continued suppression of growth after the concentration falls below the MIC |
| Spectrum | The range of organisms a drug inhibits; broad spectrum costs more collateral damage to the microbiome |
| Beta-lactamase | Bacterial enzyme hydrolysing the beta-lactam ring; the commonest resistance mechanism, countered by inhibitors such as clavulanic acid |
| Biofilm | Structured community of slow-growing organisms in a matrix on a surface; tolerant to most antimicrobials |
| Combination therapy | Using several drugs so that resistance to all is improbable; standard for tuberculosis and HIV |
| Collateral damage | Resistance selection and microbiome disruption caused by treating one infection |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Stopping a course early breeds resistance."** Resistance is selected by exposure, so longer courses select more, not less; the right duration is the one that clears the infection, which trials have shown is often shorter than tradition.
- **"A broad-spectrum antibiotic is a safer choice when the organism is unknown."** It covers more pathogens at the cost of more collateral selection and *C. difficile* risk; the safer choice is to narrow as soon as the organism is known.
- **"Antibiotics are ineffective against viruses because viruses are too small."** They are ineffective because viruses have no wall, ribosome or folate pathway of their own; size is irrelevant.
