## Essence

A living system that is pushed in one direction pushes back. Give a drug continuously and the tissue adapts to its presence: receptors are removed or uncoupled, opposing pathways are upregulated, metabolism accelerates, so that the same dose produces less effect (tolerance). Those same adaptations are now holding the system in balance against the drug; take the drug away and the balance tips the other way, producing a withdrawal syndrome that is roughly the mirror image of the drug's acute effect (physical dependence). The single idea is homeostatic feedback: the drug has become part of the set point, and both tolerance and withdrawal are the control system doing its job. Addiction is a separate phenomenon, involving reward learning, and must not be confused with either.

## Core tenets

1. **Tolerance is a rightward shift of the dose–response curve produced by adaptation, and it has several mechanisms with different timescales.** Pharmacokinetic tolerance comes from enzyme induction (ethanol, barbiturates) and takes days to weeks. Pharmacodynamic tolerance comes from changes at or beyond the receptor: desensitisation (phosphorylation and arrestin binding uncouple the receptor within minutes), internalisation (receptors are pulled into the cell over hours) and downregulation (fewer receptors are made over days). Tachyphylaxis is rapid tolerance within minutes to hours, as when repeated doses of an indirectly acting sympathomimetic exhaust the transmitter store it releases.

2. **Tolerance is selective, not global, because adaptations are pathway-specific.** Opioid tolerance to analgesia and respiratory depression develops quickly, tolerance to constipation and miosis barely at all. The practical consequence is that a dose escalated for analgesia may be dangerous if tolerance is lost (after detoxification, or after a period in hospital without opioids), which is why overdose risk peaks after abstinence. Cross-tolerance between drugs acting at the same receptor (all opioids, benzodiazepines and ethanol at GABA-A) follows from the mechanism.

3. **Physical dependence is the state in which the adapted system needs the drug to stay in balance.** Chronic opioid exposure upregulates adenylyl cyclase and raises noradrenergic tone in the locus coeruleus to counter the drug's inhibition; remove the drug and the unopposed upregulation produces the sweating, tachycardia, diarrhoea and agitation of withdrawal, each the opposite of an acute opioid effect. Chronic depressants (ethanol, benzodiazepines) downregulate GABA-A and upregulate glutamate receptors; withdrawal is excitation, tremor, seizures and, in alcohol, delirium. Physical dependence occurs in any patient given the drug long enough and says nothing about addiction.

4. **Withdrawal's time course follows the drug's half-life.** A short-acting drug produces an early, intense withdrawal (heroin within hours; alprazolam within a day); a long-acting one a delayed, milder and prolonged syndrome (methadone, diazepam). This is the kinetic [exponential decay](../01-pharmacokinetics/elimination-half-life-and-dosing.md) unmasking the adaptation at a rate set by how fast concentration falls, and it is why substitution with a long-half-life agonist and gradual tapering reduces the severity of withdrawal: the adaptations reverse at their own slow pace while the concentration falls slowly enough to keep up.

5. **The same logic applies to non-abused drugs and to endogenous hormones.** Abrupt withdrawal of a beta-blocker after chronic use leaves upregulated beta-receptors exposed to normal catecholamines, producing rebound tachycardia and ischaemia. Long-term glucocorticoids suppress the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis by feedback; stopping them suddenly leaves an atrophied adrenal unable to respond, an [adrenal crisis](../../12-organ-system-specialties/05-endocrinology/adrenal-pituitary-and-gonadal-disorders.md). Proton pump inhibitors cause rebound acid hypersecretion. Any drug that suppresses a feedback-regulated system invites rebound on withdrawal.

6. **Sensitisation is the opposite adaptation and underlies some of addiction.** Repeated intermittent stimulant exposure increases, rather than decreases, some responses (locomotor activation, drug craving) through lasting changes in dopaminergic synapses. Tolerance to the euphoric effect and sensitisation of the incentive ("wanting") effect can coexist, which is one reason addiction persists after physical dependence has resolved.

7. **Addiction is a disorder of learning and motivation, distinct from tolerance and dependence.** It is compulsive use despite harm, driven by drug-induced plasticity in the mesolimbic dopamine pathway that assigns excessive salience to drug cues. A cancer patient on long-term morphine is tolerant and dependent but not addicted; a person using cocaine intermittently may be addicted with little physical dependence. Conflating the three leads to undertreated pain and to mislabelled patients; the clinical picture is in [addiction and substance use](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/03-psychiatry/addiction-and-substance-use.md).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Tolerance | Reduced response to a constant dose after repeated exposure; rightward shift of the dose–response curve |
| Tachyphylaxis | Tolerance developing within minutes to hours, often by depletion of a mediator |
| Desensitisation | Rapid uncoupling of a receptor from its effector by phosphorylation and arrestin binding |
| Downregulation | Reduction in receptor number by internalisation and reduced synthesis over days |
| Cross-tolerance | Tolerance to one drug conferring tolerance to another acting at the same target |
| Physical dependence | Adapted state in which removing the drug causes a withdrawal syndrome |
| Withdrawal (abstinence) syndrome | Signs opposite to the drug's acute effects, with intensity and timing set by the drug's half-life |
| Rebound | Transient overshoot of a suppressed function after a drug is stopped |
| Sensitisation | Increased response to a drug after repeated intermittent exposure |
| Addiction | Compulsive drug use despite harm; a disorder of reward learning distinct from dependence |

## How it fits

- **Below:** The adaptations are the [termination mechanisms](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/04-cell-signalling/signal-amplification-integration-and-termination.md) of cell signalling run to their limit; the logic is [negative feedback](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/03-systems-and-models/feedback-and-control.md) with the drug inside the loop; the synapses are those of [neurotransmission](../../06-physiology/02-nervous-system/synaptic-transmission-and-neurotransmitters.md). The assumption is that the regulated system has reserve to adapt; when it does not (a fully occupied enzyme), no tolerance develops.
- **Above:** Opioid and sedative prescribing in [palliative care](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/palliative-care-and-symptom-control.md), [anaesthesia](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/general-and-regional-anaesthesia.md) and [psychiatry](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/03-psychiatry/addiction-and-substance-use.md) all manage these adaptations; steroid tapering in every specialty is tenet 5 applied.
- **Siblings:** [Receptor theory](receptor-theory-agonists-and-antagonists.md) gives the static curve, this leaf its drift over time; the kinetic form of tolerance is [enzyme induction](../01-pharmacokinetics/drug-metabolism-and-interactions.md); withdrawal and rebound are a class of [adverse reaction](adverse-drug-reactions-and-pharmacovigilance.md) caused by stopping, not starting, a drug.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A patient who needs rising doses of opioid is becoming addicted."** Rising need usually reflects tolerance or progressing disease; addiction is defined by compulsive use despite harm, not by dose.
- **"Withdrawal proves the drug was being abused."** Physical dependence follows chronic exposure to many ordinary drugs (beta-blockers, corticosteroids, antidepressants) with no abuse involved.
- **"Tolerance to a drug is uniform across its effects."** It is pathway-specific: opioid tolerance spares constipation; benzodiazepine tolerance develops to sedation faster than to anxiolysis.
