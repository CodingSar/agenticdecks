## Essence

Ionising radiation kills cells by breaking DNA, and radiotherapy is the art of breaking enough DNA in a tumour while sparing the normal tissue around it. Unlike a drug, radiation can be aimed: a beam deposits energy along its path and the dose can be shaped in three dimensions, so selectivity is geometric before it is biological. The biological selectivity comes from the four Rs of radiobiology: normal cells repair sublethal damage better than tumour cells, cells redistribute through the cell cycle between doses, hypoxic tumour cells reoxygenate, and normal tissue repopulates. Fractionation, giving the dose in many small daily parts, exploits all four. The single idea is that radiotherapy has a dose–response curve for tumour control and another for normal-tissue complication, and everything in the field is an attempt to pull the two curves apart.

## Core tenets

1. **Radiation damages DNA directly and, mostly, indirectly through water radiolysis.** A photon or charged particle ionises atoms along its track. About a third of the damage comes from direct ionisation of DNA; two thirds from hydroxyl radicals produced when water is split, which attack DNA within nanometres of where they form. The lethal lesion is the double-strand break, which the cell either repairs by non-homologous end-joining or homologous recombination, or fails to repair and dies, usually at the next mitosis. Oxygen fixes the radical damage into permanent chemical change, which is why hypoxic cells are two to three times more resistant (the oxygen enhancement ratio). Dose is measured in gray (Gy), one joule per kilogram; a typical curative course is 60–70 Gy, enough to raise the tissue temperature by a thousandth of a degree.

2. **Cell survival falls exponentially with dose after an initial shoulder, and the shoulder is repair.** The linear-quadratic model, survival = exp(−αD − βD²), describes the fraction surviving a dose D. The linear term α is lethal damage from single tracks; the quadratic term β is lethal damage from two separate sublethal hits that combine, and it can be repaired if the hits are separated in time. The ratio α/β measures how much a tissue depends on the quadratic term: late-responding normal tissues (spinal cord, lung, kidney) have a low α/β (~2–3 Gy) and are spared by small fractions; most tumours and early-responding tissues (skin, mucosa, marrow) have a high α/β (~10 Gy) and are not. This asymmetry is the quantitative basis of fractionation.

3. **Fractionation spares late-responding normal tissue more than it spares the tumour.** Giving 60 Gy in 30 fractions of 2 Gy rather than one dose lets normal cells repair sublethal damage between fractions and repopulate, while the tumour, with its worse repair, poorer repopulation and reoxygenation of previously hypoxic cells, gains less. The cost is time: a course of six to seven weeks, during which a fast-growing tumour repopulates, so some regimens accelerate. Hypofractionation (fewer, larger fractions) became safe when imaging and beam-shaping made the high-dose volume small enough that little normal tissue received it; stereotactic radiotherapy delivers ablative doses in one to five fractions on that principle.

4. **The physics of the beam decides where the dose goes.** Megavoltage photons from a linear accelerator deposit maximum dose a few centimetres below the skin (skin-sparing) and attenuate exponentially; multiple beams from different angles are summed so that the tumour receives their intersection and each skin entry receives a fraction. Intensity-modulated radiotherapy shapes each beam's fluence to carve the dose around a critical organ. Protons stop at a depth fixed by their energy (the Bragg peak) and deposit most of their dose just before stopping, with none beyond, which matters when a tumour sits in front of a critical structure or in a child. Brachytherapy places a sealed source in or beside the tumour so that the inverse-square law does the sparing. Radionuclide therapy (iodine-131 for thyroid, lutetium-177 bound to a peptide for neuroendocrine tumours) is radiation targeted by chemistry rather than geometry, and overlaps with [nuclear medicine](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/03-imaging-and-physiological-measurement/nuclear-medicine-and-molecular-imaging.md).

5. **Normal-tissue toxicity divides into early and late, and they have different mechanisms and different reversibility.** Early effects (mucositis, dermatitis, diarrhoea, marrow suppression) appear during or just after treatment in rapidly renewing tissues as the stem-cell compartment is depleted, and they heal when it recovers. Late effects (fibrosis, vascular damage, necrosis, organ failure) appear months to years later in slowly renewing tissues through damage to the vasculature and the stroma, and they are largely irreversible and progressive. The tolerance dose of each organ (the dose giving ~5% complication at five years, for example ~45–50 Gy to the spinal cord) is the constraint around which every treatment plan is built. A second malignancy induced by radiation is the ultimate late effect, with a latency of a decade or more, and is the link to [causes of cancer](../../08-pathology/03-neoplasia/causes-of-cancer.md).

6. **Radiotherapy is combined with drugs that sensitise the tumour or treat distant disease, and the two toxicities add.** Concurrent cisplatin or fluorouracil radiosensitise by inhibiting repair of radiation damage and killing cells in the resistant phases of the cycle; the gain in tumour control in head and neck, cervical and rectal cancer is paid for in mucosal toxicity. Hormone deprivation before and during prostate radiotherapy shrinks the target and sensitises it. Radiotherapy also appears to release tumour antigens and can, rarely, provoke immune responses at distant sites (the abscopal effect), now studied with checkpoint inhibitors. These combinations belong to [cancer care](../../12-organ-system-specialties/07-oncology/principles-of-cancer-care.md).

7. **The same biology sets radiation protection for diagnostic imaging and the environment.** Deterministic effects (skin burns, cataract, marrow failure) have thresholds and follow the survival curves above; stochastic effects (cancer, heritable mutation) are assumed to have no threshold, with risk proportional to dose, the linear no-threshold model on which protection limits rest. Effective dose in sievert (Sv) weights absorbed dose by radiation type and tissue sensitivity; a chest radiograph gives ~0.02 mSv, a computed tomography scan of the abdomen ~10 mSv, natural background ~2–3 mSv per year, and the excess lifetime cancer risk from a 10 mSv exposure is of the order of 1 in 2,000. The principle is to keep every exposure as low as reasonably achievable, which governs [imaging](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/03-imaging-and-physiological-measurement/radiography-and-computed-tomography.md), occupational limits and [environmental health](../../15-medicine-in-society/03-populations-and-the-future/environmental-and-planetary-health.md).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Gray (Gy) and sievert (Sv) | Absorbed dose (J/kg) and effective dose weighted for radiation type and tissue |
| Double-strand break | The lethal DNA lesion; repaired by end-joining or homologous recombination or fatal at mitosis |
| Oxygen enhancement ratio | Dose needed under hypoxia divided by dose needed with oxygen for the same effect, ~2.5–3 |
| Linear-quadratic model | Survival = exp(−αD − βD²); the α/β ratio measures a tissue's sensitivity to fraction size |
| The four Rs | Repair, redistribution, reoxygenation, repopulation: why fractionation works |
| Fractionation and hypofractionation | Dividing the dose into many small or fewer large daily parts |
| Therapeutic ratio | Separation between the tumour-control and normal-tissue-complication curves |
| Bragg peak | Depth at which a proton deposits most of its energy, with no dose beyond |
| Brachytherapy | Radiation from a sealed source placed in or next to the tumour |
| Early and late effects | Toxicity in rapidly renewing tissue during treatment versus fibrosis and vascular damage years later |
| Linear no-threshold model | Assumption that cancer risk is proportional to dose with no safe threshold, used for protection |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Radiotherapy burns the tumour away."** The energy deposited is trivial as heat; cells die because their DNA is broken and they fail at the next division, which is why tumours shrink over weeks, not during treatment.
- **"Spreading the dose over weeks is only to make it tolerable."** Fractionation changes the biology: it exploits the difference in repair capacity and α/β between tumour and late-responding normal tissue, and it allows reoxygenation of resistant hypoxic cells.
- **"Diagnostic radiation is harmless because the dose is small."** It is small but not zero; the linear no-threshold model assigns a small cancer risk to every scan, which is why each must be justified.
