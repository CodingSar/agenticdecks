## Essence

Surgery is controlled injury. The surgeon cuts to remove what is diseased, reconnect what is separated, relieve what is obstructed or replace what is lost, and then depends on the body to heal the wound that this leaves. Every surgical principle follows from two facts: tissue survives only while it is perfused, and a wound heals only by the sequence of inflammation, proliferation and remodelling that pathology describes. Technique exists to preserve blood supply, avoid dead space and contamination, bring tissue edges together without tension, and give the healing process what it needs; complications are the failure of one of these. The single idea is that the surgeon does not heal the patient; the surgeon arranges conditions under which the patient heals.

## Core tenets

1. **A wound heals by primary, delayed primary or secondary intention, and the choice is dictated by contamination and tissue loss.** A clean incision with edges apposed heals by primary intention: a thin scar in days, with epithelium bridging the gap within 24–48 hours. A wound that cannot be closed (tissue loss) or should not be (heavy contamination) heals by secondary intention: granulation tissue fills the defect, myofibroblasts contract it, and epithelium migrates across over weeks, leaving a broad scar. Delayed primary closure cleans a contaminated wound by open dressing for a few days and then sutures it. The [repair sequence](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/tissue-repair-regeneration-and-fibrosis.md) is the same; what differs is the volume of tissue it must make.

2. **Healing needs perfusion, oxygen, nutrition and the absence of infection and tension, and most wound failures are a deficit in one of them.** Fibroblasts synthesise collagen only above a tissue oxygen tension of ~20–30 mmHg, which is why ischaemic limbs, irradiated tissue, diabetic microangiopathy and smoking (vasoconstriction and carbon monoxide) impair healing. Collagen cross-linking needs vitamin C; protein depletion and zinc deficiency slow synthesis; glucocorticoids suppress the inflammatory phase that initiates repair. Tension on a suture line strangulates the capillaries at the edge. Wound strength is a fraction of normal at one week, roughly half at a month and never fully returns, which sets the timetable for activity after surgery and the risk period for dehiscence and hernia.

3. **Haemostasis and asepsis are the two inventions that made surgery survivable, and both are applied physiology.** Bleeding is controlled by pressure, ligature, diathermy (which coagulates protein by heat) and the patient's own [coagulation](../../06-physiology/08-blood/haemostasis-and-coagulation.md), which is why a bleeding tendency must be known before the first cut. Lister's antisepsis (1867) and the later aseptic technique reduced infection by keeping organisms out rather than killing them in the wound; skin preparation, sterile instruments, theatre air flow and prophylactic antibiotics given before the incision (so that tissue levels are present when contamination occurs) each remove one source. Surgical site infection rates still range from ~1–2% for clean to over 10% for dirty wounds, by the contamination class of the operation.

4. **Dead space, foreign material and dead tissue are what infection needs, so surgery removes them.** Bacteria in a well-perfused wound are reached by neutrophils and antibiotics; bacteria in a haematoma, a seroma, a cavity or on a suture are not. Debridement removes devitalised tissue that cannot defend itself; drains evacuate collections; irrigation reduces the inoculum; the choice of suture (absorbable or not, monofilament or braided) trades strength against the surface a biofilm can form on. The number of organisms needed to infect a wound falls from ~10⁵ per gram of tissue to a few hundred when a foreign body is present, which links to [devices](devices-implants-and-biomaterials.md).

5. **Anatomy is the map, and planes, blood supply and lymphatic drainage decide what can be removed and how.** Operations follow fascial planes because they are bloodless and because fascia marks what is contained with what; a tumour's resection margin follows the lymphatic drainage because that is where it spreads; an organ can be removed only if the structures beyond it keep their arterial supply and venous return. [Compartments](../../05-tissues-and-anatomy/02-body-organization/body-cavities-fascia-and-compartments.md) also explain the emergencies: swelling inside a closed fascial compartment raises pressure above capillary pressure and the muscle dies unless the fascia is cut.

6. **The body's response to surgery is the stress response to injury, and it is proportional to the magnitude of the insult.** Cortisol, catecholamines, glucagon and cytokines rise; sodium and water are retained; protein is catabolised; glucose rises; coagulation is activated. This response fuels healing and defends blood volume, but it also drives the postoperative complications of hyperglycaemia, fluid overload, venous thrombosis and, in the frail, decompensation of a heart or kidney with no reserve. Minimally invasive and laparoscopic techniques work largely by shrinking the injury and therefore the response, which is why they shorten recovery; the perioperative half of the story is in [perioperative risk](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/02-anaesthesia-and-perioperative-medicine/perioperative-risk-and-complications.md).

7. **Chronic wounds are wounds in which one of the healing conditions is persistently absent.** Venous ulcers fail because venous hypertension keeps the tissue oedematous and hypoxic; arterial ulcers because there is no inflow; pressure ulcers because sustained pressure above capillary pressure (~32 mmHg) over a bony prominence infarcts the skin; diabetic foot ulcers because neuropathy removes the warning of pressure and microangiopathy and infection prevent repair. The treatment in each case is to restore the missing condition (compression, revascularisation, offloading, debridement and glycaemic control) rather than to dress the wound, and the wound bed's appearance (granulating, sloughy, necrotic) reports which phase of repair is stalled.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Primary, secondary and delayed primary intention | Healing of an apposed wound, of an open wound by granulation, or of a wound closed after a period open |
| Granulation tissue | New capillaries, fibroblasts and inflammatory cells filling a wound defect |
| Dehiscence | Separation of a wound's layers, from failure of healing or excess tension |
| Debridement | Removal of dead or contaminated tissue to leave a wound that can heal |
| Asepsis | Prevention of contamination by excluding organisms, as distinct from antisepsis, which kills them |
| Wound contamination class | Clean, clean-contaminated, contaminated, dirty; predicts infection risk |
| Dead space | A cavity in a wound where fluid collects and bacteria escape host defence |
| Fascial plane | Bloodless anatomical layer followed by dissection; also a boundary that contains infection or pressure |
| Surgical stress response | Neuroendocrine and inflammatory response to tissue injury, scaled to its magnitude |
| Compartment syndrome | Pressure in a closed fascial space exceeding capillary pressure, causing ischaemia |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Antibiotics prevent wound infection."** Prophylaxis helps only when the drug is in the tissue at the moment of contamination; given after the wound is closed, it adds resistance and little protection. Asepsis, perfusion and the absence of dead space do most of the work.
- **"A wound should be kept dry to heal."** Epithelial cells migrate faster across a moist surface; dressings are designed to keep the wound bed moist and the surroundings dry.
- **"A healed wound regains full strength."** Scar reaches ~70–80% of the original tensile strength and never more, because collagen in a scar is less organised than in uninjured tissue.
