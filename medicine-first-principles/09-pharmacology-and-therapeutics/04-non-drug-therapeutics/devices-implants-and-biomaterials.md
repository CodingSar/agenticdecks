## Essence

A device does mechanically, electrically or structurally what a drug cannot: it bears load, holds a lumen open, replaces a valve, paces a heart, filters blood or converts sound into nerve impulses. Where a drug is judged by receptor pharmacology, a device is judged by engineering (strength, fatigue, flow, signal) and by one biological fact that governs every object placed in the body: tissue recognises a foreign surface and responds with protein adsorption, inflammation, encapsulation and, if bacteria arrive, a biofilm that the immune system and antibiotics cannot clear. The single idea is that a successful implant matches the mechanical and electrical properties of the tissue it replaces while presenting a surface the body tolerates, and most device failures are either a mismatch of properties or a loss of that tolerance.

## Core tenets

1. **The foreign-body reaction is the body's standard reply to any implant.** Within seconds plasma proteins adsorb to the surface; neutrophils and then macrophages arrive; macrophages that cannot engulf the object fuse into foreign-body giant cells; fibroblasts wall it off in a fibrous capsule. This is [chronic inflammation](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/chronic-inflammation-and-granulomas.md) with an indigestible stimulus. Thick capsules distort breast implants and insulate electrodes; the goal of biomaterial design is a thin, quiet capsule or, for bone implants, direct integration with no capsule at all.

2. **Biocompatibility is a property of the material–site pair, not of the material.** Titanium forms a stable oxide and bone grows onto it (osseointegration); cobalt–chromium alloys resist wear in bearing surfaces; polyethylene bears load against metal but sheds wear particles that macrophages engulf, provoking the osteolysis that loosens joint replacements after a decade or more; silicone is inert in bulk but its gel provokes reaction when it leaks; expanded polytetrafluoroethylene makes vascular grafts that stay open because its pores admit tissue. Degradable polymers (polylactide sutures, resorbable stents) are designed to be removed by hydrolysis once their job is done. A material tolerated in bone may fail in blood and the reverse.

3. **Blood is the hardest tissue to put a surface in, because it is designed to clot on foreign surfaces.** Adsorbed fibrinogen activates platelets and the contact pathway of [coagulation](../../06-physiology/08-blood/haemostasis-and-coagulation.md); every vascular catheter, stent, mechanical valve and extracorporeal circuit is therefore thrombogenic. Mechanical heart valves demand lifelong anticoagulation; biological valves do not but wear out; coronary stents need antiplatelet drugs until an endothelial layer covers the struts, and drug-eluting stents release an antiproliferative (sirolimus analogues) to stop the smooth-muscle overgrowth that restenosed bare stents. Dialysis and bypass circuits are heparinised. Haemocompatibility is the limiting problem for every blood-contacting device.

4. **Infection on a device is a biofilm, and biofilms are cured by removal, not by antibiotics.** Bacteria adhering to a surface switch to a slow-growing community in a polysaccharide matrix, which neutrophils cannot penetrate and in which antibiotic targets are inactive; the inoculum needed to infect falls by orders of magnitude when a foreign body is present. Catheter-associated bloodstream and urinary infections, prosthetic valve endocarditis and prosthetic joint infection are the main [healthcare-associated infections](../../12-organ-system-specialties/08-infectious-diseases/infection-control-and-healthcare-associated-infection.md), and all are treated by removing or exchanging the device when they can be. Prevention is asepsis at insertion, minimal duration, and surfaces that resist adhesion.

5. **Load-bearing implants must match the stiffness and fatigue life of the skeleton, and mismatch causes failure at the interface.** Bone remodels to the load it carries; a stiff metal plate takes the load and the bone beneath it resorbs (stress shielding), while a plate too flexible allows motion that prevents union. Every implant cycles under load with each step or heartbeat, so fatigue (crack growth from millions of sub-threshold cycles) rather than static strength sets its life: a hip prosthesis sees around a million cycles a year. Wear particles and corrosion at modular junctions are the biological consequence of mechanics. The [biomechanics](../../05-tissues-and-anatomy/02-body-organization/biomechanics-of-the-musculoskeletal-system.md) of the joint being replaced dictates the design, as [orthopaedics](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/orthopaedics-fractures-and-joints.md) shows.

6. **Electrical devices talk to excitable tissue in its own currency, and their limits are those of the electrode–tissue interface.** A pacemaker delivers a pulse above the myocardial threshold and senses intrinsic depolarisation to avoid competing with it; an implantable defibrillator detects fibrillation and delivers a shock that depolarises enough myocardium at once to extinguish re-entry; a cochlear implant bypasses dead hair cells and stimulates the auditory nerve along its tonotopic axis; deep-brain stimulators and spinal cord stimulators impose high-frequency pulses that disrupt pathological firing. Each depends on the threshold behaviour of [excitable membranes](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/electricity-and-excitable-membranes.md); each is degraded by the fibrous capsule that raises the threshold over time, by lead fracture under cyclical bending, and by battery life.

7. **Extracorporeal and support devices replace an organ's function by reproducing its physics.** Dialysis is diffusion and ultrafiltration across a synthetic membrane down gradients the kidney would have made; a ventilator moves gas by applying pressure that the diaphragm would have applied; extracorporeal membrane oxygenation is gas exchange across a membrane with a pump for a heart; a ventricular assist device is a continuous-flow pump. Each reproduces one function of the organ, none reproduces its regulation, so the clinician becomes the feedback loop, which is the daily work of [organ support in intensive care](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/03-critical-care/organ-support-and-icu-syndromes.md).

8. **Devices are regulated, tested and fail differently from drugs.** A drug is one molecule given to thousands in a trial; a device is iterated, depends on the operator, and often reaches patients with mechanical bench data and small series rather than randomised trials, because blinding is hard and designs change faster than trials run. Failures appear as late mechanical events (lead fractures, valve strut failures, metal-on-metal hip debris) found by registries rather than by trials, so joint and device registries are to implants what [pharmacovigilance](../02-pharmacodynamics/adverse-drug-reactions-and-pharmacovigilance.md) is to drugs. The surgeon's learning curve is part of the device's performance.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Biomaterial | Any natural or synthetic material designed to interact with living tissue in a device |
| Foreign-body reaction | Protein adsorption, macrophage and giant-cell response and fibrous encapsulation of an implant |
| Osseointegration | Direct structural bonding of bone to an implant surface without a fibrous layer |
| Haemocompatibility | A surface's resistance to activating platelets and coagulation |
| Biofilm | Adherent, matrix-embedded bacterial community on a surface; antibiotic-tolerant |
| Stress shielding | Bone resorption where an implant carries the load the bone would have carried |
| Fatigue failure | Fracture from crack growth under repeated loads below the static strength |
| Restenosis | Renarrowing of a stented vessel by smooth-muscle proliferation |
| Capture threshold | Minimum stimulus that reliably depolarises excitable tissue through an electrode |
| Device registry | Prospective record of all implants of a type, used to detect late failure |

## Common misconceptions

- **"An inert material produces no tissue reaction."** Every implant provokes protein adsorption and encapsulation; "inert" means the reaction is mild and stable, not absent.
- **"A device infection can be sterilised with a long antibiotic course."** Biofilm organisms are tolerant to concentrations hundreds of times the laboratory MIC; cure almost always requires removal.
- **"The strongest implant is the best."** A plate stiffer than bone unloads and weakens it; the aim is to match the tissue, not to exceed it.
