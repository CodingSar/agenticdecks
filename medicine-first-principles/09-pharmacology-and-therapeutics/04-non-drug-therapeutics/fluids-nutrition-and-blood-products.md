## Essence

Water, salt, fuel and blood are the oldest therapies and the ones most often given carelessly, because they feel like replacement rather than treatment. They are drugs: each has a volume of distribution set by the compartment it enters, a dose–response curve, a therapeutic window and characteristic toxicities, and each is given to correct a deficit that the body's own regulation could not. The single idea is that a replacement fluid or nutrient goes where its physical chemistry sends it: water follows osmoles, sodium stays extracellular, glucose solutions become water once the glucose is metabolised, colloids and red cells stay in the vessel while the endothelium is intact, and nothing stays where it is put when the barrier is damaged. Knowing the compartment is knowing the dose.

## Core tenets

1. **A fluid distributes into the compartment whose composition it matches.** Total body water (~42 L in a 70 kg adult) is two thirds intracellular and one third extracellular, of which a quarter (~3 L) is plasma. Five percent glucose is water once the glucose is taken up, so it distributes through all 42 L and raises plasma volume by only ~1/14 of what was given; sodium chloride 0.9% stays in the extracellular 14 L, so a quarter of it stays in plasma; a colloid (albumin) stays in the 3 L of plasma while capillaries hold protein. Red cells stay in the vessel by size. Choosing a fluid is choosing the compartment one wants to fill, which follows directly from [fluid compartments](../../06-physiology/01-integrative-principles/body-fluid-compartments-and-electrolytes.md).

2. **Resuscitation fluid works by raising stroke volume through preload, and only while the heart can use it.** On the steep part of the ventricular function curve, volume raises cardiac output; on the flat part it raises only venous and capillary pressure and produces oedema. Whether a patient is on the steep part is tested by giving fluid and measuring the response, not by a formula. Large volumes of saline cause a hyperchloraemic acidosis because chloride displaces bicarbonate; balanced crystalloids avoid this. Colloids expand plasma more per litre but leak when the endothelium is inflamed, and starches damaged kidneys in trials; albumin has not been shown to improve survival over crystalloid in most settings. The physiology is [capillary exchange](../../06-physiology/03-cardiovascular-system/microcirculation-and-capillary-exchange.md) and the clinical use is [shock](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/03-critical-care/shock-and-haemodynamic-support.md).

3. **Fluid has a toxicity, and it is oedema.** Every litre given beyond what is lost raises interstitial volume; in the lung it lengthens the diffusion path and in the gut it slows motility and absorption; in the kidney, raised venous pressure lowers filtration. Positive fluid balance in the critically ill is associated with worse outcome in observational and some randomised data, and the hormonal response to illness and surgery (antidiuretic hormone and aldosterone) retains what is given. Maintenance fluid is a prescription with a dose: roughly 25–30 mL/kg/day of water, ~1 mmol/kg/day of sodium and potassium, and enough glucose to limit ketosis, adjusted to losses. [Oedema](../../08-pathology/02-hemodynamic-disorders/edema-and-fluid-imbalance.md) is the adverse reaction.

4. **Sodium disorders are water disorders, and the speed of correction is bounded by the brain.** Plasma sodium concentration reports the ratio of extracellular sodium to water; hyponatraemia is usually water excess from antidiuretic hormone that illness, drugs or pain have released, not sodium loss. The brain adapts to a changed osmolality over about 48 hours by losing or gaining osmoles, so correcting chronic hyponatraemia faster than ~8–10 mmol/L per day shrinks adapted cells and can demyelinate the pons, and correcting hypernatraemia too fast swells them. Potassium is the mirror problem: 98% is intracellular, so plasma potassium is a poor measure of total potassium, and shifts across the membrane (insulin, acidosis, beta-agonists) change it within minutes. [Electrolyte disorders](../../12-organ-system-specialties/03-nephrology/electrolyte-and-acid-base-disorders.md) apply these rules.

5. **Nutrition support is replacement of fuel and substrate, and the route and the rate are the pharmacology.** A resting adult needs ~25 kcal/kg/day and ~1 g/kg/day of protein, more in sepsis, burns and trauma where catabolism breaks down muscle for gluconeogenesis and acute-phase protein synthesis. Enteral feeding preserves the [gut barrier](../../06-physiology/06-gastrointestinal-system/the-gut-barrier-and-microbiome.md) and its immune function and is preferred whenever the gut works; parenteral nutrition bypasses a gut that does not, at the cost of line infection, liver dysfunction and metabolic complications. The malnourished patient fed too fast develops refeeding syndrome: insulin surges drive phosphate, potassium and magnesium into cells and thiamine is consumed, with cardiac failure and arrhythmia as the result. Starvation and overfeeding both have a dose–response, as [nutritional disease](../../08-pathology/04-genetic-metabolic-and-environmental-disease/nutritional-disease-obesity-and-malnutrition.md) describes.

6. **Blood is given as components, each replacing one function, and each is a transplant with its own immunology.** Red cells restore oxygen-carrying capacity, and the trigger for giving them is the balance of delivery and demand, not a number; restrictive thresholds have proved as safe as liberal ones in most trials. Platelets stop bleeding from thrombocytopenia; plasma supplies clotting factors; cryoprecipitate concentrates fibrinogen. Compatibility rests on [blood groups](../../06-physiology/08-blood/blood-groups-and-transfusion-compatibility.md): ABO-incompatible red cells are lysed by pre-formed antibodies within minutes, a fatal reaction; other antigens cause delayed haemolysis after sensitisation. Transfused leukocytes and plasma cause febrile reactions, allergic reactions, transfusion-related acute lung injury (donor antibodies against recipient neutrophils) and, in the immunosuppressed, graft-versus-host disease, which irradiation of the product prevents. Stored blood carries potassium, citrate (which binds calcium) and a declining ability to release oxygen.

7. **Massive haemorrhage is replaced in the proportions it was lost, because giving one component dilutes the others.** Replacing a lost blood volume with red cells and crystalloid alone leaves the patient with no clotting factors and no platelets; modern practice gives plasma and platelets early and in fixed ratios, with fibrinogen and tranexamic acid (an antifibrinolytic) and with warming, because the coagulation cascade's enzymes slow in the cold and acidotic patient. Transfusion is itself a dose-dependent risk factor for infection and organ failure, so the strongest treatment of haemorrhage is to stop it, which is the subject of [trauma and haemorrhage control](../../13-acute-perioperative-and-procedural-care/01-surgery/trauma-and-haemorrhage-control.md).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Crystalloid and colloid | Solutions of small ions that distribute through extracellular water, or of large molecules that stay in plasma while capillaries hold them |
| Balanced solution | Crystalloid whose anions approximate plasma rather than being all chloride |
| Fluid responsiveness | A rise in stroke volume with a volume load; the operational test of whether more fluid helps |
| Maintenance versus replacement versus resuscitation | Fluid for daily need, for measured losses, or for restoring circulating volume |
| Hyperchloraemic acidosis | Acidosis from large volumes of chloride-rich saline displacing bicarbonate |
| Osmotic demyelination | Pontine injury from correcting chronic hyponatraemia too fast |
| Refeeding syndrome | Electrolyte shifts and thiamine deficiency when a starved patient is fed rapidly |
| Blood component | Red cells, platelets, plasma or cryoprecipitate separated from donated whole blood |
| Restrictive transfusion threshold | Giving red cells only when haemoglobin falls below a lower limit; as safe as liberal thresholds in most trials |
| Massive transfusion | Replacement of a blood volume, requiring plasma and platelets in ratio with red cells |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Intravenous fluid is harmless because it is just water and salt."** Fluid overload causes pulmonary and gut oedema and worse outcomes in the critically ill; saline causes acidosis; it is a drug with a dose.
- **"Low sodium means the patient needs sodium."** Most hyponatraemia is water excess from antidiuretic hormone; giving saline to a patient retaining water can lower sodium further.
- **"A low haemoglobin should be corrected to normal."** Transfusion carries immunological and infectious risks and has not improved outcomes above restrictive thresholds in most trials; the trigger is oxygen delivery, not a number.
