## Essence

The equations of pharmacokinetics do not change with age, but every parameter in them does. A neonate is mostly water, with enzymes not yet switched on and slow kidneys; a pregnant woman has a dilute plasma, induced enzymes, a fast kidney and a placenta; an old person has traded water and muscle for fat, has lost much of the glomerular filtration rate with no warning in the creatinine, and takes several drugs at once. The single idea is that volume of distribution (Vd) and clearance (CL) belong to a body at a moment in its life, so the same milligram per kilogram gives different concentrations at 1 day, 5 years, 30 weeks of gestation and 85 years.

## Core tenets

1. **The newborn is a water-rich body with immature metabolism.** A term neonate is ~75% water (a preterm infant more) against ~60% in an adult, with twice the adult share of extracellular fluid, so polar drugs have a larger Vd per kilogram. Cytochrome P450 (CYP) oxidation and glucuronidation are low at birth and mature over weeks to months: chloramphenicol, cleared by glucuronidation, caused circulatory collapse ("grey baby syndrome") when neonates were dosed like small adults, and the same immaturity makes neonatal jaundice near-universal. Sulphonamides displace bilirubin from the neonate's scarce albumin into an immature brain: a rare case where protein displacement matters.

2. **Neonatal kidneys filter slowly and the absorbing surfaces behave differently.** Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) at term is about a quarter to a third of the surface-area-corrected adult value and reaches it by one to two years, so renally cleared drugs need long intervals. Gastric acid is scarce and emptying irregular, so oral absorption is erratic; thin skin and a high surface-to-mass ratio let topical agents reach systemic concentrations impossible in an adult.

3. **Children clear many drugs faster per kilogram than adults, so weight alone misleads.** Between about one and ten years the liver and kidney are large relative to body mass, and clearance per kilogram of many drugs exceeds the adult figure. Clearance scales with mass to the power of about 0.75 ([scale and allometry](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/scale-and-allometry.md)), so a per-kilogram dose derived from adults under-doses a child; body surface area, which scales similarly, is the practical correction.

4. **Pregnancy dilutes, induces and accelerates.** Plasma volume rises by ~40–50% and total body water by several litres, so Vd rises and albumin falls, raising the free fraction. GFR rises by about half. Oestrogen and progesterone induce CYP3A4, CYP2D6, CYP2C9 and glucuronidation (lamotrigine clearance can double) while CYP1A2 and CYP2C19 activity falls. Many drug concentrations therefore fall through pregnancy and rebound within weeks of delivery, so a dose adequate before conception may fail late and become excessive after birth ([pregnancy physiology](../../06-physiology/09-reproduction-and-the-life-course/pregnancy-placenta-and-lactation.md)).

5. **The placenta is a barrier to few drugs; timing decides the harm.** Most lipid-soluble, un-ionised drugs under ~500 Da cross by diffusion; fetal plasma is slightly more acidic than maternal, so weak bases (local anaesthetics, opioids) are ion-trapped on the fetal side. Structural vulnerability peaks during organogenesis, roughly weeks 3–8 after conception (thalidomide's limb defects came from a window of about two weeks). Before that window the embryo survives intact or dies; after it, drugs harm growth and function rather than form. See [congenital malformations and teratogens](../../04-genetics-evolution-and-development/03-development/congenital-malformations-and-teratogens.md).

6. **Milk is a slightly acidic, fat-rich compartment that most drugs reach in small amounts.** Lipid-soluble, weakly basic, poorly protein-bound drugs cross most, and milk's lower pH traps weak bases. The infant's exposure is milk concentration times volume drunk, divided by its own clearance; a relative infant dose below about 10% of the maternal weight-adjusted dose is conventionally regarded as low.

7. **Old age shifts the body towards fat and quietly removes clearance.** Lean mass and water fall and fat rises, so lipid-soluble drugs (benzodiazepines) have a larger Vd and longer half-life while water-soluble drugs have higher peaks. GFR falls by roughly 1 mL/min/1.73 m² per year after middle age, but creatinine stays normal because muscle, its source, shrinks in parallel; clearance must be estimated from equations using age and weight, or from cystatin C ([glomerular filtration](../../06-physiology/05-renal-system/glomerular-filtration-and-clearance.md)). Liver mass and blood flow fall by about a third, and CYP oxidation declines more than glucuronidation; a blunted baroreflex adds dynamic sensitivity.

8. **Polypharmacy multiplies interactions, and a prescribing cascade turns an adverse effect into a diagnosis.** Interactions scale with the number of drug pairs: five drugs carry ten, ten drugs forty-five. In a prescribing cascade (Rochon and Gurwitz, 1997) an adverse reaction is misread as a new disease and treated with another drug: a calcium-channel blocker causes ankle oedema, a diuretic is added, the diuretic causes gout. Every new symptom in an old patient is first a question about drugs.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Allometric scaling | Clearance scales with mass to about the 0.75 power |
| Body surface area | Tracks clearance better than weight; the basis of cytotoxic dosing |
| Organogenesis window | Weeks 3–8 after conception, when teratogens cause structural malformation |
| Relative infant dose | Infant's intake through milk as a percentage of the maternal weight-adjusted dose |
| Prescribing cascade | A drug's adverse effect misdiagnosed as disease and treated with another drug |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A normal creatinine means normal kidneys in an old patient."** Creatinine production falls with muscle mass; a frail 85-year-old can have a normal creatinine and half the GFR of a young adult.
- **"Children need smaller doses per kilogram because they are smaller."** Between infancy and puberty, clearance per kilogram is often higher than in adults.
- **"The placenta protects the fetus from drugs."** It passes most small lipid-soluble drugs; protection comes from timing and dose.
