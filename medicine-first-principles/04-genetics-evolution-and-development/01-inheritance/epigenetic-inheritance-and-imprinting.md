# Epigenetic inheritance and imprinting

## Essence

The Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) sequence is not the only heritable information in a cell. Methyl groups on cytosine and marks on histones decide which genes are open, and these marks are copied through cell division, so a liver cell's daughters are liver cells. Epigenetic inheritance is the transmission of such marks, overwhelmingly from cell to daughter cell within one body and, for a small set of imprinted genes, from parent to child. The idea that keeps the subject honest is **two very different timescales**: mitotic epigenetic memory is robust and underlies development and much cancer biology; transgenerational inheritance through the human germline is rare, because the marks are deliberately erased twice in every generation.

## Core tenets

1. **Epigenetic marks are heritable through mitosis because the copying machinery recognises them.** CpG methylation is symmetrical: after replication the maintenance methyltransferase DNMT1 reads the methylated parent strand and methylates the new one ([epigenetic mechanisms](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/03-genetic-information-flow/gene-regulation-and-epigenetic-mechanisms.md)). The result is a state stable through dozens of divisions: the memory that makes [differentiation](../../03-cell-biology/02-the-cell-life-cycle/stem-cells-differentiation-and-regeneration.md) stable.

2. **The germline erases and rewrites the marks, which is why most epigenetic states are not inherited.** After fertilisation the paternal genome is actively demethylated and the maternal one passively, wiping most methylation by the blastocyst stage; a second erasure occurs in the primordial germ cells. Two rounds of reprogramming per generation mean a methylation pattern acquired in a parent's liver has no route to a child's liver. Claims of transgenerational inheritance of acquired traits in humans rest on small observational studies with alternative explanations (in utero exposure of the grandchild's germ cells); in plants and worms the phenomenon is real.

3. **Imprinted genes escape the erasure and are expressed from one parental allele only.** About 100–200 human genes in ~20 clusters carry a mark laid down in egg or sperm that survives the post-fertilisation wave. Imprinting is reset each generation. The leading explanation is parental conflict over fetal resources (Haig): paternally expressed genes such as IGF2 promote fetal growth, maternally expressed genes restrain it.

4. **Imprinting disorders show that two normal alleles are not enough; they must come from the right parents.** Deletion of a paternally expressed region at 15q11–13 causes Prader–Willi syndrome (hypotonia, then insatiable appetite and obesity); deletion of the maternally expressed UBE3A in the same region causes Angelman syndrome (severe intellectual disability, seizures). The same deletion gives opposite diseases depending on which parent it came from. Uniparental disomy, both copies of a chromosome from one parent, produces the same disorders without any deletion.

5. **X inactivation is epigenetic inheritance within the body on the largest scale.** Each female cell silences one X early in development by coating it in XIST RNA and compacting it into a Barr body; the choice is random and then faithfully inherited, so a woman is a mosaic of paternal-X and maternal-X clones ([cytogenetics](chromosomes-and-cytogenetics.md)). The mosaic shows when the two X chromosomes differ: variable severity in female carriers of haemophilia depending on which X was silenced where.

6. **Cancer is as much an epigenetic disease as a genetic one.** Tumours show global hypomethylation (destabilising chromosomes) alongside focal hypermethylation that silences tumour suppressors: MLH1 silencing causes most sporadic microsatellite-unstable colorectal cancer, and MGMT silencing in glioblastoma predicts response to alkylating chemotherapy. Mutations in the epigenetic machinery itself (DNMT3A, TET2, IDH) are among the commonest cancer drivers. Unlike mutations, silencing is reversible, the rationale for hypomethylating drugs ([causes of cancer](../../08-pathology/03-neoplasia/causes-of-cancer.md)).

7. **The epigenome records age and exposure.** Methylation at a few hundred CpG sites changes so predictably with age that epigenetic clocks estimate age to within a few years from blood, and the discrepancy between epigenetic and chronological age predicts mortality in cohorts ([ageing](../03-development/growth-puberty-and-aging.md)). Nutrition and the uterine milieu during development shape methylation at loci that influence later metabolic disease: the molecular face of the developmental origins of adult disease, acting within one lifetime.

8. **Epigenetic variation explains part of the gap between genotype and phenotype.** Monozygotic twins are epigenetically near-identical at birth and diverge with age, which contributes to their discordance for disease. In mice, methylation at the agouti locus set by maternal diet determines coat colour and obesity: one genome, different stable phenotypes. The [pedigree](mendelian-inheritance-and-pedigrees.md) tracks sequence, but the phenotype is sequence read through chromatin.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Epigenetic mark | Heritable modification of DNA or histones that changes expression without changing sequence |
| CpG methylation | Methylation of cytosine in CG dinucleotides; dense in promoter islands, where it silences |
| Epigenetic reprogramming | Genome-wide erasure of marks after fertilisation and in the germline |
| Genomic imprinting | Parent-of-origin-specific silencing of one allele, set in the germline |
| Uniparental disomy | Both copies of a chromosome from one parent; causes imprinting disorders |
| X inactivation / Barr body | Random silencing of one X per female cell; its compacted form |
| Transgenerational epigenetic inheritance | Transmission of an acquired mark through the germline; rare in mammals |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Epigenetics means your experiences are passed to your grandchildren."** The mammalian germline erases marks twice per generation; robust human evidence is lacking, and most reported effects are explained by direct in utero exposure.
- **"Epigenetic means non-genetic."** The machinery that writes and reads marks is genetically encoded; epigenetics is how the genome regulates itself.
