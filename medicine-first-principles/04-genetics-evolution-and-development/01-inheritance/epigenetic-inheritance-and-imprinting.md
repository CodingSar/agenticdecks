# Epigenetic inheritance and imprinting

## Essence

The Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) sequence is not the only heritable information in a cell. Methyl groups on cytosine, chemical marks on histones and the three-dimensional folding of chromatin decide which genes are open, and these marks are copied through cell division, so a liver cell's daughters are liver cells. Epigenetic inheritance is the transmission of such marks, overwhelmingly from cell to daughter cell within one body and, in a small set of imprinted genes, from parent to child. Imprinting means that for ~100–200 human genes only the copy from one parent is expressed, because the other was silenced in that parent's germline. The idea that keeps the subject honest is **two very different timescales**: mitotic epigenetic memory is robust and is the basis of development and of much cancer biology; transgenerational epigenetic inheritance through the human germline is rare, because the marks are deliberately erased twice in every generation.

## Core tenets

1. **Epigenetic marks are heritable through mitosis because the copying machinery recognises them.** DNA methylation at cytosine-guanine (CpG) dinucleotides is symmetrical; after replication the maintenance methyltransferase DNMT1 reads the methylated parent strand and methylates the new one ([epigenetic mechanisms](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/03-genetic-information-flow/gene-regulation-and-epigenetic-mechanisms.md)). Histone marks are propagated by reader–writer complexes that bind an existing mark and deposit it on neighbouring nucleosomes. The result is a stable state that persists through dozens of divisions without any change in sequence: this is the memory that makes [differentiation](../../03-cell-biology/02-the-cell-life-cycle/stem-cells-differentiation-and-regeneration.md) stable and a tumour's cell of origin recognisable.

2. **The germline erases and rewrites the marks, which is why most epigenetic states are not inherited.** Shortly after fertilisation the paternal genome is actively demethylated and the maternal one passively, wiping most methylation by the blastocyst stage so that the embryo's cells can become anything; a second erasure occurs in the primordial germ cells. Marks are then re-established: in the germline according to the sex of the parent, and in the embryo according to lineage. Two rounds of reprogramming per generation mean a methylation pattern acquired in a parent's liver has no route to a child's liver. The claims of transgenerational inheritance of acquired traits in humans (famine exposure, trauma) rest on small, mostly observational studies and have alternative explanations (shared environment, in utero exposure of the grandchild's germ cells); in plants and worms the phenomenon is real and better understood.

3. **Imprinted genes escape the erasure and are expressed from one parental allele only.** About 100–200 human genes, clustered in ~20 regions, carry a methylation mark laid down in the egg or sperm that survives the post-fertilisation wave and silences one copy in every cell of the child. Imprinting is reset in each generation: a man passes a paternal mark on every chromosome, whichever parent he received it from. The leading evolutionary explanation is parental conflict over fetal resources (Haig, 1990s): paternally expressed genes such as IGF2 promote fetal growth at maternal expense, maternally expressed genes such as H19 and CDKN1C restrain it, and the opposing interests of the two genomes are written into the pattern.

4. **Imprinting disorders show that two normal alleles are not enough; they must come from the right parents.** Deletion of a paternally expressed region at 15q11–13 causes Prader–Willi syndrome (hypotonia, then insatiable appetite and obesity); deletion of the maternally expressed UBE3A in the same region causes Angelman syndrome (severe intellectual disability, seizures, a happy demeanour). The same deletion gives opposite diseases depending on which parent it came from. Uniparental disomy, in which both copies of a chromosome come from one parent (usually from a trisomy rescued by loss of one copy), produces the same disorders without any deletion. Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome (overgrowth, large tongue, embryonal tumours) and Silver–Russell syndrome (growth restriction) are reciprocal disorders of the 11p15 IGF2/H19 region: too much paternal dosage versus too little.

5. **X inactivation is epigenetic inheritance within the body on the largest scale.** Each female cell silences one X chromosome early in development by coating it in XIST RNA, methylating its promoters and compacting it into a Barr body; the choice is random and then faithfully inherited, so a woman is a mosaic of paternal-X and maternal-X clones ([cytogenetics](chromosomes-and-cytogenetics.md)). The mosaic is visible when the two X chromosomes differ: the patchy skin in X-linked dominant disorders such as incontinentia pigmenti, and the variable severity of female carriers of haemophilia or Duchenne muscular dystrophy depending on which X was silenced in the relevant tissue. Skewed inactivation can make a heterozygote as affected as a male.

6. **Cancer is as much an epigenetic disease as a genetic one.** Tumours show global hypomethylation (activating transposable elements and destabilising chromosomes) alongside focal hypermethylation of CpG islands that silences tumour suppressors: MLH1 silencing causes most sporadic microsatellite-unstable colorectal cancer, and MGMT silencing in glioblastoma predicts response to alkylating chemotherapy. Mutations in the epigenetic machinery itself (DNMT3A and TET2 in leukaemia and clonal haematopoiesis, IDH mutations that poison demethylases, histone mutations in paediatric gliomas) are among the commonest cancer drivers. Unlike mutations, epigenetic silencing is reversible, which is the rationale for hypomethylating agents in myelodysplasia ([causes of cancer](../../08-pathology/03-neoplasia/causes-of-cancer.md)).

7. **The epigenome records age and exposure.** Methylation at a few hundred CpG sites changes so predictably with age that epigenetic clocks estimate chronological age to within a few years from blood, and the discrepancy between epigenetic and chronological age predicts mortality in cohorts ([ageing](../03-development/growth-puberty-and-aging.md)). Smoking leaves a methylation signature in blood that persists for years after quitting. Environment during development, particularly nutrition and the uterine milieu, shapes the methylation set at loci that influence later metabolic disease; this is the molecular face of the developmental origins of adult disease, and it acts within one lifetime, not across generations.

8. **Epigenetic variation explains part of the gap between genotype and phenotype.** Monozygotic twins are epigenetically near-identical at birth and diverge with age and differing exposures, which contributes to their discordance for disease. Metastable epialleles (such as the agouti locus in mice, where methylation set by maternal diet determines coat colour and obesity) show that an identical genome can produce different stable phenotypes. These phenomena temper the Mendelian picture: the [pedigree](mendelian-inheritance-and-pedigrees.md) tracks sequence, but the phenotype is sequence read through chromatin.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Epigenetic mark | Heritable chemical modification of DNA or histones that changes gene expression without changing sequence |
| CpG methylation | Methylation of cytosine in CG dinucleotides; dense in promoter islands, where it silences |
| Maintenance methylation | Copying of methylation to the new strand after replication by DNMT1 |
| Epigenetic reprogramming | Genome-wide erasure of marks after fertilisation and in the germline |
| Genomic imprinting | Parent-of-origin-specific silencing of one allele, set in the germline |
| Uniparental disomy | Both copies of a chromosome from one parent; causes imprinting disorders |
| Parental conflict hypothesis | Imprinting as a tug-of-war between paternal and maternal genomes over fetal growth |
| X inactivation / Barr body | Random silencing of one X per female cell; its compacted form |
| Epigenetic clock | Age estimate from methylation at a set of CpG sites |
| Transgenerational epigenetic inheritance | Transmission of an acquired mark through the germline to descendants; rare in mammals |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Epigenetics means your experiences are passed to your grandchildren."** In mammals the germline erases marks twice per generation; robust human evidence for transgenerational inheritance is lacking, and most reported effects are explained by direct in utero exposure.
- **"Epigenetic means non-genetic."** The machinery that writes and reads marks is genetically encoded, and the pattern of marks is largely determined by sequence; epigenetics is how the genome regulates itself, not an alternative to it.
- **"Imprinted genes are inactive genes."** One allele is silenced; the other is fully expressed. The disorder arises when the active copy is lost or both copies come from the same parent.
