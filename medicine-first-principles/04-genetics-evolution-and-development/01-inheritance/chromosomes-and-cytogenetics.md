## Essence

The human genome is packaged into 46 chromosomes: 22 pairs of autosomes and two sex chromosomes, ~3.1 billion base pairs in all, each chromosome a single Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) molecule wound around histones and condensed a thousandfold at mitosis. Cytogenetics is the study of chromosomes as visible objects: their number, their structure and the large rearrangements that a microscope or an array can see. The organising idea is **dosage**: most genes are expressed in proportion to copy number, and a cell tolerates small deviations badly, so having one too many or too few of a whole chromosome, or a segment of one, disrupts hundreds of genes at once. Chromosomal abnormalities are found in roughly half of early miscarriages, in ~0.5–1% of live births, and in most cancers, where acquired rearrangements both drive the tumour and identify it.

## Core tenets

1. **A chromosome is one DNA molecule folded hierarchically, and the folding is regulatory.** DNA wraps around histone octamers (nucleosomes, 147 base pairs each), the beads coil into fibres, the fibres loop onto a scaffold, and at mitosis the loops condense into the familiar X-shaped body ([nucleic acids](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/01-macromolecules/nucleic-acids.md); [epigenetic mechanisms](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/03-genetic-information-flow/gene-regulation-and-epigenetic-mechanisms.md)). Loosely packed euchromatin is transcribed; tightly packed heterochromatin is silent. Position matters: a gene moved next to heterochromatin by a rearrangement can be silenced, and one moved next to a strong enhancer can be overexpressed, which is one way translocations cause cancer.

2. **The karyotype is read by number, then by structure.** Normal is 46,XX or 46,XY. Numerical abnormalities are whole chromosomes gained or lost (aneuploidy from [nondisjunction](../../03-cell-biology/02-the-cell-life-cycle/mitosis-meiosis-and-chromosome-segregation.md)) or whole sets (triploidy, 69 chromosomes, seen in molar pregnancy and early loss). Structural abnormalities are deletions, duplications, inversions, ring chromosomes and translocations, arising from double-strand breaks repaired to the wrong partner. A balanced rearrangement (no net gain or loss) usually leaves its carrier healthy but produces unbalanced gametes; an unbalanced one changes dosage and produces disease.

3. **Autosomal aneuploidy survives only when the chromosome is gene-poor, and the phenotype is dosage-wide.** Trisomy 21 (Down syndrome, ~1 in 700 births, 2020s, rising with maternal age) is viable because chromosome 21 is the smallest and carries ~230 protein-coding genes; trisomies 18 and 13 are usually fatal within the first year; all other autosomal trisomies and all autosomal monosomies are lost before birth. Down syndrome's consistent features (intellectual disability, characteristic facies, congenital heart defects in ~40–50%, early-onset Alzheimer pathology from three copies of the amyloid precursor protein gene, raised leukaemia risk) are the summed effect of 1.5-fold expression across the chromosome, not of one gene.

4. **Sex chromosome dosage is buffered by X inactivation, which is why its abnormalities are mild.** Early in female development each cell randomly silences one X chromosome by coating it in the long non-coding RNA XIST and heterochromatinising it; the choice is inherited by all descendants, so every female is a mosaic of two cell populations ([epigenetic inheritance](epigenetic-inheritance-and-imprinting.md)). Because extra X chromosomes are inactivated and the Y carries few genes, 47,XXY (Klinefelter syndrome, ~1 in 600 males) and 45,X (Turner syndrome, ~1 in 2,500 females) are compatible with near-normal life. Turner syndrome shows that one X is not quite enough: the genes that escape inactivation (including SHOX, for height) are then haploinsufficient, giving short stature, and 45,X is nonetheless lost in ~99% of conceptions. Skewed X inactivation explains why some female carriers of X-linked recessive disease are symptomatic.

5. **Structural rearrangements cause disease by breaking genes, changing dosage of segments, or fusing genes.** A deletion removes a block: 22q11.2 deletion (DiGeorge/velocardiofacial syndrome, ~1 in 4,000) removes ~40 genes and produces heart defects, thymic hypoplasia, hypocalcaemia and psychiatric disease. A Robertsonian translocation fuses two acrocentric chromosomes (13, 14, 15, 21, 22) into one; the carrier has 45 chromosomes and is healthy, but a 14;21 carrier can produce a child with translocation Down syndrome, which is the inherited ~4% of cases and has a recurrence risk of up to 10–15% from a carrier mother. Reciprocal translocations in a parent cause recurrent miscarriage because half or more of gametes are unbalanced.

6. **Acquired chromosomal changes define and drive cancer.** The Philadelphia chromosome, t(9;22), fuses BCR to ABL and makes a constitutively active tyrosine kinase: it defines chronic myeloid leukaemia, and the kinase inhibitor imatinib (2001) turned a fatal disease into a chronic one. t(8;14) in Burkitt lymphoma moves MYC next to the immunoglobulin heavy chain enhancer; t(15;17) in acute promyelocytic leukaemia fuses the retinoic acid receptor and is treated with the receptor's ligand ([haematological malignancies](../../12-organ-system-specialties/06-haematology/haematological-malignancies.md)). Solid tumours show gains, losses and amplifications (HER2 in breast cancer, MYCN in neuroblastoma) that guide prognosis and therapy. Cancer cytogenetics is the clearest case of a diagnostic test that is also a mechanism.

7. **Resolution determines what a test can see, so the technologies are complementary.** A banded karyotype resolves ~5–10 million base pairs and finds aneuploidy and balanced rearrangements. Fluorescence in situ hybridisation targets a known locus at ~100 kilobase resolution and is fast on single cells. Chromosomal microarray detects copy-number variants down to ~50–100 kilobases genome-wide but cannot see balanced rearrangements, and has replaced the karyotype as the first test for developmental delay. Genome sequencing now finds structural variants at base-pair resolution ([molecular techniques](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/03-genetic-information-flow/molecular-techniques.md)). Non-invasive prenatal testing sequences cell-free fetal DNA fragments in maternal plasma and infers trisomy from a small excess of chromosome 21 reads; it is a screen with ~99% sensitivity for trisomy 21, not a diagnosis.

8. **Mosaicism means the karyotype of one tissue may not be the karyotype of the person.** A nondisjunction or rearrangement after fertilisation produces a mixture of cell lines whose proportions differ by tissue. Mosaic trisomies are milder; confined placental mosaicism produces a false positive on chorionic villus sampling or cell-free DNA with a normal fetus; and somatic mosaic mutations in skin or brain cause segmental disorders and some epilepsies. The lesson is general in medicine: a sample reports the tissue it came from.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Karyotype | The ordered display of a cell's chromosomes; written as count, sex chromosomes, abnormalities |
| Aneuploidy / polyploidy | Gain or loss of individual chromosomes / of whole sets |
| Balanced / unbalanced rearrangement | Structural change with no net change in DNA content / with gain or loss |
| Reciprocal translocation | Exchange of segments between two non-homologous chromosomes |
| Robertsonian translocation | Fusion of two acrocentric chromosomes at the centromere |
| Deletion / duplication / copy-number variant | Loss or gain of a segment, from megabases down to tens of kilobases |
| X inactivation | Random silencing of one X in each female cell; dosage compensation |
| Mosaicism | Two or more cell lines of different karyotype in one person |
| Fluorescence in situ hybridisation | Labelled probe that lights up a locus on chromosomes or nuclei |
| Chromosomal microarray | Genome-wide copy-number detection at sub-microscopic resolution |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Chromosomal disorders are inherited."** Most are new events in a parent's meiosis or after fertilisation; only translocation carriers transmit a predictable risk.
- **"A normal karyotype excludes a chromosomal cause."** It excludes changes above ~5 million base pairs; microdeletions and most copy-number variants need an array or sequencing.
- **"The Y chromosome determines sex and does little else."** SRY on the Y triggers testis development, but sex differentiation then depends on hormones and dozens of autosomal genes, and disorders of sex development arise at every step.
