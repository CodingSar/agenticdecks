## Essence

Mendel's insight (1866) was that heredity is particulate: each parent passes one of two copies of each factor, the copies do not blend, and which copy is passed is chance. Meiosis is the mechanism: the two alleles of a gene sit on homologous chromosomes that segregate to different gametes. A pedigree is a diagram of who is related to whom and who is affected, and reading one is applied probability: the pattern of affected relatives gives the mode of inheritance, and the mode gives the risk to the next child. The idea that makes the subject work is **one gene, two alleles, random segregation**, from which dominant, recessive and X-linked patterns follow as arithmetic. Some 7,000 single-gene disorders are known, together affecting ~1% of births.

## Core tenets

1. **Segregation and independent assortment are facts about meiosis.** A diploid cell has two alleles of every autosomal gene; meiosis I separates the homologues, so a gamete carries one allele with probability 1/2 ([meiosis](../../03-cell-biology/02-the-cell-life-cycle/mitosis-meiosis-and-chromosome-segregation.md)). Genes on different chromosomes assort independently; genes on the same chromosome travel together unless a crossover separates them, with probability rising with distance.

2. **Dominance is a statement about the heterozygote, and it has molecular reasons.** An allele is dominant if one copy produces the trait: when half the normal protein is insufficient (haploinsufficiency, as in Marfan syndrome), when the mutant protein poisons the normal one in a multimer (dominant negative: glycine substitutions in collagen), or when the mutation activates the protein (gain of function: achondroplasia's overactive fibroblast growth factor receptor 3). Recessive disorders are mostly lost enzymes: one working copy supplies enough catalysis because enzymes are rarely rate-limiting at half dose ([enzymes](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/01-macromolecules/enzymes-and-catalysis.md)).

3. **Each mode of inheritance has a signature in the pedigree.** Autosomal dominant: every generation, both sexes, half of an affected parent's children, male-to-male transmission possible. Autosomal recessive: affected children of unaffected carrier parents, 1 in 4 recurrence, consanguinity raises the odds. X-linked recessive: affected males, carrier mothers, no male-to-male transmission (haemophilia, Duchenne muscular dystrophy). An affected father with an X-linked recessive allele has carrier daughters and unaffected sons; a carrier mother has half her sons affected and half her daughters carriers.

4. **Penetrance and expressivity are why the arithmetic does not always match the family.** Penetrance is the fraction of carriers who show the phenotype at all; expressivity is how severely. A BRCA1 mutation carries ~60–80% lifetime penetrance for breast cancer, so an unaffected carrier mother is common. Neurofibromatosis type 1 is fully penetrant but ranges from a few skin spots to disfiguring tumours. Reduced penetrance comes from modifier genes, environment and chance, and is why a dominant disease seems to skip a generation.

5. **New mutations, mosaicism and anticipation break naive expectations.** Many dominant disorders arise as new mutations (most achondroplasia, about a third of Duchenne cases), so no family history does not exclude a genetic disease, and recurrence risk to the parents is low but not zero, because a mutation in a fraction of a parent's germ cells (germline mosaicism) can recur. Trinucleotide repeat disorders (Huntington disease, fragile X) show anticipation: the repeat expands at each transmission, so onset is earlier in each generation.

6. **Bayesian calculation is the core of genetic counselling.** A woman whose brother has Duchenne muscular dystrophy, with an obligate-carrier mother, has a prior carrier probability of 1/2. If she has three unaffected sons, the probability of that outcome is 1/8 if she is a carrier and 1 if not; her posterior carrier probability is (1/2 × 1/8) / (1/2 × 1/8 + 1/2 × 1) = 1/9 ([Bayesian reasoning](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/01-probability-and-measurement/bayesian-reasoning.md)). Counselling conveys these numbers and the options (prenatal or preimplantation diagnosis, testing relatives) without directing the choice.

7. **Genotype–phenotype relations are rarely one-to-one.** The same gene causes several diseases depending on the mutation (different cystic fibrosis alleles give classic disease, isolated male infertility or pancreatitis), and the same disease results from many genes (retinitis pigmentosa has more than 80). "One gene, one disease" was always an approximation of "one gene, one protein, many contexts".

8. **Mendelian disease is the proving ground for mechanism and therapy.** Because a single defect produces the phenotype, Mendelian disorders reveal gene functions: familial hypercholesterolaemia gave the Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL) receptor pathway and the statins; cystic fibrosis gave the chloride channel and, in the 2010s, small-molecule correctors. The path from rare disease to common treatment recurs in [drug development](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/05-drug-development-and-toxicology/from-target-to-approval.md).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Allele | One of the alternative versions of a gene at a locus |
| Homozygous / heterozygous / hemizygous | Two identical alleles / two different / one copy only (X-linked genes in males) |
| Dominant / recessive | Phenotype shown by the heterozygote / only by the homozygote |
| Haploinsufficiency, dominant negative, gain of function | Three molecular reasons for dominance |
| Penetrance / expressivity | Proportion of carriers affected / severity among those affected |
| Germline mosaicism | A mutation in a fraction of a parent's gametes, allowing recurrence |
| Anticipation | Earlier onset and greater severity in successive generations; repeat expansion |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Dominant means common or strong."** It means the heterozygote is affected; many dominant alleles are rare and mild.
- **"A recessive disease needs an affected relative."** Carriers are healthy and unaware; most children with a recessive disorder have no family history, which is why carrier screening exists.
