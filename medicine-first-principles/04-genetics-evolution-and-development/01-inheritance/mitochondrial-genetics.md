## Essence

Every cell carries a second genome. Mitochondrial Deoxyribonucleic Acid (mtDNA) is a circle of ~16.6 kilobases with 37 genes, present in hundreds to thousands of copies per cell and inherited only from the egg. Those three facts break every rule of the previous leaves: there is no paternal allele, no segregation into halves, no diploid dosage. Instead a cell carries a *population* of genomes, and disease depends on what fraction of them are mutant. The single idea is **inheritance of a population, not a pair**: a mother transmits a sample of her mitochondrial genomes to each child, each tissue inherits a sample of each cell's, and phenotype follows the mutant load against a threshold. Mitochondrial disorders are the clinical result, and the same population genetics inside one body explains why they vary so widely between a mother and her children and between one tissue and another.

## Core tenets

1. **mtDNA encodes a small, essential part of the respiratory chain; the rest comes from the nucleus.** The 37 genes are 13 subunits of the oxidative phosphorylation complexes, 22 transfer Ribonucleic Acids (tRNAs) and 2 ribosomal RNAs ([oxidative phosphorylation](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/02-metabolism/glycolysis-tca-cycle-and-oxidative-phosphorylation.md)). The other ~1,500 mitochondrial proteins, including the polymerase and repair enzymes that maintain mtDNA, are nuclear and follow [Mendelian rules](mendelian-inheritance-and-pedigrees.md). So "mitochondrial disease" has two genetics: mtDNA mutations with maternal inheritance, and nuclear mutations, mostly autosomal recessive, that present identically. A pedigree decides which.

2. **Inheritance is strictly maternal.** The egg contributes ~10^5 mitochondrial genomes, the sperm a few hundred that are tagged and destroyed after fertilisation. A mother with an mtDNA mutation can transmit it to every child of either sex; an affected father transmits it to none. A pedigree with affected children of affected mothers only, no male transmission and both sexes affected is the signature, distinct from X-linked inheritance by the absence of carrier daughters of affected fathers.

3. **Heteroplasmy and threshold decide whether a mutation causes disease.** Most pathogenic mtDNA variants coexist with normal genomes in the same cell (heteroplasmy). Biochemical failure appears only when the mutant fraction exceeds a threshold, typically 60–90%, because the normal genomes complement the mutant until they are too few. The threshold is lowest in tissues with the highest Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) demand and least capacity to divide: brain, retina, cochlea, heart, skeletal muscle and the endocrine pancreas, which is why the diseases are neuromuscular, visual, auditory, cardiac and metabolic.

4. **Mitotic segregation makes heteroplasmy drift between cells, so phenotype varies by tissue and with time.** At each division mitochondria are shared out at random, so daughter cells inherit different mutant fractions ([probability](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/01-probability-and-measurement/probability.md)). Over a lifetime some lineages drift toward purity. In blood, the common m.3243A>G load falls with age as mutant-rich stem cells are selected against; in muscle fibres and neurons that never divide, mutant genomes expand clonally and the load rises, so the disease is progressive and a blood test can understate it. The same mutation can be mild in one sibling and lethal in another.

5. **A germline bottleneck resets the sample each generation.** During oogenesis mtDNA copy number falls to a few hundred molecules per primordial germ cell before expanding again, so each egg's mutant fraction is a small random sample of the mother's. One woman's eggs span a wide range of heteroplasmy, and the recurrence risk for a child cannot be given as a Mendelian fraction; it is a distribution around the mother's load, with the most damaging variants also removed by selection in the germline. The bottleneck is why a mutation can disappear or become fixed within a few generations.

6. **mtDNA mutates faster than nuclear DNA and does not recombine, so it records history.** Lacking histones, sitting beside the main source of reactive oxygen species and repaired less efficiently, mtDNA accumulates substitutions roughly 10–20 times faster per base than the nuclear genome ([mutation](genomic-variation-and-mutation.md)). Because it passes unmixed down the maternal line, it is a molecular clock: haplogroups trace maternal lineages and support the recent African origin of [modern humans](../02-evolution/human-evolution-and-variation.md). Somatically, deletions accumulate with age in muscle and substantia nigra neurons, one of the hallmarks of [ageing](../03-development/growth-puberty-and-aging.md).

7. **The phenotypes are syndromes of energy failure, and a few variants recur.** m.3243A>G in a tRNA gene causes MELAS (Mitochondrial Encephalopathy, Lactic Acidosis and Stroke-like episodes) at high load and maternally inherited diabetes with deafness at lower load. Leber hereditary optic neuropathy is usually homoplasmic, with incomplete penetrance and a male bias, so other factors modify it. Single large deletions cause Kearns–Sayre syndrome and are usually sporadic. Lactate rises because electrons cannot reach oxygen; muscle shows ragged red fibres packed with proliferating mitochondria. Clinically affected mitochondrial disease of either genetic origin affects about 1 in 5,000 people (high-income countries, 2010s), and pathogenic mtDNA variants are carried by roughly 1 in 200 ([single-gene disorders](../../08-pathology/04-genetic-metabolic-and-environmental-disease/single-gene-and-chromosomal-disorders.md); [inborn errors](../../08-pathology/04-genetic-metabolic-and-environmental-disease/inborn-errors-of-metabolism.md)).

8. **The only way to prevent transmission is to replace the population.** No treatment restores a mutant respiratory chain; care is supportive, and some drugs (aminoglycosides in carriers of m.1555A>G, which causes deafness; valproate in some nuclear forms) are avoided. Prenatal and preimplantation testing can select low-load embryos but cannot promise safety because load drifts. Mitochondrial replacement transfers the parents' nuclear genome into a donor egg stripped of its nucleus, so the child inherits donor mtDNA; it was legalised in the United Kingdom in 2015 and the first births there were reported in 2023. A small carry-over of maternal mtDNA can in principle expand, so the bottleneck that causes the problem also limits the fix ([fertility treatment](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/fertility-contraception-and-menopause.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| mtDNA | The 16.6-kilobase circular mitochondrial genome: 13 proteins, 22 tRNAs, 2 ribosomal RNAs |
| Maternal inheritance | Transmission from mother to all children of either sex; never from father |
| Heteroplasmy / homoplasmy | Mixture of mutant and normal mtDNA in a cell / a single type only |
| Threshold effect | Mutant fraction above which respiratory function fails; lowest in high-demand tissues |
| Mitotic segregation | Random partition of mitochondria at cell division, letting heteroplasmy drift |
| Germline bottleneck | Fall in mtDNA copies per germ cell that makes each egg a small sample of the mother's population |
| Ragged red fibre | Muscle fibre with accumulated abnormal mitochondria, the histological sign of mtDNA disease |
| Mitochondrial replacement | Transfer of the parental nuclear genome into an enucleated donor egg to avoid transmitting mutant mtDNA |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Mitochondrial disease is inherited from the mother."** Only mtDNA disease is; most mitochondrial disorders in children are nuclear, autosomal recessive, and come from both parents.
- **"The mutation load in a blood test is the patient's."** Heteroplasmy differs by tissue and changes with age; blood can be nearly clear while muscle and brain are heavily affected.
- **"A child of a carrier mother has a fixed risk."** The bottleneck gives each egg a different load; the risk is a distribution, and siblings can range from unaffected to severely ill.
