## Essence

Height, blood pressure, body mass, intelligence test scores and the risk of diabetes, coronary disease, schizophrenia and most cancers all run in families, yet none follows a Mendelian pattern. They are complex traits: shaped by hundreds to thousands of genetic variants, each of small effect, together with environment, and distributed continuously in the population. Fisher showed in 1918 that many Mendelian factors acting additively produce exactly the bell-shaped, familial, non-Mendelian pattern observed. Heritability is the fraction of the population's variation in a trait that is attributable to genetic differences, and the single idea that prevents misunderstanding is that **heritability describes variance in a population, not causation in an individual, and it depends on the environment the population lives in**. Genome-wide association studies have now found most of the common variants behind common disease; their use is risk stratification, drug target discovery and causal inference, not fortune-telling.

## Core tenets

1. **Many small additive effects produce a normal distribution and a familial but non-Mendelian trait.** If a trait is the sum of contributions from n loci, each with two alleles of small effect, the central limit theorem gives a Gaussian distribution ([distributions](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/01-probability-and-measurement/distributions-and-descriptive-statistics.md)), relatives are correlated in proportion to the alleles they share (1/2 for parent–child and siblings, 1/4 for half-siblings, 1/8 for first cousins), and no simple ratio appears in any family. Disease is modelled as a liability on this scale with a threshold: those beyond it are affected. The threshold model explains why the recurrence risk to a sibling is roughly the square root of the population prevalence (a disease of 1% has a sibling risk of ~10%), and falls off quickly for more distant relatives, unlike the Mendelian halving.

2. **Heritability is a ratio of variances, and it is a population statistic.** Phenotypic variance V_P = V_G + V_E (plus interaction and covariance terms); broad-sense heritability H² = V_G / V_P, and narrow-sense heritability h² = V_A / V_P counts only additive genetic variance, which is what parents transmit. Height has h² ≈ 0.8 in well-nourished populations; type 2 diabetes ~0.4–0.7; schizophrenia ~0.6–0.8; most common diseases 0.3–0.6. A heritability of 0.8 does not mean 80% of a person's height is genetic; it means 80% of the differences between people in that population, in that environment, are associated with genetic differences. Equalise the environment and heritability rises; a famine lowers everyone's height and raises the share of variance due to environment.

3. **Heritability is estimated from relatives and from genomes, and the two estimates disagree.** Twin studies compare the resemblance of monozygotic twins (sharing all genes) with dizygotic twins (sharing half) and infer h² from the difference; adoption studies separate shared genes from shared homes. These gave the figures above. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) then found that the significant common variants for height explain only a few percent of variance in early studies and ~40–50% with sample sizes in the millions, leaving a gap once called the missing heritability. The gap is now largely accounted for by very many common variants of effect too small to reach significance individually, rare variants not well captured by arrays, and some inflation of twin estimates by shared environment and interaction.

4. **Association is found by comparing allele frequencies in cases and controls across the genome, with a severe multiple-testing correction.** A GWAS tests roughly a million variants, so the significance threshold is set at p < 5 × 10^-8 ([hypothesis testing](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/02-inference-and-causation/hypothesis-testing-and-confidence-intervals.md)). A hit is a region, not a gene: the associated variant is in linkage disequilibrium with the causal one and is usually in non-coding, regulatory DNA. Typical odds ratios for common variants are 1.05–1.3 per allele. Population stratification (cases and controls of different ancestry) produces spurious associations and must be adjusted for ([regression and adjustment](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/02-inference-and-causation/regression-adjustment-and-survival-analysis.md)). By the 2020s, tens of thousands of loci have been robustly associated with hundreds of traits.

5. **Polygenic scores sum many small effects into one number, and their value is in stratification.** A polygenic risk score weights each of a person's alleles by its GWAS effect size and sums them. For coronary artery disease, people in the top few percent of the score have a risk about three times the average, comparable to a monogenic familial hypercholesterolaemia variant, and the score adds information to conventional risk factors ([risk prediction](../../10-epidemiology-and-evidence/03-screening-and-prevention/risk-prediction-and-stratification.md)). Its limits are real: discrimination for most diseases is modest (area under the curve ~0.6–0.7), the scores are built mostly on European-ancestry cohorts and transfer poorly to others (an equity problem), and a score is a probability of a population outcome, not a diagnosis.

6. **Genetic variants can be used as natural experiments to test whether a risk factor is causal.** Because alleles are assigned at conception, independently of later lifestyle and confounders, a variant that raises LDL cholesterol lifelong can be used as an instrument: if carriers have more coronary disease in proportion to their LDL, LDL is causal. This is Mendelian randomisation ([causal inference](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/02-inference-and-causation/causal-inference.md)). It confirmed LDL and blood pressure as causes of coronary disease, and showed that HDL cholesterol and C-reactive protein, though associated, are not causal, which explains the failure of drugs that raised HDL. It is one of the strongest tools for turning association into mechanism.

7. **Rare, large-effect variants and common, small-effect variants are ends of one spectrum, and both inform drug discovery.** Within a complex disease a minority of patients carry a rare Mendelian variant (monogenic diabetes within type 2 diabetes, familial hypercholesterolaemia within hyperlipidaemia), and finding them changes management. Conversely, the genes at GWAS loci name pathways: the lipid loci include every target of a current lipid-lowering drug, and loss-of-function variants in PCSK9 that lower LDL and protect against coronary disease led directly to PCSK9 inhibitors (approved 2015). A drug target with human genetic support is about twice as likely to reach approval.

8. **Gene and environment interact and are correlated, so the partition into V_G and V_E is a model, not a fact of nature.** Phenylketonuria is fully genetic and fully environmental: the gene destroys the enzyme, the diet determines whether the brain is damaged. Alcohol-flushing variants in ALDH2 reduce alcohol intake and so oesophageal cancer, a gene acting through behaviour. Children inherit both genes and the environment their parents' genes helped build. [Multifactorial disease](../../08-pathology/05-disease-as-process/multifactorial-disease-and-risk.md) is the rule, and the practical response to a high polygenic risk is usually an environmental one: in people at the top of the polygenic score for coronary disease, a favourable lifestyle roughly halves the risk.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Complex (multifactorial) trait | Trait shaped by many genes and environment, with continuous variation |
| Liability–threshold model | Disease as the tail of a continuous latent risk distribution |
| Heritability (h², H²) | Fraction of phenotypic variance due to additive / all genetic variance in a population |
| Twin and adoption studies | Designs that separate genetic from environmental resemblance in relatives |
| Genome-wide association study (GWAS) | Hypothesis-free comparison of allele frequencies across the genome between cases and controls |
| Linkage disequilibrium | Correlation between nearby alleles; an associated variant tags a region |
| Polygenic risk score | Weighted sum of a person's risk alleles |
| Missing heritability | The gap between twin estimates and the variance explained by significant GWAS variants |
| Mendelian randomisation | Using genetic variants as instruments to test the causality of an exposure |
| Gene–environment interaction | The effect of a genotype depending on the environment, and vice versa |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A heritability of 80% means the trait is 80% genetic in each person."** Heritability partitions differences between people in one population and environment; it says nothing about the make-up of one individual and changes when the environment changes.
- **"High heritability means environment cannot change the trait."** Height is highly heritable and rose by ~10 cm in many countries in a century through nutrition; phenylketonuria is fully heritable and fully treatable by diet.
- **"A GWAS hit identifies the disease gene."** It identifies a region, usually regulatory, in linkage disequilibrium with the causal variant; the gene is a hypothesis until functional work supports it.
