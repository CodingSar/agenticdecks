## Essence

Any two unrelated human genomes differ at roughly 4–5 million positions, about one base in 1,000, and every person carries ~60–100 new mutations that neither parent had. Variation is the raw material of inheritance, evolution and individuality, and mutation is its source: errors in Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) replication and repair, damage by chemicals and radiation, and the mobile elements that make up almost half the genome. The idea that makes the subject tractable is **classification by consequence**: a variant matters according to what it does to a protein or to the control of a gene, not according to how large it is, and most variants do nothing at all. Distinguishing the pathogenic variant from the benign background is the central task of clinical genomics, and the mutations acquired by somatic cells over a lifetime are the cause of cancer and part of ageing.

## Core tenets

1. **Mutation is inevitable because replication and repair are imperfect, and the rate is known.** Replication copies 6 billion bases per cell division with an error rate, after proofreading and mismatch repair, of ~10^-9 to 10^-10 per base ([DNA replication and repair](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/03-genetic-information-flow/dna-replication-and-repair.md)). The germline mutation rate is ~1.2 × 10^-8 per base per generation, giving ~60–100 new single-nucleotide variants per child, about 80% from the father, and rising by one to two per year of paternal age because sperm precursors keep dividing. Somatic cells accumulate ~15–40 mutations a year in most tissues, so a cell in a 60-year-old carries thousands; the lifetime mutation count, not any single exposure, explains why cancer incidence rises steeply with age.

2. **Variants are classified by size and by effect, and the effect is what matters.** Single-nucleotide variants are the commonest; small insertions and deletions next; then copy-number variants, repeat expansions and large rearrangements ([cytogenetics](chromosomes-and-cytogenetics.md)). By effect: synonymous (no amino acid change), missense (one amino acid changed), nonsense (premature stop), frameshift (an indel not divisible by three scrambles the downstream reading frame; [translation](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/03-genetic-information-flow/translation-and-protein-targeting.md)), splice-site (exon skipped or intron retained) and regulatory (promoter or enhancer altered). A nonsense or frameshift variant usually destroys the protein and its transcript is degraded by nonsense-mediated decay; a missense variant may do anything from nothing to a dominant gain of function. Size is a poor guide: a single base in a splice site can abolish a protein, while a 100-kilobase deletion in a gene desert does nothing.

3. **Most variation is neutral, and the frequency of a variant tells you about its effect.** Of the ~4–5 million variants in a genome, the vast majority lie outside coding sequence; about 10,000–12,000 are missense and perhaps 100 are predicted to destroy a protein, mostly in genes where loss is tolerated. Common variants (allele frequency above 1–5%) are almost never the cause of severe childhood disease, because selection would have removed them ([population genetics](../02-evolution/natural-selection-and-population-genetics.md)). So the first filter in interpreting a genome is frequency in large reference populations: a variant seen in 1% of healthy people cannot cause a fully penetrant rare disease. Reference databases must represent all ancestries, or benign variants common in an under-sampled population will be mistaken for pathogenic ones.

4. **Interpreting a variant is a Bayesian weighing of evidence, and uncertainty is the usual verdict.** Population frequency, predicted effect on protein, functional assays, segregation with disease in families, and whether the variant is new in an affected child are combined into five tiers: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign, benign (American College of Medical Genetics framework, 2015). A large share of missense variants found in a clinical gene panel are of uncertain significance, and that proportion is higher in people of non-European ancestry. Reclassification over time is normal. Clinical reports therefore state evidence, not certainty, and a variant of uncertain significance should not drive treatment ([molecular diagnostics](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/02-laboratory-medicine/histopathology-cytology-and-molecular-diagnostics.md)).

5. **Mutagens leave signatures, which link cause to cancer.** Ultraviolet light makes cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers and so cytosine-to-thymine changes at adjacent pyrimidines: melanomas and skin carcinomas carry this signature by the thousand. Tobacco smoke's benzo[a]pyrene adducts guanine and leaves guanine-to-thymine changes in lung cancer. Aflatoxin produces a specific codon 249 change in p53 in liver cancer. Deficient mismatch repair gives microsatellite instability; APOBEC enzymes give clustered cytosine mutations; spontaneous deamination of methylated cytosine gives the universal cytosine-to-thymine background that accumulates with age. Reading the signature in a tumour genome can name the exposure decades later and predicts response to immunotherapy, since highly mutated tumours present more foreign peptides ([oncogenes and genomic instability](../../08-pathology/03-neoplasia/oncogenes-tumour-suppressors-and-genomic-instability.md)).

6. **Somatic and germline mutations have different consequences, and both matter in cancer.** A germline variant is in every cell and is heritable; a somatic variant is in a clone and is not. A person inheriting one defective copy of a tumour suppressor (retinoblastoma, BRCA1, the mismatch repair genes) needs only one somatic hit to lose the function in a cell, which is why familial cancers are earlier, multiple and bilateral (Knudson's two-hit hypothesis, 1971). Sequencing a tumour alongside normal tissue separates the two; a somatic finding guides therapy, a germline finding has implications for relatives and must be handled as such.

7. **Repeat expansions and mobile elements are mutational mechanisms of their own.** Microsatellite repeats slip during replication; once a trinucleotide repeat passes a threshold (~36 CAG repeats in the Huntington gene) it becomes unstable and expands further in each generation, producing [anticipation](mendelian-inheritance-and-pedigrees.md). Transposable elements (~45% of the genome; the LINE-1 retrotransposon is still active) occasionally insert into genes and cause disease (some haemophilia A cases), and their repeated sequences are the substrate for the misaligned recombination that produces recurrent deletions and duplications such as 22q11.2.

8. **Variation is also the basis of individual differences in drug response and disease risk.** Common variants in drug-metabolising enzymes (CYP2D6, CYP2C19, TPMT), transporters and targets make a standard dose toxic in one person and ineffective in another ([pharmacogenomics](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/02-pharmacodynamics/pharmacogenomics-and-individual-variation.md)); HLA variants set the risk of severe drug hypersensitivity (HLA-B*57:01 and abacavir, HLA-B*15:02 and carbamazepine in East Asian populations). Thousands of common variants of tiny effect sum into the polygenic risk discussed in [complex traits](complex-traits-and-heritability.md). The same genome that explains a rare disease in one family explains ordinary variation in everyone.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Single-nucleotide variant (SNV) | A one-base difference; polymorphism if common |
| Indel | Small insertion or deletion; a frameshift if not a multiple of three in coding sequence |
| Missense / nonsense / synonymous | Amino acid changed / premature stop / no change |
| Loss of function / gain of function | Protein absent or inactive / protein overactive or with a new activity |
| Allele frequency | Proportion of chromosomes in a population carrying a variant |
| Variant of uncertain significance | A variant whose effect on health cannot yet be classified |
| Germline / somatic | In the gametes and all cells / acquired in a clone of body cells |
| Mutational signature | Characteristic pattern of base changes left by a mutagen or repair defect |
| De novo mutation | Present in a child and absent in both parents' blood |
| Transposable element | Mobile DNA sequence; LINE-1 and Alu make up a large share of the genome |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A mutation is a harmful change."** Most are neutral, some are beneficial, and "mutation" and "variant" describe the same thing; clinical usage reserves "pathogenic variant" for the harmful subset.
- **"Finding a variant in a disease gene explains the disease."** Everyone carries variants in every gene; without frequency, segregation and functional evidence, a variant is a hypothesis.
- **"Cancer mutations come from carcinogens."** Many are the unavoidable by-product of normal replication over decades; carcinogens add to a baseline that is never zero, which is why cancer occurs without any identifiable exposure.
