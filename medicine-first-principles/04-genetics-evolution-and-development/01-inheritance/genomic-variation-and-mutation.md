## Essence

Any two unrelated human genomes differ at roughly 4–5 million positions, about one base in 1,000, and every person carries ~60–100 new mutations that neither parent had. Variation is the raw material of inheritance, evolution and individuality; mutation is its source: errors in Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) replication and repair, damage by chemicals and radiation, and the mobile elements that make up almost half the genome. The idea that makes the subject tractable is **classification by consequence**: a variant matters according to what it does to a protein or to a gene's control, not according to its size, and most variants do nothing. Telling the pathogenic variant from the benign background is the central task of clinical genomics.

## Core tenets

1. **Mutation is inevitable because replication and repair are imperfect, and the rate is known.** After proofreading and mismatch repair the error rate is ~10^-9 to 10^-10 per base per division ([DNA replication and repair](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/03-genetic-information-flow/dna-replication-and-repair.md)). The germline rate is ~1.2 × 10^-8 per base per generation, giving ~60–100 new variants per child, mostly from the father and rising with paternal age. Somatic cells accumulate ~15–40 mutations a year; the lifetime count, not any single exposure, explains why cancer incidence rises steeply with age.

2. **Variants are classified by size and by effect, and effect is what matters.** Single-nucleotide variants are commonest; then small insertions and deletions, copy-number variants, repeat expansions and large rearrangements ([cytogenetics](chromosomes-and-cytogenetics.md)). By effect: synonymous (no amino acid change), missense (one changed), nonsense (premature stop), frameshift (an indel not divisible by three scrambles the downstream frame; [translation](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/03-genetic-information-flow/translation-and-protein-targeting.md)), splice-site and regulatory. A single base in a splice site can abolish a protein while a 100-kilobase deletion in a gene desert does nothing.

3. **Most variation is neutral, and frequency tells you about effect.** Common variants (frequency above 1–5%) almost never cause severe childhood disease, because selection would have removed them ([population genetics](../02-evolution/natural-selection-and-population-genetics.md)). So the first filter in interpreting a genome is frequency in large reference populations, which must represent all ancestries, or benign variants common in an under-sampled population will be mistaken for pathogenic ones.

4. **Interpreting a variant is a weighing of evidence, and uncertainty is the usual verdict.** Population frequency, predicted effect, functional assays, segregation in families and whether the variant is new in an affected child are combined into five tiers: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign, benign (American College of Medical Genetics framework, 2015). Many missense variants on a clinical panel are of uncertain significance, more so in non-European ancestry. A variant of uncertain significance should not drive treatment ([molecular diagnostics](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/02-laboratory-medicine/histopathology-cytology-and-molecular-diagnostics.md)).

5. **Mutagens leave signatures, which link cause to cancer.** Ultraviolet light makes cytosine-to-thymine changes at adjacent pyrimidines, found by the thousand in melanoma; tobacco's benzo[a]pyrene leaves guanine-to-thymine changes in lung cancer; aflatoxin produces a specific p53 codon change in liver cancer; deamination of methylated cytosine gives the universal background that accumulates with age. A tumour's signature can name an exposure decades later and predicts response to immunotherapy ([genomic instability](../../08-pathology/03-neoplasia/oncogenes-tumour-suppressors-and-genomic-instability.md)).

6. **Somatic and germline mutations differ in consequence, and both matter in cancer.** A germline variant is in every cell and heritable; a somatic variant is in a clone. A person inheriting one defective copy of a tumour suppressor (retinoblastoma, BRCA1, mismatch repair genes) needs only one somatic hit to lose the function in a cell, which is why familial cancers are early, multiple and bilateral (Knudson's two-hit hypothesis, 1971). A somatic finding guides therapy; a germline finding has implications for relatives.

7. **Repeat expansions and mobile elements are mutational mechanisms of their own.** Microsatellites slip during replication; once a trinucleotide repeat passes a threshold (~36 CAG repeats in the Huntington gene) it expands in each generation, producing [anticipation](mendelian-inheritance-and-pedigrees.md). Transposable elements (~45% of the genome) occasionally insert into genes, and their repeats are the substrate for the misaligned recombination behind recurrent deletions such as 22q11.2.

8. **Variation also underlies individual differences in drug response.** Common variants in drug-metabolising enzymes (CYP2D6, CYP2C19, TPMT) make a standard dose toxic in one person and ineffective in another ([pharmacogenomics](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/02-pharmacodynamics/pharmacogenomics-and-individual-variation.md)); HLA variants set the risk of severe drug hypersensitivity (HLA-B*57:01 and abacavir). Thousands of variants of tiny effect sum into the polygenic risk of [complex traits](complex-traits-and-heritability.md).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Single-nucleotide variant (SNV) | A one-base difference; a polymorphism if common |
| Indel | Small insertion or deletion; a frameshift if not a multiple of three in coding sequence |
| Missense / nonsense / synonymous | Amino acid changed / premature stop / no change |
| Loss of function / gain of function | Protein absent or inactive / overactive or with a new activity |
| Allele frequency | Proportion of chromosomes in a population carrying a variant |
| Variant of uncertain significance | A variant whose effect on health cannot yet be classified |
| Germline / somatic | In the gametes and all cells / acquired in a clone of body cells |
| Mutational signature | Pattern of base changes left by a mutagen or repair defect |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A mutation is a harmful change."** Most are neutral; clinical usage reserves "pathogenic variant" for the harmful subset.
- **"Finding a variant in a disease gene explains the disease."** Everyone carries variants in every gene; without frequency, segregation and functional evidence a variant is a hypothesis.
