## What this area is about

Inheritance explains how a genome passes from parent to child; evolution explains why the genome is what it is. This area asks the question that the rest of medicine rarely asks aloud: why is the body vulnerable in the particular ways it is? The answer is that selection maximises reproduction in past environments, acts on populations rather than individuals, is weak after reproductive age, and can only modify what already exists. From those four facts follow the persistence of genetic disease, the clustering of alleles by geography, the mismatch diseases of modern life, the trade-offs behind inflammation, clotting and ageing, and the fact that bacteria, viruses and tumours evolve inside a patient faster than any drug can be developed. The unifying idea is **populations change because some variants reproduce more than others**, and that idea is as true of a tumour under chemotherapy as of a species over millennia.

Read natural selection first; it gives the quantitative rules every other child applies. Evolutionary medicine is the broad interpretation of the body in that light. Host–pathogen coevolution takes one of its themes, the arms race, and develops it because it has the most immediate clinical consequences: antibiotic resistance, vaccine design and combination therapy. Human evolution and variation closes with the specific history of our species, which decides which alleles a given patient is likely to carry and which clinical uses of ancestry are sound.

## Connections to sibling areas

- **[Inheritance](../01-inheritance/README.md):** supplies the alleles, their frequencies and their modes of transmission; evolution explains why those frequencies are what they are, why recessive diseases persist, why founder populations carry particular disorders and why a variant's frequency is the first clue to its pathogenicity.
- **[Development](../03-development/README.md):** the developmental programme is itself the product of selection and the carrier of its constraints: the recurrent laryngeal nerve, the narrow pelvis, the thrifty fetal phenotype. Evolutionary developmental biology shows that the same conserved genes build every vertebrate, which is why animal models work and why congenital malformations recur in recognisable patterns.
