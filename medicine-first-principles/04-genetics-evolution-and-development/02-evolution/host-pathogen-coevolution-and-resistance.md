# Host–pathogen coevolution and resistance

## Essence

Hosts and the organisms that infect them evolve in response to each other, and the contest is unequal in tempo: a bacterium's generation is minutes, a virus's hours, a human's decades. The host answers with a somatically diversifying immune system and the most polymorphic genes in its genome; the pathogen answers with mutation, recombination, horizontal gene transfer and antigenic variation. Antimicrobial drugs enter this contest as a new selective pressure, and resistance is the predictable result: in a population of 10^12 bacteria every single-step resistance mutation already exists, and the drug merely selects it. The single idea is **selection acts on populations, not individuals, at a rate set by population size, mutation supply and generation time**, which is why resistance appears within years of every new antibiotic, why tuberculosis is treated with four drugs, why influenza vaccines are reformulated annually, and why stewardship is an evolutionary intervention.

## Core tenets

1. **Mutation supply in a microbial population is enormous, so resistance pre-exists treatment.** With ~10^9 bacteria per gram of infected tissue and a mutation rate of ~10^-9 to 10^-10 per base per division ([replication fidelity](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/03-genetic-information-flow/dna-replication-and-repair.md)), each possible point mutation arises roughly once per generation in a large infection. If one mutation confers resistance (rifampicin resistance in *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* arises at ~10^-8 per division), a resistant cell is almost certainly present before the first dose. Treatment with that drug alone kills the sensitive 99.99999% and leaves the resistant clone a cleared field ([exponential growth](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/03-systems-and-models/compartment-models-and-exponential-processes.md)). Fluctuation experiments (Luria and Delbrück, 1943) showed exactly this: mutants exist before selection.

2. **Combination therapy works because the probability of simultaneous resistance is the product of the single probabilities.** If resistance to each of two drugs arises at 10^-8, a doubly resistant mutant arises at 10^-16, far beyond the size of any infection. This is why tuberculosis has been treated with three or four drugs since the 1950s, why HIV was uncontrolled by monotherapy and controlled by three-drug therapy from 1996, and why cancer chemotherapy is given in combinations. The logic fails when drugs share a resistance mechanism, when adherence is partial (so that the patient receives sequential monotherapy), or when sub-inhibitory concentrations (a missed dose, poor tissue penetration) let partially resistant cells survive and take the next step.

3. **Resistance spreads horizontally, so it is a property of the microbial community, not of one lineage.** Bacteria exchange plasmids by conjugation, take up free DNA by transformation and receive genes from phages by transduction. Resistance genes, often clustered on mobile elements with genes for other resistances, move between species and across the gut, the hospital and the farm. An extended-spectrum beta-lactamase gene selected by cephalosporin use in one patient's *Escherichia coli* can appear in a *Klebsiella* in another ward. This is why antibiotic use anywhere (including agriculture, which consumes a large share of global antibiotics) selects resistance everywhere, and why the pre-antibiotic reservoir of resistance genes in soil bacteria, which have made and resisted antibiotics for a billion years, is so large.

4. **The biochemical routes to resistance are few and recur across drugs.** Destroy the drug (beta-lactamases hydrolysing penicillins; [enzyme catalysis](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/01-macromolecules/enzymes-and-catalysis.md)); alter the target so the drug no longer binds (mutated penicillin-binding protein in methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*, mutated ribosomal RNA for macrolides, mutated gyrase for fluoroquinolones); keep the drug out or pump it out (porin loss and efflux pumps in Gram-negatives); bypass the blocked pathway (an alternative enzyme); or protect the target. Each resistance mechanism has a fitness cost in the absence of the drug, which is usually small and is often reduced by compensatory mutations, so resistance declines only slowly when a drug is withdrawn ([antimicrobials](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/antimicrobials.md)).

5. **The host's answer to a faster-evolving enemy is to evolve within a lifetime.** The adaptive immune system generates ~10^11 distinct antibody and T-cell receptor specificities by somatic recombination and mutation, then selects the clones that bind: Darwinian evolution run inside one body over days ([antigen presentation](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/03-adaptive-immunity/antigen-presentation-and-mhc.md)). At the population level the Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) genes carry thousands of alleles, maintained by frequency-dependent and heterozygote-advantage selection: a pathogen that has evolved to evade the common alleles is caught by rare ones. Mate choice and the difficulty of transplant matching are side effects of this diversity.

6. **Pathogens evade recognition by antigenic variation, and the mechanism sets the vaccination strategy.** Influenza A accumulates point mutations in its surface proteins (antigenic drift), so immunity wanes over seasons and the vaccine is reformulated annually; occasionally it reassorts whole gene segments with animal strains (antigenic shift) and a pandemic follows. HIV's error-prone reverse transcriptase produces a swarm of variants in each patient that outruns neutralising antibodies and makes a conventional vaccine extremely hard. Trypanosomes switch among hundreds of surface coat genes; *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* and malaria parasites recombine their antigens. Pathogens with stable antigens (measles, smallpox, polio) were the ones eradicated or nearly eradicated by vaccination ([viruses](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/01-microbes-and-infection/viruses.md)).

7. **Virulence is a trait under selection, shaped by how the pathogen is transmitted.** A pathogen that depends on a mobile host to spread (common cold, spread by contact) is selected toward mildness; one spread by vectors, water or durable spores can afford to incapacitate (malaria, cholera, anthrax). Myxoma virus released into Australian rabbits in 1950 evolved from >99% to ~50–90% lethality within years because strains that killed too fast were transmitted less. The trade-off model predicts that improving sanitation (making water-borne spread harder) selects for milder strains, which was observed in cholera. Virulence is not a fixed property of a species but an equilibrium that human behaviour shifts.

8. **Stewardship, infection control and vaccination are evolutionary interventions, and they work by changing the selective landscape.** Reducing total antibiotic exposure lowers selection pressure; using the narrowest effective agent limits the bystander selection on the gut flora; completing a course at adequate dose avoids sub-inhibitory selection; combination therapy makes resistance improbable; isolation stops horizontal spread of resistant clones; vaccination removes the host population that a strain needs. Resistance to each new antibiotic class has appeared within 1–20 years of introduction since penicillin (1940s), and the pipeline of new classes has thinned since the 1980s; global deaths attributable to bacterial resistance were estimated at ~1.3 million in 2019. The economics (a new antibiotic should be used as little as possible) and the biology together make this a problem of population management rather than of any one prescription ([stewardship](../../12-organ-system-specialties/08-infectious-diseases/antimicrobial-stewardship.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Coevolution | Reciprocal adaptation of two interacting species over time |
| Red Queen dynamics | Continuous adaptation needed merely to maintain fitness against an evolving opponent |
| Mutation supply | Population size × mutation rate: how many variants arise per generation |
| Selective window | Drug concentration range that kills sensitive but not partially resistant cells |
| Horizontal gene transfer | Movement of genes between bacteria by plasmids, transformation or phage |
| Fitness cost of resistance | Reduced growth of resistant cells without the drug; often compensated |
| Antigenic drift / shift | Gradual point mutation / abrupt reassortment of pathogen surface antigens |
| MHC polymorphism | Extreme allelic diversity of antigen-presenting genes maintained by pathogen pressure |
| Virulence–transmission trade-off | Selection on how much harm a pathogen does, set by its route of spread |
| Antimicrobial stewardship | Managing antibiotic use to minimise selection for resistance |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Patients become resistant to antibiotics."** Bacteria do; the resistant population may be acquired from another person or selected from the patient's own flora, and it can be transmitted onward.
- **"Stopping antibiotics early always breeds resistance."** The evidence is mixed; unnecessarily long courses also select resistance in the bystander flora. The principle is adequate dose for the time needed to clear the infection, which varies by infection.
- **"Pathogens evolve to kill their hosts."** Killing is incidental to transmission; selection on virulence depends on how the pathogen spreads, and many evolve toward mildness.
