# Host–pathogen coevolution and resistance

## Essence

Hosts and pathogens evolve in response to each other, and the contest is unequal in tempo: a bacterium's generation is minutes, a human's decades. The host answers with a somatically diversifying immune system and the most polymorphic genes in its genome; the pathogen answers with mutation, horizontal gene transfer and antigenic variation. Antimicrobial drugs are a new selective pressure, and in a population of 10^12 bacteria every single-step resistance mutation already exists for the drug to select. The single idea is **selection acts on populations at a rate set by population size, mutation supply and generation time**, which is why resistance follows every new antibiotic, why tuberculosis is treated with four drugs and why stewardship is an evolutionary intervention.

## Core tenets

1. **Mutation supply in a microbial population is enormous, so resistance pre-exists treatment.** With ~10^9 bacteria per gram of tissue and a mutation rate of ~10^-9 per base per division ([replication fidelity](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/03-genetic-information-flow/dna-replication-and-repair.md)), each point mutation arises about once per generation in a large infection. Rifampicin resistance in *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* arises at ~10^-8 per division, so a resistant cell is almost certainly present before the first dose; monotherapy kills the sensitive majority and leaves it a cleared field ([exponential growth](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/03-systems-and-models/compartment-models-and-exponential-processes.md)).

2. **Combination therapy works because the probability of simultaneous resistance is the product of the single probabilities.** If resistance to each of two drugs arises at 10^-8, a doubly resistant mutant arises at 10^-16, beyond the size of any infection. This is why tuberculosis has been treated with several drugs since the 1950s and why Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) was controlled only by triple therapy from 1996. The logic fails when drugs share a resistance mechanism, when adherence is partial, or when sub-inhibitory concentrations let partially resistant cells take the next step.

3. **Resistance spreads horizontally, so it belongs to the microbial community, not one lineage.** Bacteria exchange plasmids, take up free Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) and receive genes from phages, so resistance genes move between species and across gut, hospital and farm. Antibiotic use anywhere, including agriculture, selects resistance everywhere.

4. **The biochemical routes to resistance are few and recur across drugs.** Destroy the drug (beta-lactamases; [enzyme catalysis](../../02-biochemistry-and-molecular-biology/01-macromolecules/enzymes-and-catalysis.md)); alter the target so the drug no longer binds (mutated penicillin-binding protein in methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*, mutated gyrase for fluoroquinolones); keep the drug out or pump it out (porin loss, efflux pumps); bypass the blocked pathway. Each mechanism has a fitness cost without the drug, usually small and often compensated, so resistance declines slowly when a drug is withdrawn ([antimicrobials](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/03-drug-classes-by-mechanism/antimicrobials.md)).

5. **The host's answer to a faster enemy is to evolve within a lifetime.** The adaptive immune system generates ~10^11 antibody and T-cell receptor specificities by somatic recombination and mutation, then selects the clones that bind: Darwinian evolution run inside one body over days ([antigen presentation](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/03-adaptive-immunity/antigen-presentation-and-mhc.md)). The Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) genes carry thousands of alleles, maintained by frequency-dependent selection: a pathogen adapted to the common alleles is caught by rare ones.

6. **Pathogens evade recognition by antigenic variation, and the mechanism sets the vaccine strategy.** Influenza A accumulates point mutations in its surface proteins (drift), so the vaccine is reformulated annually; occasionally it reassorts segments with animal strains (shift) and a pandemic follows. HIV's error-prone reverse transcriptase produces a swarm in each patient that outruns antibodies. Pathogens with stable antigens (measles, smallpox, polio) are the ones vaccination has eradicated or nearly ([viruses](../../07-microbiology-and-immunology/01-microbes-and-infection/viruses.md)).

7. **Virulence is a trait under selection, shaped by transmission.** A pathogen that needs a mobile host (the common cold) is selected toward mildness; one spread by vectors, water or durable spores (malaria, cholera, anthrax) can afford to incapacitate. Myxoma virus released into Australian rabbits in 1950 evolved from >99% lethality toward intermediate levels within years because strains that killed too fast were transmitted less. Virulence is an equilibrium that human behaviour shifts.

8. **Stewardship, infection control and vaccination work by changing the selective landscape.** Less antibiotic exposure lowers selection; narrow agents limit bystander selection on gut flora; adequate dosing avoids the sub-inhibitory window; isolation stops spread; vaccination removes the hosts a strain needs. Resistance has appeared within 1–20 years of every antibiotic class since penicillin, and deaths attributable to bacterial resistance were estimated at ~1.3 million worldwide in 2019 ([stewardship](../../12-organ-system-specialties/08-infectious-diseases/antimicrobial-stewardship.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Coevolution | Reciprocal adaptation of two interacting species over time |
| Mutation supply | Population size × mutation rate: how many variants arise per generation |
| Horizontal gene transfer | Movement of genes between bacteria by plasmids, transformation or phage |
| Fitness cost of resistance | Reduced growth of resistant cells without the drug; often compensated |
| Antigenic drift / shift | Gradual point mutation / abrupt reassortment of pathogen surface antigens |
| MHC polymorphism | Extreme allelic diversity of antigen-presenting genes maintained by pathogen pressure |
| Virulence–transmission trade-off | Selection on how much harm a pathogen does, set by its route of spread |
| Antimicrobial stewardship | Managing antibiotic use to minimise selection for resistance |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Patients become resistant to antibiotics."** Bacteria do; the resistant population may be selected from the patient's own flora or acquired from others, and can be transmitted onward.
- **"Stopping antibiotics early always breeds resistance."** Unnecessarily long courses also select resistance in bystander flora; the principle is adequate dose for the time needed.
