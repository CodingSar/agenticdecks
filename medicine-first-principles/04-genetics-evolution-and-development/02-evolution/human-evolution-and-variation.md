## Essence

Anatomically modern humans arose in Africa within the last ~300,000 years, left it in small numbers ~60,000–70,000 years ago, and spread across the planet in a few tens of thousands of years, picking up a few percent of Neanderthal and Denisovan ancestry on the way. That history is written in every genome and it shapes medicine in three ways. First, humans are genetically homogeneous: any two people differ at ~0.1% of positions, most variation is found within every population, and the differences between continental groups are gradients rather than boundaries. Second, the variation that does differ in frequency between populations was mostly shaped by infection, diet, climate and chance, and much of it is medically important: malaria resistance alleles, lactase persistence, drug-metabolising enzyme variants, skin pigmentation. Third, ancestry is therefore a useful but crude proxy for genotype, and "race" is a social category that correlates imperfectly with it. The one idea is **recent African origin, serial founder effects and local adaptation**, from which the pattern of human variation and its clinical uses and misuses both follow.

## Core tenets

1. **Human genetic diversity is low and is greatest in Africa.** The effective population size through most of human history was ~10,000, and non-African populations descend from a subset of Africans, so each step out of Africa lost diversity ([drift and founder effects](natural-selection-and-population-genetics.md)). African populations carry more variants, shorter linkage blocks and deeper lineages than all others combined. A consequence for medicine: genome-wide association studies and reference databases built on European-ancestry samples miss African variation, so variants of uncertain significance and false pathogenic calls are more frequent in people of African ancestry, and polygenic scores transfer poorly ([complex traits](../01-inheritance/complex-traits-and-heritability.md)).

2. **Most variation is within populations; between-population differences are clinal.** About 85–90% of human genetic variance is found between individuals within any one population, and only ~10–15% between continental groups (Lewontin, 1972, confirmed by genome-wide data). Allele frequencies change gradually with geography, following migration and selection gradients, so a frequency map looks like contour lines, not blocks. Clusters can be recovered statistically when sampling is sparse, but they are a property of the sampling. This is why ancestry predicts a single genotype only weakly, and why a test for the variant itself always beats inferring it from appearance or self-report.

3. **Infection, especially malaria, has been the strongest selective force on recent human genomes.** Haemoglobin S, haemoglobin C, haemoglobin E, the alpha- and beta-thalassaemias, glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency and the Duffy-null blood group each reach high frequency precisely where *Plasmodium* has been endemic, each by heterozygote advantage, and each at the cost of a recessive or X-linked disease ([anaemias](../../12-organ-system-specialties/06-haematology/anaemias.md); [red cells and haemoglobin](../../06-physiology/08-blood/red-cells-haemoglobin-and-iron.md)). The sickle allele arose independently several times. These are the clearest examples of selection in humans, and they make a person's ancestry clinically informative for haemoglobin disorders and for the risk of haemolysis with oxidant drugs such as primaquine.

4. **Diet and climate left further signatures.** Lactase persistence into adulthood arose independently in European and several African pastoralist populations in the last ~10,000 years and is the strongest selective sweep known in humans; lactose intolerance is the ancestral, majority human state, not a disease. Amylase gene copy number is higher in populations with starch-rich diets. Skin pigmentation tracks ultraviolet exposure as a trade-off between folate protection and vitamin D synthesis, which is why darker-skinned people at high latitudes are prone to vitamin D deficiency and rickets. Tibetan (EPAS1) and Andean populations adapted to altitude by different routes. Cold adaptation shaped body proportions. Each adaptation is a local solution, and each becomes a vulnerability when the environment changes ([evolutionary medicine](evolutionary-medicine.md)).

5. **Archaic admixture contributed functional variants.** Non-Africans carry ~1–2% Neanderthal ancestry and Oceanians up to ~5% Denisovan. Some introgressed segments were favoured (immune genes, the Tibetan EPAS1 allele is Denisovan in origin, skin and hair genes), while Neanderthal ancestry is depleted on the X chromosome and in genes expressed in testis, suggesting partial incompatibility. A Neanderthal haplotype on chromosome 3 roughly doubled the risk of severe COVID-19 (reported 2020). Admixture is a normal part of human history, ancient and recent, and most people's genomes are mosaics of several ancestral populations.

6. **Pharmacogenetic variation differs in frequency between populations and matters for prescribing.** CYP2D6 poor metabolisers are ~5–10% of Europeans and under 2% of East Asians, while ultra-rapid metabolisers are common in North Africa and the Middle East; CYP2C19 loss-of-function alleles that reduce clopidogrel activation reach ~30–50% in East Asia; the HLA-B*15:02 allele that predicts carbamazepine-induced Stevens–Johnson syndrome is found mainly in South and South-East Asian populations and is screened for there; NAT2 slow acetylation affects isoniazid toxicity; the warfarin dose required varies with VKORC1 and CYP2C9 alleles whose frequencies differ by ancestry ([pharmacogenomics](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/02-pharmacodynamics/pharmacogenomics-and-individual-variation.md)). The lesson is the same each time: ancestry raises or lowers the prior probability of a genotype, but the genotype test is what should guide the dose.

7. **Reference ranges and risk equations that include race need to be examined for what the variable actually captures.** Serum creatinine-based estimates of kidney function used a race coefficient until 2021 that systematically overestimated function in Black patients and delayed referral; it was removed in the United States when it was shown to encode an unsupported biological claim. Spirometry reference values and some pain and cardiology algorithms have had similar corrections. Where a difference is real and genetic (haemoglobin disorders, G6PD, pharmacogenes), test for the variant; where it reflects environment, access or discrimination, race is a proxy for a social exposure and should be treated as one ([reference ranges](../../11-clinical-method-and-diagnostics/02-laboratory-medicine/clinical-chemistry-and-reference-ranges.md)). Most disparities in disease outcome between racial groups are explained by social and economic factors, not by allele frequencies.

8. **The human body shows its evolutionary history in its anatomy and its vulnerabilities.** Bipedalism remodelled the pelvis, spine, knee and foot and left the lower back, the knee ligaments and childbirth as weak points; a large brain and a narrow pelvis produced a helpless infant and a long childhood; a shortened gut and a dependence on cooked, processed food produced the small jaw and crowded teeth; loss of body hair and abundant sweat glands made us endurance runners prone to heat illness and dehydration. These are the historical constraints of [evolutionary medicine](evolutionary-medicine.md) made specific to our species, and they frame the anatomy of [layer 05](../../05-tissues-and-anatomy/README.md).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Recent African origin | Emergence of modern humans in Africa and dispersal within the last ~70,000 years |
| Serial founder effect | Progressive loss of diversity as small groups colonise new territory |
| Cline | Gradual change in allele frequency across geography |
| Apportionment of diversity | The finding that most human genetic variance lies within, not between, populations |
| Local adaptation | Selection fitting a population to its diet, pathogens, climate or altitude |
| Heterozygote advantage | Fitness of carriers exceeding both homozygotes; maintains malaria-resistance alleles |
| Archaic admixture | Neanderthal and Denisovan ancestry in modern genomes |
| Ancestry versus race | Genetic descent, continuous and measurable / a social classification correlated with it |
| Pharmacogene | Gene whose variants alter drug handling or response, often with population-specific frequencies |
| Race-based correction | A coefficient in a clinical equation keyed to race; often unsupported and now being removed |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Races are genetically distinct groups."** Human variation is clinal and mostly within populations; the clusters recovered statistically depend on sampling and do not correspond to the social categories used in records.
- **"Ancestry tells you a patient's genotype."** It shifts the probability. For any variant that changes treatment, testing the variant is cheaper than being wrong.
- **"Lactose intolerance is a disorder."** It is the ancestral human condition; persistence of lactase is the recent adaptation, present in a minority of humans worldwide.
