## Essence

About 3% of live-born infants have a major structural abnormality, and congenital anomalies are a leading cause of infant death in high-income countries now that infection and prematurity are better controlled. A malformation is an error of formation; a deformation is a normal structure bent by force; a disruption is a normally formed structure destroyed. The causes are chromosomal (~10%), single-gene (~10–15%), environmental and teratogenic (~5–10%), and in roughly half of cases unknown or multifactorial. A teratogen is any agent (drug, infection, chemical, maternal condition) that disturbs development, and its effect depends on what it does, how much reaches the embryo, and above all when. The single idea is **the embryo's response to an insult is determined by its developmental stage more than by the nature of the insult**, which is why timing dominates every tenet below and why pharmacovigilance in pregnancy is a distinct discipline.

## Core tenets

1. **Timing determines outcome: the all-or-none period, the embryonic period, and the fetal period.** In the first two weeks after fertilisation an insult usually kills the conceptus or is repaired by totipotent cells, so it rarely produces a malformed survivor. From week 3 to week 8, when organs are forming ([organogenesis](germ-layers-and-organogenesis.md)), the embryo is most vulnerable to structural malformation, and the organ affected is the one being built at the time of exposure. From week 9 to birth, exposure produces growth restriction, functional deficits (especially of the brain, which develops throughout) and minor anomalies, but not gross malformation of organs already formed. Thalidomide produced limb defects only when taken between about days 20 and 36 after conception, and the exact day predicted whether arms or legs, and which bones, were missing.

2. **The classification by mechanism tells you about recurrence and cause.** A malformation is intrinsically abnormal development (a septal defect, cleft palate, spina bifida) and may be genetic or teratogenic. A deformation is mechanical (club foot or hip dislocation from oligohydramnios or a breech position) and often corrects after birth. A disruption is destruction of normal tissue (amniotic band amputation, vascular accidents) and carries essentially no recurrence risk. A dysplasia is abnormal organisation of a tissue type (skeletal dysplasias, ectodermal dysplasias) and is usually single-gene. A sequence is one primary anomaly producing a cascade (Potter sequence: no kidneys, hence no urine, hence oligohydramnios, hence compressed lungs and face); a syndrome is a recurring pattern with a single underlying cause.

3. **Malformations cluster because patterning pathways are shared, and the pattern names the cause.** The same signalling pathway builds several organs ([morphogens and developmental genes](morphogens-patterning-and-developmental-genes.md)), so a pathway lesion affects them together: heart, thymus, parathyroid and face in 22q11.2 deletion because all derive from the pharyngeal arches and neural crest; the VACTERL association (vertebral, anal, cardiac, tracheo-oesophageal, renal, limb) because these form at the same time from the same mesoderm; midline brain, face and heart in disorders of the hedgehog pathway. A dysmorphologist reads the combination of anomalies as a fingerprint of the stage and the pathway, and increasingly confirms it by sequencing ([cytogenetics](../01-inheritance/chromosomes-and-cytogenetics.md)).

4. **Teratogens act through a small number of mechanisms.** Folate antagonism and interference with neural tube closure (valproate, methotrexate, maternal folate deficiency); retinoid signalling and Hox boundaries (isotretinoin); disruption of angiogenesis (thalidomide, now understood to degrade a transcription factor needed for limb bud vessels); blockade of the renin–angiotensin system in the fetal kidney (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors); interference with vitamin K and bone (warfarin, nasal hypoplasia and stippled epiphyses); direct neurotoxicity and apoptosis in the developing brain (alcohol); and infection of fetal tissue (rubella, cytomegalovirus, Zika, toxoplasma, syphilis). Knowing the mechanism predicts both the window and the organ.

5. **Maternal disease and nutrition are teratogens too.** Poorly controlled maternal diabetes doubles or triples the malformation rate (heart, neural tube, caudal regression), probably through hyperglycaemia-induced oxidative stress during organogenesis; control before conception removes most of the excess. Maternal phenylketonuria damages the fetal brain even though the fetus is heterozygous. Hyperthermia in early pregnancy is associated with neural tube defects. Iodine deficiency is the world's commonest preventable cause of intellectual disability. Folate supplementation before conception prevents most neural tube defects, which makes it one of the few primary preventions for a malformation and the reason for flour fortification. Alcohol is the commonest preventable teratogen in high-income countries: fetal alcohol spectrum disorder affects on the order of 1% of births in some populations, mostly as cognitive and behavioural impairment without the classic facial features.

6. **Proving teratogenicity is hard, and the methods are those of causal inference.** Randomised trials are impossible; animal tests have poor species concordance (thalidomide was safe in rats); the background rate of 3% means that any drug taken by many pregnant women will be followed by malformations by chance; recall bias inflates reported exposures in mothers of affected infants; and the disease being treated (epilepsy, diabetes) is itself a risk ([causal inference](../../01-quantitative-and-scientific-foundations/02-inference-and-causation/causal-inference.md)). Evidence comes from case series with a distinctive pattern (thalidomide was recognised within a few years because phocomelia is otherwise vanishingly rare), from pregnancy registries, and from cohort and case–control studies with careful timing data. The Bradford Hill-style criteria for a teratogen include a specific pattern, exposure in the critical period, biological plausibility and a dose–response ([pharmacovigilance](../../09-pharmacology-and-therapeutics/02-pharmacodynamics/adverse-drug-reactions-and-pharmacovigilance.md)). Most drugs have never been adequately studied in pregnancy, and absence of evidence is routinely misread as evidence of safety or of harm.

7. **Dose and genotype modify susceptibility.** Teratogens show thresholds: below a certain exposure no effect is detectable, above it the risk rises with dose and the phenotype worsens. Valproate's neural tube defect risk is dose-dependent; the fetal alcohol spectrum ranges from no effect to full syndrome with intake. Genetic variation in the mother (folate metabolism enzymes), the fetus (drug-metabolising enzymes, pathway genes already carrying a mild variant) and the placenta (transporters) explains why one exposed fetus is affected and another is not. Most teratogens raise the absolute risk of a specific malformation from well under 1% to a few percent, so even after a known exposure most infants are unaffected, which is the key message in counselling.

8. **Detection, counselling and prevention are the clinical applications.** Preconception care (folate, glucose control, medication review, vaccination against rubella) prevents a share of malformations. Antenatal screening by first-trimester ultrasound and maternal serum markers and by cell-free fetal Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) identifies chromosomal risk; the detailed anomaly scan at 18–22 weeks finds most major structural defects and allows planning of delivery in a centre that can repair them ([pregnancy and its complications](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/02-obstetrics-and-gynaecology/pregnancy-and-its-complications.md)). After birth the newborn examination, pulse oximetry for critical heart disease and the biochemical screen catch the rest ([the newborn](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/the-newborn-and-neonatology.md)). Recurrence risk depends on the category: near zero for disruptions, ~3–5% for most isolated multifactorial malformations after one affected child, Mendelian ratios for single-gene syndromes.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Malformation / deformation / disruption / dysplasia | Error of formation / mechanical distortion / destruction / abnormal tissue organisation |
| Sequence / syndrome / association | A cascade from one primary defect / a pattern with one cause / anomalies occurring together more often than chance |
| Teratogen | Any agent that disturbs embryonic or fetal development |
| Critical period | The window in which an organ's formation can be disrupted |
| All-or-none period | The first two weeks, when insults usually kill or are repaired rather than malform |
| Dysmorphology | Reading the pattern of anomalies to infer stage and cause |
| Threshold and dose–response | No detectable effect below a dose; rising risk and severity above it |
| Pregnancy registry | Prospective record of exposed pregnancies and outcomes; the main source of evidence |
| Anomaly scan | Mid-trimester ultrasound examination for structural defects |
| Recurrence risk | Probability of the anomaly in a subsequent pregnancy; depends on category |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A drug taken in pregnancy that was followed by a malformation caused it."** The background rate is 3%; causation needs a specific pattern, exposure in the window, and controlled comparison.
- **"Teratogens are dangerous throughout pregnancy."** Most structural risk is confined to weeks 3–8; later exposure has different and usually lesser effects, though the brain remains vulnerable.
- **"Most malformations are genetic."** Roughly half have no identified cause; chromosomal and single-gene causes together account for about a quarter, and most of the rest are multifactorial with modest recurrence risk.
