## Essence

How does a cell in an embryo know where it is, and therefore what to become? The answer, worked out between 1980 and 2000 and rewarded with the 1995 Nobel Prize, is that a handful of secreted signalling molecules form concentration gradients across fields of cells, and cells read their position from the local concentration and switch on different transcription factors at different thresholds. The signalling molecules are morphogens; the transcription factors they control, above all the Hox genes, give each region its identity; and the same few pathways (Wnt, Hedgehog, Notch, Bone Morphogenetic Protein (BMP), Fibroblast Growth Factor (FGF), retinoic acid) are reused at every stage and in every organ. The one idea is **position encoded as concentration, decoded by thresholds, locked in by gene regulation**. Because the toolkit is small and reused, a mutation or a drug that disturbs one pathway produces malformations in several organs at once, the same pathways drive wound repair, and their reactivation in adults is a recurring cause of cancer.

## Core tenets

1. **A morphogen gradient converts a source into a coordinate system.** A morphogen is secreted from a localised source, spreads by diffusion through the tissue and is degraded or bound as it goes, so its concentration falls with distance ([diffusion](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/diffusion-osmosis-and-transport.md)). Cells express different genes above and below set concentration thresholds (Wolpert's French flag model, 1969), so one gradient can specify three or more cell types in order. In the neural tube, Sonic hedgehog from the notochord and floor plate rises ventrally and BMPs from the roof plate rise dorsally; motor neurons form where hedgehog is high, sensory relay neurons where BMP is high, and the interneuron types between at intermediate ratios. Gradients are made robust by feedback (a target gene that degrades the morphogen) and are read over time as well as concentration.

2. **The three body axes are set by three gradient systems.** Head–tail: the primitive streak and node secrete Wnt, FGF and Nodal at the tail end while the anterior visceral endoderm secretes their inhibitors at the head end, so a posteriorising gradient runs along the embryo ([early embryogenesis](fertilization-and-early-embryogenesis.md)). Dorsal–ventral: BMP from the ventral side opposed by the organiser's inhibitors (chordin, noggin) from the dorsal side. Left–right: cilia at the node beat in one direction, sweep signalling molecules to the left, and so confine Nodal expression to the left side; the heart loops and the gut rotates accordingly. Defective ciliary motility therefore randomises asymmetry, which is why half of people with primary ciliary dyskinesia have situs inversus ([cytoskeleton](../../03-cell-biology/01-cell-structure/the-cytoskeleton-and-cell-motility.md)).

3. **Hox genes give segments their identity, and their order on the chromosome matches their order in the body.** The 39 human Hox genes lie in four clusters, and within each cluster the genes are expressed in order from head to tail (colinearity), with each more posterior gene expressed from a more posterior boundary. A cell's combination of Hox genes is a positional code: a somite reading one code becomes a cervical vertebra, another a thoracic one with ribs, another lumbar. Retinoic acid, a vitamin A derivative, sets the anterior boundaries of Hox expression, which is why excess retinoids (isotretinoin for acne) cause malformations of the head, heart and thymus, and deficiency causes others ([teratogens](congenital-malformations-and-teratogens.md)). Hox mutations in humans shift identities: synpolydactyly (HOXD13) and hand–foot–genital syndrome (HOXA13).

4. **The same few pathways are reused, which is why single mutations cause syndromes.** About a dozen signalling pathways do nearly all the patterning of all organs. Sonic hedgehog patterns the neural tube, the limb (the number and identity of digits), the face and the gut; its loss in the midline gives holoprosencephaly (one cerebral hemisphere, a single central incisor, cyclopia at the extreme), and the midline defects of fetal alcohol syndrome arise partly through this pathway. FGF receptor gain-of-function mutations cause achondroplasia (FGFR3, in cartilage growth) and the craniosynostosis syndromes (FGFR1–3, in skull sutures) because the same receptor family controls growth plates and sutures. Notch patterns somites, blood vessels and the heart's valves: Alagille syndrome (JAG1) affects liver bile ducts, heart, eye and vertebrae together. A syndrome is a pathway's footprint.

5. **Patterning uses a small set of cell behaviours triggered by position.** Once a cell knows where it is it does one of a few things: proliferate, migrate, change shape, change adhesion so it sorts away from neighbours, or die. Programmed cell death sculpts form: the separation of fingers by apoptosis of the interdigital webs, the hollowing of ducts, the removal of the tail and of half the neurons made in the brain ([apoptosis](../../03-cell-biology/02-the-cell-life-cycle/cell-death-apoptosis-necrosis-and-autophagy.md)). Syndactyly is interdigital death that did not happen. Lateral inhibition through Notch spaces out cell types in a field ([cell communication](../../03-cell-biology/03-cells-in-community/cell-to-cell-communication-and-junctions.md)). Morphogenesis is these behaviours applied in sequence to sheets of cells.

6. **Induction and competence make development a sequence of conversations.** A tissue that releases a signal (the inducer) changes the fate of a neighbour only if the neighbour is competent, that is, expressing the receptor and the transcription factors that let it respond at that moment. The optic vesicle induces the overlying ectoderm to form a lens, but only head ectoderm at the right stage can respond. Competence windows explain why the same morphogen does different things in different places and times, and why an insult during a window affects only the structures then being induced ([critical periods](germ-layers-and-organogenesis.md)). Reciprocal induction between epithelium and mesenchyme builds every branching organ.

7. **The developmental toolkit is kept and reused in the adult, in repair and in cancer.** Wnt from the crypt base maintains intestinal stem cells; hedgehog maintains hair follicles; Notch governs blood cell fate; BMPs are the bone-inducing factors used in orthopaedic surgery. Wound healing reactivates the embryonic programmes for migration, proliferation and matrix deposition ([tissue repair](../../08-pathology/01-cell-and-tissue-injury/tissue-repair-regeneration-and-fibrosis.md)). Mutations that switch these pathways on permanently are oncogenic: the APC mutation that activates Wnt signalling is the initiating event in most colorectal cancers, PTCH1 loss activates hedgehog in basal cell carcinoma (the commonest human cancer; [skin cancer](../../12-organ-system-specialties/11-dermatology/skin-cancer-and-skin-infections.md)) and medulloblastoma, and Notch mutations drive T-cell leukaemia. Hedgehog inhibitors (vismodegib) treat advanced basal cell carcinoma and are teratogenic for exactly the reason they work. Cancer reuses development; [the hallmarks of cancer](../../08-pathology/03-neoplasia/hallmarks-of-cancer.md) are embryonic behaviours in the wrong place and time.

8. **Conservation of the toolkit makes evolution a matter of regulation and makes model organisms informative.** The homeobox genes were found in flies and then in every animal with the same order and function; fly *eyeless* and human PAX6 are interchangeable, and PAX6 mutations cause aniridia. Differences between species arise mainly from changes in when and where the shared genes are expressed (regulatory evolution) rather than from new genes. This is why a mouse with a knocked-out gene predicts the human phenotype, why zebrafish screens find human disease genes, and why the body plan is as conserved as [organogenesis](germ-layers-and-organogenesis.md) shows.

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Morphogen | Secreted molecule whose concentration gradient specifies cell fates by position |
| French flag model | Fates assigned by concentration thresholds along a gradient |
| Organiser | A signalling centre (node, notochord, zone of polarising activity) that patterns a field |
| Hox genes | Clustered homeobox transcription factors giving head-to-tail segmental identity |
| Colinearity | Correspondence between a Hox gene's position in the cluster and its expression boundary in the body |
| Induction / competence | A signal changing a neighbour's fate / the neighbour's ability to respond at that time |
| Lateral inhibition | Notch-mediated suppression of the same fate in adjacent cells |
| Positional information | A cell's knowledge of its location, encoded in morphogen levels and transcription factor combinations |
| Regulatory evolution | Change in species form by altering expression of conserved genes |
| Developmental pathway reactivation | Use of embryonic signalling in adult stem cells, repair and cancer |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Each organ has its own genes."** A dozen pathways and a few hundred transcription factors build everything; organ identity comes from combinations, timing and place, which is why one mutation affects several organs.
- **"Developmental genes switch off at birth."** They maintain adult stem cells, run wound repair and are the commonest oncogenic targets.
- **"Humans have more genes than simpler animals because they are more complex."** Human and nematode gene counts are similar (~20,000); complexity comes from regulation, splicing and reuse.
