## Essence

Development does not end at birth. The body grows for two decades along a curve with a fast infant phase, a slow childhood, a pubertal spurt and a stop; puberty switches on the reproductive axis and remodels body and brain; and from the third decade onward every system loses function at roughly 1% a year until reserve is exhausted. These are one life history, and the single idea that unites them is **allocation**: an organism has finite energy to divide among growth, maintenance and reproduction, and the schedule by which it shifts from one to the next was set by selection on ancestors, not by any plan for a long and healthy old age. Growth charts, delayed and precocious puberty, the developmental origins of adult disease, the hallmarks of ageing and frailty are all readings of that schedule.

## Core tenets

1. **Growth is a programmed curve with three phases driven by different hormones.** Infancy continues the fetal trajectory and depends on nutrition and insulin-like growth factors; childhood growth of ~5–7 cm a year is driven by growth hormone acting through insulin-like growth factor 1 on the growth plates of long bones; the pubertal spurt (~8–12 cm a year at peak) adds sex steroids, which first accelerate growth and then fuse the growth plates and end it. Height at any age is best predicted by parental height ([heritability](../01-inheritance/complex-traits-and-heritability.md) ~0.8), and deviation from a child's own centile line is the signal of disease: hormone deficiency, chronic illness, malnutrition, neglect. Weight and head circumference have their own curves; crossing centiles is the paediatrician's main screening tool ([child growth](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/growth-development-and-the-child-as-patient.md)).

2. **Growth is allometric: organs grow at different rates, so proportions change.** The head is a quarter of body length at birth and an eighth in adulthood; the brain reaches ~90% of adult volume by age 5 while the reproductive organs barely grow until puberty; lymphoid tissue peaks in childhood and regresses ([scale and allometry](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/scale-and-allometry.md)). Metabolic rate per kilogram falls as size rises, so infants need ~100 kcal/kg/day and adults ~30, infants lose heat and water fast, and drug doses scale with surface area rather than weight. The long human childhood, unique among primates, is a period of brain growth and learning funded by delaying reproduction, a life-history trade-off ([evolutionary medicine](../02-evolution/evolutionary-medicine.md)).

3. **Puberty is the reawakening of a hormonal pulse generator that was active in the fetus and then silenced.** The hypothalamus releases gonadotropin-releasing hormone in pulses in late fetal life and early infancy, then falls quiet for a decade under central inhibition. Its reactivation around age 8–13, permitted by the kisspeptin neurons and signalled by body energy stores through leptin, drives pituitary gonadotropins, gonadal sex steroids and the sequence of breast development, pubic hair, growth spurt and menarche in girls, or testicular enlargement, pubic hair, growth spurt and voice change in boys ([reproductive endocrinology](../../06-physiology/07-endocrine-system/reproductive-endocrinology.md)). Timing is heritable and nutritional: the age of menarche fell from ~17 to ~13 in Europe between 1850 and 1950 as nutrition improved, and obesity now advances it further. Precocious puberty (before 8 or 9) and delayed puberty (no signs by 13 or 14) are investigated as disorders of this axis or as markers of chronic disease.

4. **Puberty remodels the brain, not only the body.** Grey matter is pruned and white matter myelinated through adolescence into the mid-twenties, with the prefrontal cortex last; the reward and emotional systems mature earlier. This asynchrony is the biological ground of adolescent risk-taking, and adolescence is the period of onset of most psychiatric disorders (anxiety, depression, psychosis, eating disorders, substance use). Sex steroids organise brain circuits as well as activating them, and the pubertal rise in sex differences in mood disorders dates from this period.

5. **Early life sets the trajectory for later disease: the developmental origins hypothesis.** Low birth weight, and rapid catch-up growth after it, predict higher rates of type 2 diabetes, hypertension and coronary disease decades later (Barker, 1980s onward), and the pattern has been reproduced in cohorts worldwide and in the children of famine. The mechanisms are a reduced nephron number (set before birth; fewer nephrons means higher pressure per nephron), altered pancreatic beta cell mass, and epigenetic settings of metabolic genes ([epigenetic inheritance](../01-inheritance/epigenetic-inheritance-and-imprinting.md)) laid down as a forecast of a scarce world. Childhood adversity similarly predicts adult disease through stress physiology and behaviour. Growth is not just a stage; it is where much adult risk is set.

6. **Ageing is the progressive loss of reserve in every system at roughly 1% a year from the fourth decade.** Maximal cardiac output, glomerular filtration rate, vital capacity, muscle mass, bone density, nerve conduction velocity and maximal oxygen uptake all decline approximately linearly in cross-sectional studies, with wide individual variation. Resting function is maintained for decades because the body runs well below its maximum; what is lost is the margin for stress, and that is why old age is revealed by illness, surgery or a fall rather than by rest. Mortality rate doubles about every 8 years after age 30 (Gompertz, 1825), a regularity consistent with damage accumulating and reserve running out ([compensation and reserve](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#compensation-and-decompensation)).

7. **The hallmarks of ageing are the cellular processes of layer 03 running down together.** Genomic instability and accumulated somatic mutation; telomere attrition; epigenetic drift; loss of proteostasis; deregulated nutrient sensing; mitochondrial dysfunction; cellular [senescence](../../03-cell-biology/02-the-cell-life-cycle/senescence-and-telomeres.md); [stem cell exhaustion](../../03-cell-biology/02-the-cell-life-cycle/stem-cells-differentiation-and-regeneration.md); altered intercellular communication and chronic low-grade inflammation (López-Otín, 2013). No single one is the cause; they interact, and they share a root: selection never built repair good enough to last beyond the ages at which our ancestors reproduced and died ([antagonistic pleiotropy](../02-evolution/evolutionary-medicine.md)). Interventions that extend lifespan in animals (dietary restriction, reduced growth signalling, rapamycin, senolytics) act on these shared processes rather than on any one disease.

8. **Ageing is plastic within limits, and the clinical target is healthspan.** Lifespan is modestly heritable (~0.2–0.3); lifestyle (smoking, exercise, diet, blood pressure) explains more of the variation in age at death than genes do, and the extreme of longevity has genetic contributors (FOXO3, APOE variants). Physical activity preserves muscle, bone, cardiovascular and cognitive reserve more than any drug. The practical goal is to compress the period of disability: in the 2020s, life expectancy at birth is ~80–84 years in high-income countries, but healthy life expectancy is roughly a decade less, and that gap, filled with multimorbidity and [frailty](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/frailty-multimorbidity-and-polypharmacy.md), is where geriatric medicine works ([diseases of aging](../../08-pathology/04-genetic-metabolic-and-environmental-disease/diseases-of-aging-and-degeneration.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Growth velocity / centile | Rate of growth / position relative to a reference population at the same age |
| Growth plate (epiphyseal plate) | Cartilage zone where long bones lengthen; fused by sex steroids at the end of puberty |
| Growth hormone–IGF-1 axis | The main driver of childhood growth |
| Allometry | Different growth rates of different parts; proportions change with size |
| Life-history trade-off | Allocation of finite energy among growth, maintenance and reproduction |
| Gonadotropin-releasing hormone pulse generator | Hypothalamic pacemaker whose reactivation starts puberty |
| Secular trend | Shift in the population's timing of growth or puberty across generations |
| Developmental origins of adult disease | Early-life conditions setting later risk of metabolic and cardiovascular disease |
| Physiological reserve | The margin between resting and maximal function, lost with age |
| Hallmarks of ageing | The interacting cellular processes that produce age-related decline |
| Healthspan | Years lived without significant disability |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Ageing is a disease."** It is the loss of reserve that makes disease likely; the diseases of old age are distinct conditions sharing ageing as a risk factor, and treating them one at a time leaves the shared process untouched.
- **"Ageing is programmed, like development."** No gene exists to cause ageing; it is the absence of selection for repair beyond reproductive age. Development is a programme; ageing is its warranty expiring.
- **"A child's growth is normal if they are on the chart."** The signal is change in centile over time, not position; a child falling from the 75th to the 25th centile is the one to investigate.
