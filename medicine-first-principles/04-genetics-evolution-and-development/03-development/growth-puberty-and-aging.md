## Essence

Development does not end at birth. The body grows for two decades along a curve with a fast infant phase, a slow childhood, a pubertal spurt and a stop; puberty switches on the reproductive axis; and from the third decade every system loses function at roughly 1% a year until reserve is exhausted. The idea that unites them is **allocation**: finite energy divided among growth, maintenance and reproduction on a schedule set by selection on ancestors, not by any plan for a long and healthy old age.

## Core tenets

1. **Growth is a programmed curve with three phases driven by different hormones.** Infancy continues the fetal trajectory and depends on nutrition; childhood growth of ~5–7 cm a year is driven by growth hormone through insulin-like growth factor 1 on the growth plates; the pubertal spurt (~8–12 cm a year at peak) adds sex steroids, which first accelerate growth and then fuse the plates and end it. Height is best predicted by parental height ([heritability](../01-inheritance/complex-traits-and-heritability.md) ~0.8), and deviation from a child's own centile line is the signal of disease ([child growth](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/01-paediatrics/growth-development-and-the-child-as-patient.md)).

2. **Growth is allometric: organs grow at different rates, so proportions change.** The head is a quarter of body length at birth and an eighth in adulthood; the brain reaches ~90% of adult volume by age 5 ([scale and allometry](../../00-physical-and-chemical-foundations/01-physics-of-the-body/scale-and-allometry.md)). Metabolic rate per kilogram falls as size rises, so infants need ~100 kcal/kg/day and adults ~30, and drug doses scale with surface area. The long human childhood, unique among primates, funds brain growth and learning by delaying reproduction, a life-history trade-off ([evolutionary medicine](../02-evolution/evolutionary-medicine.md)).

3. **Puberty is the reawakening of a hormonal pulse generator that was active in the fetus and then silenced.** The hypothalamus releases gonadotropin-releasing hormone in pulses in infancy, then falls quiet for a decade. Its reactivation around age 8–13, permitted by kisspeptin neurons and energy stores signalled through leptin, drives gonadotropins, sex steroids and the sequence of breast development, growth spurt and menarche in girls, or testicular enlargement, growth spurt and voice change in boys ([reproductive endocrinology](../../06-physiology/07-endocrine-system/reproductive-endocrinology.md)). Timing is heritable and nutritional: the age of menarche fell from ~17 to ~13 in Europe between 1850 and 1950 as nutrition improved.

4. **Puberty remodels the brain, not only the body.** Grey matter is pruned and white matter myelinated into the mid-twenties, prefrontal cortex last; reward and emotional systems mature earlier. This asynchrony underlies adolescent risk-taking, and adolescence is the period of onset of most psychiatric disorders.

5. **Early life sets the trajectory for later disease.** Low birth weight, and rapid catch-up growth after it, predict higher rates of type 2 diabetes, hypertension and coronary disease decades later (Barker, 1980s onward). The mechanisms are a reduced nephron number set before birth, altered beta cell mass, and epigenetic settings of metabolic genes ([epigenetic inheritance](../01-inheritance/epigenetic-inheritance-and-imprinting.md)) laid down as a forecast of a scarce world.

6. **Ageing is the progressive loss of reserve in every system at roughly 1% a year from the fourth decade.** Maximal cardiac output, glomerular filtration rate, vital capacity, muscle mass and bone density all decline approximately linearly, with wide individual variation. Resting function is maintained because the body runs well below its maximum; what is lost is the margin for stress, so old age is revealed by illness, surgery or a fall. Mortality doubles about every 8 years after age 30 (Gompertz, 1825), consistent with damage accumulating and reserve running out ([compensation and reserve](../../CROSS-CUTTING.md#compensation-and-decompensation)).

7. **The hallmarks of ageing are the cellular processes of layer 03 running down together.** Genomic instability; telomere attrition; epigenetic drift; loss of proteostasis; deregulated nutrient sensing; mitochondrial dysfunction; cellular [senescence](../../03-cell-biology/02-the-cell-life-cycle/senescence-and-telomeres.md); [stem cell exhaustion](../../03-cell-biology/02-the-cell-life-cycle/stem-cells-differentiation-and-regeneration.md); chronic low-grade inflammation (López-Otín, 2013). No single one is the cause; they interact and share a root: selection never built repair good enough to last beyond the ages at which our ancestors reproduced ([antagonistic pleiotropy](../02-evolution/evolutionary-medicine.md)). Interventions that extend lifespan in animals (dietary restriction, rapamycin, senolytics) act on these shared processes.

8. **Ageing is plastic within limits, and the clinical target is healthspan.** Lifespan is modestly heritable (~0.2–0.3); lifestyle explains more of the variation in age at death than genes do. In the 2020s life expectancy at birth is ~80–84 years in high-income countries but healthy life expectancy is roughly a decade less, and that gap, filled with multimorbidity and [frailty](../../14-life-course-mind-and-integrative-care/04-geriatrics-and-palliative-care/frailty-multimorbidity-and-polypharmacy.md), is where geriatric medicine works ([diseases of aging](../../08-pathology/04-genetic-metabolic-and-environmental-disease/diseases-of-aging-and-degeneration.md)).

## Key concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Growth velocity / centile | Rate of growth / position relative to a reference population at the same age |
| Allometry | Different growth rates of different parts; proportions change with size |
| Life-history trade-off | Allocation of finite energy among growth, maintenance and reproduction |
| Gonadotropin-releasing hormone pulse generator | Hypothalamic pacemaker whose reactivation starts puberty |
| Developmental origins of adult disease | Early-life conditions setting later risk of metabolic and cardiovascular disease |
| Physiological reserve | The margin between resting and maximal function, lost with age |
| Healthspan | Years lived without significant disability |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Ageing is a disease."** It is the loss of reserve that makes disease likely; treating the diseases of old age one at a time leaves the shared process untouched.
- **"Ageing is programmed, like development."** No gene exists to cause ageing; it is the absence of selection for repair beyond reproductive age.
